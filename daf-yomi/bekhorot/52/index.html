📖 Overview: The Limits of the Double Portion

Bekhorot 52a–52b
הַבְּכוֹר נוֹטֵל פִּי שְׁנַיִם בְּנִכְסֵי הָאָב, וְאֵינוֹ נוֹטֵל פִּי שְׁנַיִם בְּנִכְסֵי הָאֵם
The scene: This daf completes the mishna on the firstborn's double portion: he takes it only from the father's property, never the mother's, never from value that appreciated after death, and never from property still due to the father but not yet in his possession. The Gemara grounds each limit in the verse 'all that he has' (Deut. 21:17) and extends the same restrictions to a wife's marriage contract, daughters' sustenance, and a yavam's levirate inheritance. A new mishna then asks which acquisitions — the firstborn's extra portion, a husband's inheritance of his wife, a yavam's levirate share, and gifts — must revert to their original owners in the Jubilee Year, generating a four-way tannaitic dispute. The daf, and the whole first perek of Bekhorot, ends with the practicalities of a husband inheriting his late wife's family burial plot.

📈 The Same Rule, Three More Contexts

Wife, daughters, and yavam all face the identical limitation

Wife's Ketubah

  • Cannot claim increase in value, though a creditor normally could (Shmuel)
  • Rabbi Abba: this is one of the leniencies the Sages built into a marriage contract

Daughters' Sustenance

  • A stipulation in the ketubah is treated like the ketubah itself
  • Cannot claim what the ketubah itself cannot claim

The Yavam

  • Scripture calls him a 'firstborn' (Deut. 25:6)
  • Abaye: excluded only for increase between death and levirate marriage — after that, he does take it, since he has 'succeeded' his brother
Rava's stricter view: Even the increase between levirate marriage and the actual distribution is excluded — a yavam, like a firstborn, has no special claim before the inheritance is formally divided.

🔄 Which Portions Return in the Jubilee Year?

The new mishna's four-way dispute
❓ Fields acquired by sale return to original owners in the Jubilee Year — but what about the firstborn's extra share, a husband's inheritance, a yavam's share, and gifts?
↓
🟢 Rabbi Meir: none of them return — each is an inheritance or gift, not a sale, and 'sold' land alone must return
↓
Rabbis
A gift IS like a sale ('you shall return' teaches this) — so gifts return, but true inheritances (firstborn, husband, yavam) do not
R' Elazar
ALL of these — including the firstborn's portion — count as gifts and DO return
↓
👨‍👩‍👧 Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Beroka: even a husband's inheritance from his wife must return to her family, deducting its monetary value
Rav Sheshet's clarification: 'Do not return' in the mishna means the firstborn doesn't lose his portion for nothing — when the property IS redistributed, he again takes his double share.

⚖️ Are Divided Brothers 'Purchasers' From Each Other?

Rabbi Yoḥanan's ruling and Rabbi Elazar's objection
R' Yochanan
Brothers who divide an inherited estate are considered purchasers from one another — so they must return their shares to each other in the Jubilee Year.
Objection
But the mishna explicitly lists the firstborn's portion among those that do NOT return in the Jubilee!
R' Elazar
'Do not return' means they don't return for nothing — the firstborn gets his double share again once redistribution happens.
Confirmed
Ravin cites R' Yoḥanan (or R' Elazar ben Shammua): yes — 'they return' really does mean forfeiting the portion entirely, for naught.

🪦 Deducting the Cost of a Family Burial Plot

כְּגוֹן שֶׁהוֹרִישַׁתּוּ אִשְׁתּוֹ בֵּית הַקְּבָרוֹת
Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Beroka's ruling explained: A husband who inherits his wife's property must sometimes return it to her family — for example, if she bequeathed him the family graveyard. Since burying outsiders' relatives there would be a family disgrace, the Sages ruled he must return the plot itself, but first deduct from its value the cost of his wife's own burial there, since a husband is obligated to bury his wife. This closes the first perek of Bekhorot, 'Yesh Bekhor.'
