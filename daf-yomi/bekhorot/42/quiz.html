QUESTION 1 OF 5
What recurring move does the Gemara make when a baraita seems to prove that a tumtum is definitely disqualified as a distinct category?
A. It accepts the proof immediately
B. It responds 'omit the tumtum from here,' explaining the source really refers only to the hermaphrodite
C. It rejects the entire baraita as unreliable
D. It concludes the halakha is undecided
Correct: B — Each time a source seems to definitively exclude the tumtum alongside the hermaphrodite, the Gemara explains that the source is really only about the hermaphrodite, since the tumtum's status is merely doubtful, not definitively excluded.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
According to Rabbi Eliezer, why does a tumtum or hermaphrodite bird NOT transmit impurity when pinched, unlike an animal tumtum/hermaphrodite?
A. Birds cannot be tumtum or hermaphrodite
B. The Torah never uses 'male' and 'female' language regarding bird offerings, so his rule for excluding tumtum/hermaphrodite (which applies only where that language appears) doesn't apply to birds
C. Birds are never fit for the altar
D. Pinching always purifies impurity
Correct: B — Rabbi Eliezer's principle is that 'male/female' language in the Torah is what triggers the exclusion of tumtum and hermaphrodite; since bird offerings are described only as 'doves and pigeons,' no such exclusion applies, and the bird remains fit.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
According to Reish Lakish, why is an animal tumtum's sex not really a case of doubt, unlike a human tumtum's?
A. Animals have no legal status at all
B. In animals, the male and female sex organs are located in different places, so which location the animal urinates from reveals its true sex; in humans, both organs occupy the same location, creating genuine doubt
C. Animal tumtums do not exist
D. Human tumtums are always resolved by examination
Correct: B — Reish Lakish distinguishes based on anatomy: since an animal's male and female organs are in different places, urination reveals the true sex, unlike in humans where the concealed area is shared.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
What is the concern behind the principle 'since it changed, it changed' (הוֹאִיל וְאִישְׁתַּנִּי אִישְׁתַּנִּי) as applied to a tumtum animal?
A. That the animal's color might change
B. That since the animal is already abnormal (a tumtum), its male organ might also be abnormally inverted toward the female location, undermining the urination test
C. That the animal might change species
D. That the animal's age is uncertain
Correct: B — The principle suggests that an animal already known to be unusual (a tumtum) might have additional hidden abnormalities, such as an inverted organ, casting doubt on relying on its urination location alone.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
How does the Gemara reconcile the two contradictory baraitot about a human tumtum's levirate marriage (ḥalitza/yibum) obligations?
A. It declares one baraita simply incorrect
B. By attributing them to the dispute between Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Akiva over whether a natural eunuch's wife may undergo levirate marriage
C. By saying the two baraitot discuss different genders entirely
D. There is no reconciliation offered
Correct: B — The baraita permitting levirate marriage follows Rabbi Eliezer (who permits it for a natural eunuch's wife), while the baraita restricting to ḥalitza only follows Rabbi Akiva (who forbids it), combined with the 'since it changed, it changed' concern that a tumtum might also be a natural eunuch.
