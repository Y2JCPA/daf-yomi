📖 Overview: Borders, Bridges, and a New Exemption

Bekhorot 55a–55b
רַבִּי מֵאִיר אוֹמֵר: הַיַּרְדֵּן מַפְסִיק לְמַעְשַׂר בְּהֵמָה
The scene: The daf resolves the debate over how many animals bridge distant flocks by reinterpreting a challenging baraita, then raises Rav Pappa's extension of Shmuel's view to even a shepherd's tools or dog. It then turns to Rabbi Meir's ruling that the Jordan River itself divides flocks for tithing purposes regardless of distance, and Rabbi Ami's qualification that a bridge reunites them. A close look at the biblical text asks whether the Jordan is truly a border, or even part of the Land of Israel at all, launching a broader excursus on the world's rivers — their sources, names, and halakhic uses — before the daf closes with a fresh mishna: one who purchases an animal, or receives one as a gift, is exempt from separating animal tithe on it.

🐕 Sharpening Shmuel's Rule

How little does it take to bridge two distant flocks?
Baraita
Five sheep in Kfar Ḥananya and five in Kfar Otnai (32 mil apart) don't combine unless there's also one sheep in Tzippori, between them — a challenge to Rav's 'five in the middle' rule.
Shmuel resolves
Not really five and five — the case is nine and nine, with just one in the middle bridging both nines to reach ten on either side. This fits Rav too.
Rav Pappa extends
For Shmuel, even just the SHEPHERD himself in the middle — without any sheep — bridges the two flocks. Even his tools left there would suffice, since he must go collect them.
Rav Ashi's open question
What about the shepherd's dog in the middle? Since it usually comes when called, maybe it doesn't count — but sometimes he must go fetch it himself. Unresolved (teiku).

🌊 The Jordan River Divides — Unless Bridged

Rabbi Meir's ruling and its exact textual basis
📜 Rabbi Meir: the Jordan River separates flocks for animal tithe, regardless of distance
↓
🌉 Rabbi Ami: only true when there's no bridge — a bridge reunites the two banks
↓
❓ Objection: a baraita says flocks on opposite riverbanks, or even in two adjoining provinces with NO water between them, still don't combine — so it's not just about physical proximity!
↓
✅ Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba (citing R' Yoḥanan): the real reason is textual — 'the Jordan shall be its border' (Joshua 18:20) makes the river a border in its own right, a formal legal boundary, not merely a physical obstacle

💧 A River Digression: Names, Sources, and Uses

Rabbinic geography before returning to halakha

The Jordan's Course

  • Emerges from the Cave of Pamyas
  • Flows through Lake Ḥula, the Sea of Galilee, and the Dead Sea
  • Called 'Jordan' (yored, 'descends') specifically from Beit Jericho southward

The Euphrates

  • Rav: source of the other three rivers of Eden
  • Named 'Perat' because its waters are 'fruitful and multiply' (parim)
  • Halakhic use: one who vows against 'water from the Euphrates' broadly is barred from ALL water everywhere
Practical impact: Shmuel's father made his daughters a standing mikveh in Nisan (rainy season, concerned about flowing rainwater) but let them immerse directly in the Euphrates in Tishrei, when the water was purely from the river's own source.

🎁 New Mishna: Purchased and Gifted Animals Are Exempt

הַלּוֹקֵחַ, אוֹ שֶׁנִּיתַּן לוֹ בְּמַתָּנָה — פָּטוּר מִמַּעְשַׂר בְּהֵמָה
The rule: One who purchases an animal, or receives one as a gift, is exempt from separating animal tithe on it. This sets up the next daf's inquiry into the verse's source for this exemption — comparing it to the redemption of a firstborn son, which likewise applies only to one's own biological child, never a purchased or adopted one.
