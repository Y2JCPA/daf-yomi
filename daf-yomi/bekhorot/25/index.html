📖 Overview: Plucking, Shedding, and a Cryptic Instruction

Bekhorot 25a–25b
לֹא בָּזֶה הִתִּיר עֲקַבְיָא בֶּן מַהֲלַלְאֵל, אֶלָּא שְׂעַר בַּעַל מוּם שֶׁנָּשַׁר וְהִנִּיחוֹ בַּחַלּוֹן וְאַחַר כָּךְ מֵת
The scene: The daf resolves Rav Huna's Festival dilemma from 24b by testing and refining exactly what Rav holds about unintentional acts, using a barrel-stopper case and the concept of an inevitable result (פְּסִיק רֵישֵׁיהּ). It then tests whether plucking ever counts as true shearing through Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam's surprising leniency about trimming a red heifer's black hair-tips, before refining the original mishna's rules for plucking a firstborn's neck-hair — by hand only, and permitted from the outset even to examine a blemish. A new mishna raises a fresh question: what about hair that sheds from a blemished firstborn while it is still alive? Akavya ben Mahalalel and the Rabbis disagree, but only once Rabbi Yosei clarifies which exact scenario they're arguing about. The daf closes with Rav Sheshet's puzzle over a baraita's terse instruction — 'let it be investigated' — which the Gemara connects to no fewer than four different, already-familiar tannaitic disputes.

🍷 Rav's Festival Ruling Reconsidered

Testing whether Rav truly permits unintentional acts on a Festival
1️⃣ Challenge: Rav elsewhere forbids tightly inserting a cloth barrel-stopper on a Festival, since liquid is inevitably squeezed out — suggesting Rav forbids unintentional acts generally
↓
2️⃣ Answer: that case is different — even Rabbi Shimon, who usually permits unintentional acts, concedes when the forbidden result is inevitable (פְּסִיק רֵישֵׁיהּ, 'cut off its head and expect it not to die')
↓
3️⃣ Further challenge: doesn't Rav actually rule like Rabbi Yehuda, who forbids unintentional acts as a rule — not like the lenient Rabbi Shimon?
↓
4️⃣ Resolution: Rav does hold an unintentional act is generally forbidden — but he permits plucking a firstborn's hair because plucking is not a form of shearing at all, and on a Festival it counts as an unusual, non-standard way of uprooting growth, which Torah law doesn't prohibit
Bottom line: The permission to pluck hair on a Festival rests on redefining the act itself, not on any general leniency for unintentional consequences.

🐄 Does Plucking Ever Count as Shearing?

Testing the claim with a bird's feather and a red heifer's hairs

Plucking a Feather = Shearing

  • A baraita: plucking a bird's wing-feather on Shabbat brings liability for shearing
  • Reish Lakish confirms: plucking IS the normal method for removing feathers, so it counts as shearing there

Plucking Wool ≠ Shearing

  • Wool is normally removed with scissors, not by plucking — so plucking wool is an unusual method, unlike shearing
  • This is why Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam's plucking technique for a firstborn's neck doesn't violate the Torah's shearing ban

The Red Heifer Test Case

  • Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam permits trimming two black hair-tips on a red heifer with scissors — an apparent act of true shearing on a consecrated animal
  • Several explanations are tried and rejected (no wool status; only Temple-maintenance sanctity) before the Gemara settles on: it is simply an uncommon case the Sages' fence never covered
Open question: Does Rav's position match Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam's reasoning exactly, or do they merely reach the same practical result by different routes?

✂️ Hand or Tool? Ab Initio or After the Fact?

Refining exactly how and when a firstborn's hair may be plucked
Reish Lakish
The mishna's permission to clear hair applies only when done by hand — using an actual tool looks too much like deliberate shearing, and is forbidden.
Objection
But the mishna explicitly says he clears space 'with a cleaver' (בְּקוֹפִיץ)!
Answer
Reread it: he clears space with his hands, in preparation to then use a cleaver for the actual slaughter — not that the cleaver itself plucks the hair.
New Question
Is plucking hair to examine a blemish permitted from the outset (ab initio), or only tolerated after the fact?
R' Yirmeya's Proof
A baraita: wool tangled in a firstborn's ear, hiding a blemish — Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam says one may pluck it outright to show a Sage. This proves it's permitted ab initio.
Rav Mari
Confirmed by the mishna's own comparison: 'and likewise' equates examining a blemish with the original, ab initio permission to pluck for slaughter.
Settled: Plucking (never cutting with a tool) is permitted from the very outset, both for slaughter and for blemish examination.

🐑 New Mishna: Hair Shed From a Blemished Firstborn

Akavya ben Mahalalel and the Rabbis disagree — but about exactly which scenario?
ScenarioRuling
Hair shed while alive, animal later slaughteredEveryone agrees: the shed hair may be used (per Rabbi Yosei's correction of the dispute)
Hair shed while alive, animal later died naturally (not slaughtered)Akavya ben Mahalalel: permitted; the Rabbis: forbidden — this is the true point of dispute
Dangling wool that visibly looks part of the fleecePermitted once the animal is later shorn after death
Dangling wool that does not visibly look part of the fleeceForbidden
Rabbi Yosei's correction: Rabbi Yehuda first reported the dispute as applying to a slaughtered animal — Rabbi Yosei corrects him: everyone permits that case; the real disagreement is about an animal that simply died.

🔍 Rav Sheshet's 'Let It Be Investigated' Puzzle

A baraita's two cryptic words (יְבַקֵּר) get four different explanations
❓ A blemished firstborn mixes into a flock and is never found. Rabbi Yosei: 'יְבַקֵּר' — let it be investigated. Investigated for what?
↓
Reading 1
About sheared wool already mixed with ordinary wool — mirrors the Rabbi Yehuda/Rabbi Yosei dispute on a slaughtered firstborn's wool
Reading 2
Whether an expert had certified the blemish before the wool was shed (Rava's refinement)
↓
Reading 3
About the animals themselves, when a search failed to find the blemished one — mirrors Rabbi Meir vs. the Rabbis on an unresolved impurity search
Reading 4
About animals where the blemished one was found — mirrors Rebbi vs. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel on a once-lost, now-found grave
Pattern: The Gemara repeatedly explains a difficult local dispute by mapping it onto a structurally identical dispute already known from elsewhere in the Mishna.

📋 Bekhorot 25 — Summary Map

🍷 Rav permits plucking on a Festival because plucking wool is not shearing, not because unintentional acts are generally allowed
↓
🐄 The red heifer's black hair-tips may be trimmed only because that case is uncommon, not because plucking is universally harmless
↓
✂️ Hand-plucking, not tool-cutting, is permitted from the outset — for slaughter and for blemish examination alike
↓
🐑 Akavya ben Mahalalel and the Rabbis truly disagree only about hair shed before a natural death, not before slaughter
🔍 'Let it be investigated' recruits four separate, already-familiar disputes to explain one baraita
↓
🎓 Key Principle: Precise definitions of method, timing, and intention — not broad assumptions — determine what counts as forbidden shearing or usable hair
