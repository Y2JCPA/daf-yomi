📖 Overview: Cataloguing the Eye's Blemishes

Bekhorot 38a–38b
הָרִיס שֶׁל עַיִן שֶׁנִּיקַּב, שֶׁנִּפְגַּם, שֶׁנִּסְדַּק, הֲרֵי בְּעֵינוֹ דַּק, תְּבַלּוּל, חִלָּזוֹן, נָחָשׁ, עָצָב
The scene: After closing out the technical measurement discussion for skull deficiencies (used earlier to calibrate the size of an ear-piercing), the Gemara moves to a new mishna cataloguing blemishes of the eye that permit a firstborn's slaughter: a torn or pierced eyelid, cataracts, and growths shaped like a snail, snake, or berry. The Gemara sorts out which cataracts count depending on whether they sit in the black or white of the eye, tells a story about a sage who ruled a halakha for his own benefit only because he had taught it beforehand, and defines exactly how long certain conditions — pale spots, constantly tearing eyes — must persist to count as permanent blemishes rather than passing ailments.

👁️ New Mishna: The Eye's Disqualifying Blemishes

Beyond blindness, several specific conditions of the eye permit slaughter
BlemishDescription
Eyelid (רִיס) pierced, damaged, or splitThe 'outer barrier' of the eye — Rav Pappa's definition
Dak (cataract)A film or cloudiness in the eye; counts depending on location (black/white) and whether sunken or floating
TevallulA thread of one color bisecting the iris and entering the other — disputed whether direction matters
Chilazon, snake, or berry-shaped growthA growth covering the pupil, considered a permanent blemish
Terminology dilemma: Is 'snail-shaped growth' the same thing the Sages elsewhere call 'snake-shaped,' or are these two distinct blemishes? The Gemara resolves via a transmitted tradition that they are one and the same.

⚪⚫ Sunken vs. Floating: Two Contradictory Baraitot

A cataract's status depends on both its depth and its color
Baraita 1
A sunken cataract is a blemish; a floating one is not.
Objection
But another baraita teaches the exact opposite — sunken is NOT a blemish, floating IS!
First try
Perhaps one baraita discusses the black of the eye, the other the white — but 'there are no blemishes in the white'!
Resolution
Rabba bar bar Ḥana, citing Yoshiya of Usha: a black cataract — sunken is a blemish, floating is not; a white cataract — sunken is NOT a blemish, floating IS. Mnemonic: בַּרְקָא (white patches that float ARE a blemish).
Result: Both baraitot are correct — each describes a different color of cataract, with opposite rules for sunken versus floating.

📖 Rabbi Shimon ben Yosei Lakonya's Lesson

Can a sage rule on a matter that benefits himself?
1️⃣ An elder, Rabbi Shimon ben Yosei Lakonya, calls a young Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Elazar to sit and teaches him: a snail-shaped growth in the eye is a permanent blemish permitting slaughter — and this is the same thing the Sages call 'snake'
↓
2️⃣ He adds: though 'a person may not rule on blemishes for himself,' a teacher may instruct his students in halakha, and the students may then apply that ruling back to their teacher's own animal
↓
3️⃣ Objection: doesn't Rav Huna say a scholar's self-interested ruling is heeded only if he stated it BEFORE the case became relevant to him?
↓
4️⃣ Resolution: that is precisely what happened here — he had already been teaching this halakha to his students well before his own firstborn developed the blemish
Principle: A ruling given in advance of self-interest, then applied by others, avoids the conflict-of-interest concern entirely.

🧵 The Tevallul Dispute: Which Direction Counts?

A thread of one color crossing into the other — Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yosei disagree

Rabbi Meir

  • Either direction is a blemish: white entering black, OR black entering white
  • Reasoning: tevallul means 'that which confuses (מְבַלְבֵּל) the eyes' — any mixing counts

Rabbi Yosei

  • Only white entering black counts as a blemish
  • Black entering white does NOT count, since 'there are no blemishes in the white of the eye'
  • Source: Psalms 73:7 calls the white 'the fat of the eye,' not 'their eyes' itself — so Leviticus 21:20's 'tevallul in his eye' excludes it
The mishna follows: Rabbi Yosei's view — only a white thread entering the black pupil is called tevallul.

💧 How Long Must a Condition Persist?

New mishna: pale spots and constantly-tearing eyes, and the tests that confirm them are permanent
ConditionThresholdNote
Pale spots (חֲוַרְוָור) — Rabbi Yehuda (mishna)80 daysRabbi Meir (baraita): only 40 days needed
Constant tears — fodder testMoist fodder, then dry fodder, with no improvementOrder matters — dry-then-moist does NOT prove the condition is permanent
Rain-watered vs. irrigated fieldsRain-watered fodder proves it; irrigated fodder does notOnly Rabbi Yehuda's version, per the Gemara's emended reading
Duration of the whole testThree monthsCorrelated to Adar–half-Nisan (moist season) and Elul–half-Tishrei (dry season)
Rabbi Ḥananya ben Antigonus adds: For pale spots, examine the animal three separate times within the 80-day window to confirm the condition is truly constant.
