📖 Overview: Species, Resemblance, and What May Be Eaten

Bekhorot 6a–6b
זֶה הַכְּלָל: הַיּוֹצֵא מִן הַטָּמֵא — טָמֵא, וְהַיּוֹצֵא מִן הַטָּהוֹר — טָהוֹר
The scene: The daf first nails down why only donkeys, among non-kosher animals, require redemption at all — comparing two independent derivations. It then works through a chain of dilemmas about cross-species births with partial resemblance to the mother, before turning to the mishna's kashrut rule that an offspring's status always follows its mother, not its own appearance. Rabbi Shimon dissents sharply regarding a camel born to a cow, which launches a wide-ranging discussion — including a famous aggadic aside about Shimon HaAmasoni's rule for expounding the word 'et' — into why the Torah needs a special verse to forbid a non-kosher animal's milk at all.

🐴 Excluding Horses and Camels From Redemption

Two independent routes to the same halakha
❓ How do we know firstborn horses and camels need no redemption at all — not even with a non-lamb item?
↓
📖 The doubled phrase 'firstborn of a donkey...firstborn of a donkey' teaches: donkeys alone are redeemed
↓
Rav Pappa's source
'From all your livestock take the males' (generalization) — 'ox, lamb, donkey' (detail). The generalization is limited to the detail: only these three species
R' Yosei HaGelili vs. Rabbis
Does 'firstborn' before 'donkey' interrupt the list (R' Yosei: donkey excluded), or does the extra 'and' fold it back in (Rabbis: donkey included)?
↓
✅ Rabbis: donkey needs its own split-and-rejoin because it carries a different TYPE of sanctity (value) than ox/lamb (inherent, offered on the altar)

🤔 Four Escalating Dilemmas: Partial Resemblance

How far does 'some characteristics of the mother' extend across species?
1️⃣ A cow gives birth to a donkey-like creature with some of its mother's traits. Does partial resemblance obligate it, even though cow and donkey carry different types of sanctity (inherent vs. value)?
↓
2️⃣ Resolved from a baraita: yes — since both species carry some form of firstborn sanctity, partial resemblance is enough
↓
3️⃣ Next: does this extend to a donkey giving birth to a horse-like creature (a species with no firstborn sanctity at all)? A second baraita turns out to address a different concern — that a donkey and horse both lack horns and have closed hooves, so one might mistake the offspring for merely an odd-colored donkey
↓
4️⃣ Left open: whether a cow's horse-like offspring — combining kosher-vs-non-kosher and sanctified-vs-unsanctified — ever gets firstborn status through partial resemblance
Method: The Gemara tests each new dilemma against existing baraitot, but not every baraita answers the question it initially seems to.

🐫 Kashrut Follows the Mother — Except According to Rabbi Shimon

A camel born to a cow: permitted or forbidden to eat?

The Rabbis (mishna's rule)

  • An offspring's kashrut always follows its mother's status, never its own appearance
  • Source: Leviticus 11:4 excludes only a kosher-looking creature born of a non-kosher mother
  • A camel born to a cow (kosher mother) would be permitted

Rabbi Shimon

  • The doubled verse 'camel...camel' (Leviticus 11:4, Deuteronomy 14:7) forbids both a camel born of a camel and one born of a cow
  • Also derives from the extra word 'et' (אֶת הַגָּמָל) that a camel's milk is forbidden

The Rabbis on Milk

  • They too use the doubled 'camel, camel' — but split it differently: one verse forbids the camel itself, the other its milk
  • They reject deriving new halakhot from the word 'et' altogether

📜 Shimon HaAmasoni and the Word 'Et'

A method of exegesis pushed until it hits a wall
Shimon HaAm.
I expound every extra word 'et' (אֶת) in the Torah to derive new halakhot — until I reached 'You shall fear et the Lord your God' (Deuteronomy 6:13). What could 'et' possibly add to the fear of God?
Students
But Rabbi — what becomes of everything you already derived from every earlier 'et'?
Shimon HaAm.
Just as I received reward for expounding, so I receive reward for withdrawing.
R' Akiva
Later I found the answer: that 'et' includes Torah scholars — one must revere them just as one reveres God.
Why it matters here: This is exactly the dispute behind Rabbi Shimon's camel-milk derivation: he expounds 'et hagamal,' while the Rabbis, following Shimon HaAmasoni's retraction, do not.
