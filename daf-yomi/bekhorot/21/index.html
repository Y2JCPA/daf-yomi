📖 Overview: Timing, Discharges, and a New Mishna

Bekhorot 21a–21b
רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר בֶּן יַעֲקֹב אוֹמֵר: בְּהֵמָה גַּסָּה שֶׁשָּׁפְעָה חֲרָרַת דָּם, הֲרֵי זוֹ תִּקָּבֵר, וּפְטוּרָה מִן הַבְּכוֹרָה
The scene: The daf finishes the goat-triplets tithe puzzle by adding a fourth possible explanation for the dispute between the baraita's first tanna and Rabbi Shimon: whether an animal too young to be sacrificed (מְחוּסַּר זְמַן) may still enter the pen to be counted for the tithe. The Gemara then works out where that halakha itself comes from, testing an analogy to the firstborn against one to ordinary sacrifices before settling on a verbal analogy Rabbi Shimon employs. A short discussion defines what physically counts as the murky discharge that exempts a future offspring, and Rav Ḥisda's related question about how long fetal formation takes in an animal is left unresolved. The Gemara then asks what happens when an animal is purchased from a fellow Jew rather than a gentile, weighing three opinions before the mishna turns to a new case: a large animal that expels a mass of congealed blood.

🐐 Tanna Kama vs. Rabbi Shimon: Four Explanations

The second-generation goats discharge at the start of month seven — did they still give birth in time?
1️⃣ Explanation 1 (Ze'eiri's rule): the first tanna accepts that a discharge blocks pregnancy for 30 days, pushing any birth into year two; Rabbi Shimon rejects Ze'eiri, allowing a year-one birth
↓
2️⃣ Explanation 2 (premature birth): both accept Ze'eiri, but dispute whether an animal can give birth prematurely (מְקוּטָּעִין) — the first tanna says no, Rabbi Shimon says yes
↓
3️⃣ Explanation 3 (part of a day): both reject premature birth, but dispute whether part of a day counts as a whole day — the first tanna says no, Rabbi Shimon says yes
↓
4️⃣ Explanation 4 (new): all agree part of a day counts as whole — instead they dispute whether an animal too young to be sacrificed (מְחוּסַּר זְמַן) may even enter the pen to be tithed at all
Pattern: Each 'if you wish, say instead' isolates one more variable, the same technique used on 20a–20b for a different dispute.

📜 Where Does 'Mechusar Zman Enters the Pen' Come From?

Rabbi Shimon ben Yehuda, in Rabbi Shimon's name: it does — but why?

Parallels to the Firstborn (chosen first)

  • Cannot be redeemed even once blemished
  • A blemish does not prevent consecration
  • A substitute animal is never sacrificed
  • Eaten without any redemption required

Parallels to Ordinary Sacrifices (rejected)

  • Not limited to males only
  • Sanctified through human action, not intrinsically
  • Not among the gifts given to a priest
  • Begins life as an ordinary, unremarkable animal

Rabbi Shimon's Real Source

  • Not an analogy of traits at all — a verbal analogy (גְּזֵרָה שָׁוָה)
  • 'You shall pass' (תַּעֲבִיר, Exodus 13:12, firstborn)
  • 'Whatsoever passes under the rod' (יַעֲבוֹר, Leviticus 27:32, tithe)
Twist: After the Gemara argues both ways about which comparison fits better, it concludes the real derivation was never about shared traits at all.

🩸 What Exactly Is a 'Murky Discharge'?

Defining the sign that exempts a future offspring from firstborn status
AuthorityPosition
Rava (quoting shepherds)A discharge indicating the animal absorbed or suppressed a fetus within the womb
ShmuelBubbles of blood
RequirementThe discharge must be shown to a knowledgeable shepherd, familiar with the different kinds of discharge — not a Torah scholar
Rav Ḥisda's open questionFetal formation in a woman takes 40 days — how long does it take in an animal? Left unresolved, and distinct from Ze'eiri's 30-day post-discharge infertility rule
Practical stakes: Getting the definition right determines whether an owner may treat a future birth as certainly non-sacred.

🛒 Buying an Animal From a Fellow Jew

Three views on an animal purchased from another Jew, with unknown birth history
Rav
Its offspring is certainly a firstborn. If the seller's animal had already given birth, he would have boasted about it to reassure the buyer.
Shmuel
Its status is uncertain. The seller assumes the buyer wants the animal for slaughter, so he wouldn't bother mentioning its birth history either way.
R' Yoḥanan
Its offspring is certainly non-sacred. If the animal had not yet given birth, the seller — knowing a prohibition is involved — would surely have informed the buyer.
Challenge
A baraita about slaughtering a mother and offspring together supports Rabbi Yoḥanan's logic: if the seller didn't warn the buyer, he may slaughter freely. Doesn't this refute Rav and Shmuel?
Answer
No — there, the duty to inform rests on the seller. Here, the duty to inquire rests on the buyer. The two cases allocate responsibility differently.
Key difference from the gentile case: A fellow Jew is assumed to follow social norms of disclosure that a gentile seller is not assumed to follow.

🩸 A Mass of Congealed Blood

New mishna: Rabbi Eliezer ben Ya'akov on an unusual expulsion
❓ A large animal expels a mass of congealed blood (חֲרָרַת דָּם) — what is its status?
↓
⚖️ Rabbi Eliezer ben Ya'akov: it must be buried — perhaps a male fetus was consecrated as firstborn within it when it emerged — and the mother is exempt from having any future offspring counted firstborn
↓
🔍 Gemara challenge (Rabbi Ḥiyya): the mass does not impart ritual impurity through touching or carrying, unlike an actual fetus
↓
❔ If it isn't treated as a real fetus for impurity, why must it still be buried? An open question as the daf continues
Underlying logic: Burial expresses doubt about sanctity even where the mass fails the standard test for being a fetus.
