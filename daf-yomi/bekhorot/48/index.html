📖 Overview: When Twins Blur the Firstborn

Bekhorot 48a–48b
מִי שֶׁלֹּא בִּכְּרָה אִשְׁתּוֹ וְיָלְדָה שְׁנֵי זְכָרִים — נוֹתֵן חָמֵשׁ סְלָעִים לְכֹהֵן
The scene: The daf's new mishna addresses a wife's first pregnancy that produces twin boys: since one of them is definitely a firstborn but no one knows which, the father owes five sela after 30 days regardless. The Gemara probes what happens if the father dies first — do the sons, as heirs, still owe the debt, and does it matter whether they already divided the estate? This spins into a debate about whether 'a loan written in the Torah' can be collected from liened property already sold, and whether the mitzva requires exactly five sela or accepts partial payment. The daf then turns to two wives who each bore a son that became intermingled, generating a parallel set of complications for two priests instead of one.

🏠 Why Does Dividing the Estate Matter?

The Gemara narrows the mishna to a case within 30 days of birth
❓ If the father died after 30 days, the property is already liened for the mitzva — dividing shouldn't matter at all
↓
💡 So the mishna must mean the father died WITHIN 30 days — the obligation attaches to the sons themselves, not yet to the property
↓
🤝 Rabbi Yirmeya: like two men both named Yosef ben Shimon who jointly own a field — a creditor of either can collect from it, since he's owed by one of them either way
↓
⚖️ Rava's answer: brothers who divide inherited property are half-heirs, half-purchasers (Rav Asi) — and an oral Torah debt collects only from heirs, not purchasers (Rav Pappa)
Result: Once divided, the priest can collect only half the debt — the half where the sons remain 'heirs' rather than 'purchasers' from each other.

💰 Five Sela — Or Half of Five?

Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehuda locate their true disagreement
R' Meir
The Torah requires giving specifically five sela — never half or part of five. If only two-and-a-half sela are available from the property, we take nothing.
R' Yehuda
The Torah's five sela can be given even in part — two-and-a-half sela of liened property is still collectible.
Gemara
But a baraita has R' Yehuda requiring ten dinars specifically from each brother's inherited share — doesn't that show he also needs the full five?
Resolution
Both agree on 'five, not half of five' — their real dispute is Rav Asi's rule about heirs-vs-purchasers, which R' Yehuda rejects entirely.
Bottom line: R' Yehuda holds the whole five sela remains collectible from the divided estate because he does not accept that division makes brothers into purchasers from each other.

👩‍👩‍👦 Two Wives, One Confused Pair of Sons

The mishna extends the uncertainty to two separate priests

Both Give to One Priest

  • 10 sela given for two intermingled firstborns
  • If one son dies within 30 days: priest returns 5 sela
  • Each mother's son is certainly her own firstborn

Each Gives to a Different Priest

  • If one son then dies, neither priest returns anything
  • Each priest claims: 'I received payment for the surviving child'
  • The father cannot recover from either possession

Mixed Combinations

  • Male + female (either mother): 5 sela owed — one is certainly firstborn
  • Two females + one male, or 2+2: nothing owed — female may have come first each time

🔀 Why Can't the Priest Just Reject Each Claimant?

Rava's guarantor analogy resolves the two-priest puzzle
1️⃣ Question: why can't each father say to each priest, 'go collect from the other one instead'?
↓
2️⃣ Rava: a person's own property is his own guarantee — one cannot claim from a guarantor when one could have claimed from the debtor directly
↓
3️⃣ Yet the mishna clearly lets the priest collect from divided property even when he can't identify which brother owes him
↓
4️⃣ Answer: Rabbi Yirmeya's joint-field precedent shows a creditor CAN collect from either partner precisely because he's owed by one of the two
Two versions: The Gemara records a first version built around brothers who bought a shared field, and a second (48b) built around one Yosef ben Shimon selling to the other — reaching the same halakhic conclusion.
