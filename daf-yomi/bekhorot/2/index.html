📖 Overview: The Firstborn Donkey Belongs to a Jew Alone

Bekhorot 2a–2b
בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל, אֲבָל לֹא בַּאֲחֵרִים
The scene: Masechet Bekhorot opens not with the firstborns of kosher animals but with the firstborn donkey (פֶּטֶר חֲמוֹר), which its owner must redeem with a lamb or whose neck he must break. The very first mishna teaches that this obligation applies only when the donkey belongs entirely to a Jew: if a gentile has any share in it — through purchase, sale, partnership, receivership, or care-taking — the donkey is fully exempt, based on the verse 'I sanctified to Me all the firstborn in Israel' (Numbers 3:13). The Gemara first shows why the mishna needed all five scenarios rather than one, then pursues a related question raised elsewhere in Shas: does Rabbi Yehuda, who permits selling a damaged animal to a gentile, also permit selling an unborn fetus, or even an animal sold 'for its fetuses' alone?

🐴 The Mishna's Five Cases — Why Each Is Needed

Every scenario in the mishna teaches something the others could not
CaseGemara's reason it's necessary
Purchaser (לוֹקֵחַ)Otherwise I'd think it's exempt only because the Jew brings the fetus into sanctity
Seller (מוֹכֵר)Teaches he is not penalized for removing the fetus from sanctity, even though the sale itself is forbidden
Partner (מִשְׁתַּתֵּף)Excludes Rabbi Yehuda's view that a gentile partnership animal is obligated in firstborn status
Receiver (מְקַבֵּל)Stated mainly to set up the parallel case that follows
Giver in receivership (נוֹתֵן בְּקַבָּלָה)Teaches he is not penalized, lest this be confused with a case where the gentile has no rights to the offspring
The verse: "I sanctified to Me all the firstborn in Israel, both man and animal" (Numbers 3:13) — the mitzva applies only to Jews, so any gentile share exempts the animal.

🗡️ Does 'Broken' Include a Fetus?

Rabbi Yehuda permits selling a damaged animal to a gentile — what about an unborn one?
Dilemma
Rabbi Yehuda permits selling a broken (שְׁבוּרָה) animal to a gentile, since it can't work. A fetus also can't work — does his leniency extend to it?
Or not
Or is a broken animal different? That's not its natural state. A fetus's incapacity is natural and temporary — it will grow to work.
Proof?
Come and hear: the mishna says the seller acted without permission, and does not exempt Rabbi Yehuda from that ruling — so he must agree it's forbidden!
Rejected
But the mishna also doesn't exempt Rabbi Yehuda from the partnership case — yet we know from a baraita that he disputes it. The mishna simply omits his view throughout.
Rav Ashi
Come and hear instead: Rabbi Yehuda permits a broken animal specifically because it cannot be cured — implying a curable animal is forbidden.
Conclusion
שְׁמַע מִינַּהּ — a fetus will eventually be able to work, so it counts as curable. Learn from this that Rabbi Yehuda forbids selling a fetus to a gentile.
Result: Rabbi Yehuda's leniency for a damaged animal does not extend to an unborn fetus.

🐮 Selling an Animal 'For Its Fetuses'

A new dilemma: the Jew keeps the mother, sells only rights to her future offspring
❓ A Jew sells a gentile only the rights to an animal's future offspring, keeping the animal itself. Permitted?
↓
Rabbi Yehuda's side
Maybe he permits only a broken animal (visibly distinct, no confusion) but forbids this sale — or the opposite: since the mother never leaves the Jew's hand, all the more permitted
The Rabbis' side
Maybe they forbid only selling a broken animal (it visibly leaves him) but permit this sale — or the opposite: the risk of confusion with a full sale is even greater here
↓
⚖️ Both dilemmas are raised but left unresolved in our sugya
Note: The Gemara raises the identical structural question for both Rabbi Yehuda and the Rabbis, and answers neither.

💰 Rabbi Yehuda's Baraita: Two Very Different Penalties

Same firstborn donkey, opposite outcomes depending on who acted improperly

Received From a Gentile (מְקַבֵּל)

  • A permitted arrangement — the Jew cares for the gentile's animal in exchange for a share of offspring
  • If it bears a firstborn: assess its value
  • The Jew gives half its value to the priest

Given in Receivership (נוֹתֵן בְּקַבָּלָה)

  • Prohibited — the Jew hands his own animal to a gentile
  • The Sages penalize him: buy out the gentile's share for up to ten times its value
  • He gives the entire value to the priest

Gemara's Fix

  • The penalty text (דָּמָיו) is emended to the feminine (דָּמֶיהָ) — it targets the animal, not the fetus
  • Case: a pregnant animal given to a gentile just to fatten it
  • Since we penalize for the animal, we penalize for its fetus too
Big idea: Acting within a permitted structure costs half the value; violating the prohibition against entrusting an animal to a gentile costs everything.

🐐 Rabbi Yehuda's Real Reason for the Leniency

The Rabbis press him: doesn't a broken animal still give birth?
1️⃣ The Rabbis object: you permit selling a broken animal, but don't they breed it with a male, and it still gives birth to gentile-owned offspring?
↓
2️⃣ That would suggest the Rabbis' true objection is the future offspring — so they should also forbid a fetuses-only sale
↓
3️⃣ Gemara clarifies: the Rabbis' actual concern is confusion with a full sale of a healthy animal, not the offspring itself
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Yehuda's own answer: he permits a broken animal because it is 'as good as sold for slaughter' — and practically, a broken animal does not accept a male, so this concern rarely arises
Closing line: The Rabbis' governing rule: כׇּל זְמַן שֶׁיַּד הַגּוֹי בָּאֶמְצַע — פְּטוּרָה מִן הַבְּכוֹרָה — whenever a gentile's hand is involved at all, the animal is exempt from firstborn status.
