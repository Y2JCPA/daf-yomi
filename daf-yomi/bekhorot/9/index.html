📖 Overview: Twin Births and the Limits of Benefit

Bekhorot 9a–9b
וּפֶטֶר חֲמוֹר תִּפְדֶּה בְשֶׂה
The scene: After a brief coda to the Athens story, a new mishna sets out how many lambs a priest receives when one or two never-before-birthing donkeys give birth to various combinations of males and females. The Gemara first debates whose opinion the mishna follows regarding twins whose heads emerge simultaneously, then turns to a substantial dispute between Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon: may one derive benefit from a firstborn donkey before it is redeemed with a lamb? The dispute is traced to two competing readings of a single verse, and closes with the edge case of a donkey whose neck was broken instead of being redeemed.

How many lambs are owed, across several birth scenarios
CaseResult
One donkey (first birth), two malesOne lamb to the priest — certainly one is firstborn
One donkey (first birth), male + femaleOwner keeps one lamb for himself — uncertain which was born first, and the burden of proof rests on the priest
Two donkeys (first births), each bears one maleTwo lambs to the priest
Two donkeys together bear male+female, or 2 males+1 femaleOne lamb to the priest — at least one male is certainly firstborn
Two donkeys together bear 2 females+1 male, or 2+2The priest gets nothing — perhaps both firstborns were female
The redeeming lamb: May be a sheep or goat, male or female, young or old, unblemished or blemished — and once the priest returns it, the same lamb may redeem donkeys again and again.

👥 Simultaneous Twins — Whose View Does the Mishna Follow?

Rabbi Yirmeya and Abaye debate the mishna's silent assumption
R' Yirmeya
The mishna's 'one lamb only' rule can't follow Rabbi Yosei HaGelili — he holds it's possible for two heads to emerge in one simultaneous instant, in which case both would be firstborn.
Abaye
Not necessarily. For a kosher animal, the plural verse 'the males shall be the Lord's' teaches simultaneous twins are both firstborn — but no such plural language exists for a donkey.
Gemara
But a baraita has Rabbi Yosei HaGelili himself ruling that donkey-twins with simultaneous heads both go to the priest — deriving it from that very same kosher-animal verse!
Conclusion
So Abaye's distinction fails. The mishna simply follows the Rabbis, who reject Rabbi Yosei HaGelili's 'simultaneous birth' premise entirely.

🐴 May One Use a Firstborn Donkey Before Redemption?

Ulla explains the reasoning behind each side
1️⃣ Rabbi Yehuda: forbidden — no item the Torah insists be redeemed specifically with a lamb (even below its own value) remains usable beforehand
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Shimon: permitted — no item whose redemption medium (the lamb) is fully permitted should have its own use forbidden beforehand
↓
3️⃣ Challenge to Rabbi Shimon: what about Sabbatical-year produce? Its redemption money is usable, yet the produce itself becomes forbidden!
↓
4️⃣ Resolved: no real counterexample — that redemption money is also eventually forbidden, following the produce through a whole chain of exchanges — 'the last item is always forbidden'

📜 'Your Ox... Your Flock' — Two Readings, One Verse

Deuteronomy 15:19 excludes different firstborns for each sage

Rabbi Yehuda

  • 'Your ox' excludes a woman's firstborn son — labor with him is always permitted
  • 'Your flock' excludes an animal owned in partnership with a gentile — since he holds such partnerships ARE obligated in firstborn status
  • A firstborn donkey remains forbidden for benefit before redemption

Rabbi Shimon

  • 'Your ox' also excludes a woman's firstborn son
  • 'Your flock' excludes a firstborn donkey instead — since he holds gentile partnerships are already exempt from firstborn status, no special verse was needed there
  • A firstborn donkey is fully permitted for benefit before redemption

⚰️ After the Neck Is Broken — and What Counts as 'Food'

Even Rabbi Shimon draws a line
❓ Rabbi Shimon permits benefit from a live, unredeemed firstborn donkey — but what about one whose neck was broken instead of redeemed?
↓
📖 Rabba: forbidden — a shared phrase, 'breaking the neck,' links it to the heifer of the unsolved murder (עֶגְלָה עֲרוּפָה), whose carcass is also forbidden
↓
General rule
Forbidden-benefit items (orla, a stoned ox, this donkey) still count as 'food' for ritual impurity, since they were once fit to eat
Rabbi Shimon's view
None of them count as 'food' at all — 'food' (הָאֹכֶל אֲשֶׁר יֵאָכֵל) means only something you could feed to others
↓
✅ Even Rabbi Shimon concedes: meat cooked in milk IS susceptible to food-impurity, since both ingredients were briefly permitted food before being forbidden together
