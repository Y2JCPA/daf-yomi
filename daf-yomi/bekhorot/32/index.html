📖 Overview: Selling Sacred Meat, and Who May Share It

Bekhorot 32a–32b
בִּבְכוֹר הוּא אוֹמֵר: ״לֹא יִפְדֶּה״, וְנִמְכָּר; בְּמַעֲשֵׂר נֶאֱמַר: ״לֹא יִגָּאֵל״, וְאֵינוֹ נִמְכָּר
The scene: The daf continues the discussion of the animal tithe offering (ma'aser behema) that became blemished: since Torah law says 'it shall not be redeemed' (Leviticus 27:33), its meat cannot be sold directly, only indirectly, by folding its value into the price of parts that are normally sold. The Gemara searches for the textual source of this rule, working through an elaborate chain of verbal analogies (gezeira shava) linking the tithe, dedications (cherem), and the firstborn offering, before Rava and Rav Ashi each propose their own explanation. The daf then turns to a new mishna: once a blemished firstborn belongs to a priest, may an ordinary Israelite — or even a gentile — join his household group in eating it?

🐐 Selling the Blemished Tithe's Meat

Bekhor vs. ma'aser: two verses, two different rules
OfferingVerseMay the meat be sold?
Firstborn (bekhor)"You shall not redeem" (Numbers 18:17)Yes — the priest may sell it directly, alive or slaughtered
Animal tithe (ma'aser)"It shall not be redeemed" (Leviticus 27:33)Never sold directly — only by 'inclusion' (הַבְלָעָה) in its hide, fat, sinews or horns
Orphans' exception: For young orphans the Sages permitted selling a blemished tithe's meat in the ordinary way, treating the rabbinic safeguard as if it did not apply, since Deuteronomy 12:15's 'blessing... after slaughter' is understood to refer specifically to the tithe.

🦴 Can the Meat's Value Be Folded Into the Bones?

Rabbi Ḥiyya and Rabbi Shimon disagree — but maybe they don't
❓ May one include the meat's value in the price of the bones, the way one does with hide, fat, sinews and horns?
↓
⚖️ Rabbi Ḥiyya and Rabbi Shimon b'Rabbi disagree: one permits, one forbids
↓
Reading 1
Small animal — bones unusable, never sold, so no inclusion. Large animal — bones are sold, so inclusion works
Reading 2
Both discuss a large animal. Each sage simply follows the custom of his own locale about whether bones are sold there
↓
✅ Either way, the underlying rule is the same: an item never appraised while the animal was alive can absorb the tithe-meat's value once slaughtered
Principle: The Sages never decreed against selling parts that were not price-factors while the animal lived; for orphans, the law is enforced as if by Torah law itself.

📜 Two Ways to Read 'It Shall Not Be Redeemed'

Rava and Rav Ashi each explain why the tithe's meat cannot be sold

Rava's Approach

  • Skips the whole gezeira-shava chain to cherem
  • Cherem is either fully sacred (still with the owner) or fully non-sacred (given to the priest) — never in between
  • So 'lo yigael' by cherem is free to teach the ma'aser's prohibition on sale directly

Rav Ashi's Approach

  • Reads Leviticus 27:33, 'it and its substitute... shall not be redeemed', together
  • Substitution (temura) only works on a live animal — so 'not redeemed' here must mean 'not redeemed', i.e. sold, while alive
  • After slaughter the sanctity is weaker, yet the Gemara shows by a fortiori that it must remain unsellable even then
Why not write 'not sold' plainly? Had the Torah written only 'it shall not be sold,' one might think redemption (transferring sanctity to coins) remained possible. 'Lo yigael' teaches both: not sold, and not redeemed.

🔑 Rava's Key Move: Where Do Dedications Sit?

כׇּל זְמַן שֶׁהֵן בְּבֵית בְּעָלִים — הֲרֵי הֵן כְּהֶקְדֵּשׁ לְכׇל דִּבְרֵיהֶם... נְתָנָן לְכֹהֵן — הֲרֵי הֵן כְּחוּלִּין לְכׇל דִּבְרֵיהֶם
The baraita Rava cites: As long as a dedicated item (cherem) sits with its owner it counts as fully consecrated (Leviticus 27:28, 'most holy to the Lord'). The moment it is handed to the priest, it becomes fully non-sacred (Numbers 18:14, 'shall be for you'). Since cherem is never in an intermediate 'redeemable' state, the phrase 'lo yigael' written about it has no local purpose — freeing it to teach, by verbal analogy, that the animal tithe's meat may not be sold.

⚖️ New Mishna: Who May Eat the Blemished Firstborn?

Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel dispute whether a non-priest — or a gentile — may join the group
Mishna
Beit Shammai: an Israelite may not be counted together with the priest to partake of a blemished firstborn. Beit Hillel permit it — even for a gentile.
Beit Shammai
Numbers 18:18, 'their flesh shall be yours,' is compared to the priestly breast and thigh: just as an Israelite may not eat those, he may not eat this.
Beit Hillel
That verse discusses only an unblemished firstborn offered on the altar. For the blemished one, Deuteronomy 15:22 says הַטָּמֵא וְהַטָּהוֹר יַחְדָּו יֹאכְלֶנּוּ — 'the impure and the pure alike may eat it.'
Beit Hillel
A fortiori: if an impure priest — barred from lesser sacrifices — may eat the blemished firstborn, surely a non-priest, who may eat lesser sacrifices, may eat it too!
Objection
That inference can be refuted: impurity's general ban is sometimes lifted (impure priests may serve when no pure ones exist); a non-priest's ban on service is never lifted.
Beit Hillel
We spoke of eating, not service — and a non-priest's eating is even better suited to compare than an impure priest's.
Rabbi Akiva's addition: The mishna's 'even a gentile' follows Rabbi Akiva, who derives it from 'like the gazelle and the deer' (Deuteronomy 15:22) — animals a gentile may freely eat.
