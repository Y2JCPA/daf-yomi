📖 Overview: Payment, Suspicion, and What's Safe to Buy

Bekhorot 29a–29b
מָה אֲנִי בְּחִנָּם, אַף אַתֶּם בְּחִנָּם
The scene: The daf opens by explaining Ila's carefully structured fee for examining firstborns at Yavne — paid regardless of the verdict, and only once per animal, precisely to remove any incentive for a dishonest ruling. A new mishna then broadens the principle: judges, witnesses, and those who handle red heifer purification water may not take payment at all, a rule traced back to Moses teaching Torah for free. The Gemara carefully distinguishes real compensation — food and drink for an impure priest, transport for an elderly examiner, pay calculated like an idle laborer — from a forbidden fee for the ruling itself. The daf then turns to a very different concern: what may safely be purchased from someone suspected of illegally selling firstborn meat, Sabbatical-year produce, or teruma disguised as ordinary food, mapping out which goods are too easily disguised to trust and which show enough invested effort to be reliably non-sacred.

⚖️ Why Judges, Witnesses, and Water-Sprinklers May Not Take Pay

New mishna, traced to Moses's own example
Mishna
One who takes payment to judge or testify — his rulings and testimony are void. One paid to sprinkle or sanctify red heifer water — the water is mere cave water, the ashes mere burnt ash.
Source
'Behold, I have taught you...as the Lord commanded me' — just as Moses taught for free, so must every generation teach Torah for free.
Refinement
If you cannot find a free teacher, you may pay to learn — but you may never then charge to teach what you learned ('buy the truth, and do not sell it').
Objection
But a mishna in Kiddushin rules a woman CAN be betrothed with the value of red heifer purification water or ashes — so they clearly do carry transactable value!
Abaye
No contradiction: that mishna concerns payment for fetching and filling the water, a legitimate service. Our mishna forbids payment for the actual sprinkling and sanctifying itself.
Confirmed
The precise wording of each mishna — 'to sprinkle and to sanctify' versus 'purification water and ashes' — supports exactly this distinction.
Bigger picture: Payment is permitted for logistics and labor, but never for the sacred act or ruling itself.

How a priest, elder, or busy professional is still made whole

If he is elderly
He is transported by donkey rather than walking

💵 In every case, he receives the rate of 'an idle laborer' — Abaye clarifies: idle specifically from HIS OWN trade, factoring in its particular difficulty and pay

🥩 Buying From Someone Suspected of Selling Firstborn Meat

New mishna: which goods are too easily disguised to trust?

Forbidden to Buy

  • Deer meat — easily confused with calf meat
  • Untanned hides — a firstborn's hide wouldn't be worth risking the effort of tanning
  • Bleached or cleaned wool

Permitted to Buy

  • Tanned hides — too much effort invested for a seller to risk it being confiscated as a firstborn's
  • Spun thread and garments (even felt) — processed far beyond raw wool

Rabbi Eliezer's Exception

  • Female-animal hides may be bought — firstborn law applies only to males, and people can visibly tell the difference
  • The first tanna disagrees: sellers could simply cut off the telltale male organ and blame mice
Underlying test: A good is trustworthy to purchase when the effort or visible evidence involved makes concealment implausible.
