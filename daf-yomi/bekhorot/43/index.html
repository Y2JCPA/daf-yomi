📖 Overview: From Animal Blemishes to Priestly Ones

Bekhorot 43a–43b
מוּמִין אֵלּוּ, בֵּין קְבוּעִין בֵּין עוֹבְרִים — פּוֹסְלִין בָּאָדָם, וְיָתֵר עֲלֵיהֶן
The scene: After a final note closing the tumtum discussion, the mishna opens a new chapter: every blemish that disqualifies an animal from the altar also disqualifies a priest from Temple service — and priests have additional disqualifying blemishes all their own, since a priest must simply look 'equal to the seed of Aaron.' The Gemara derives the overlap between human and animal blemish lists through a verbal analogy on 'garav' and 'yalefet,' then explains why the Torah needed to write the law of disqualifying blemishes three separate times — for a person, for sacrificial animals, and for a firstborn. The daf catalogues an array of head-shape and baldness blemishes unique to priests, then opens two new mishnayot defining the eyebrow blemish (gibben) and the nose blemish (ḥarum).

👰 Closing the Tumtum Sugya

לִפְסוֹל בִּמְקוֹם אַחִים... וְלַחְלוֹץ שֶׁלֹּא בִּמְקוֹם אַחִים, אִיכָּא בֵּינַיְיהוּ
The practical difference: Between Rabbi Yehuda (who treats a human tumtum as a certain eunuch) and Rabbi Yosei b'Rabbi Yehuda (who treats him as merely uncertain): if the deceased brother had other brothers, Rabbi Yehuda's view frees the widow to marry any of them without the tumtum's ḥalitza, since his ḥalitza means nothing; Rabbi Yosei's view requires it, since the tumtum might genuinely be a valid male whose ḥalitza would otherwise leave her improperly released. And if the tumtum is the ONLY brother, Rabbi Yehuda exempts her from ḥalitza entirely, while Rabbi Yosei still requires it, just in case.

🔗 New Chapter: Linking Human and Animal Blemishes

A verbal analogy imports each list's unique terms into the other
Gemara
Why does the mishna say all of an animal's disqualifying blemishes disqualify a priest too? A wart (yabelet) is written only regarding animals; a cataract (dak) and tevallul are written only regarding people!
Resolution
They're derived from each other: the word גָּרָב ('garav') appears in both lists, and so does יַלֶּפֶת ('yalefet') — a verbal analogy (גְּזֵירָה שָׁוָה) transfers each list's unique blemishes to the other.
Gemara
But is this analogy even valid, or could logic refute it — an animal is stricter since IT is sacrificed; a person is stricter since he's obligated in mitzvot?
Resolution
The terms are 'free' (superfluous) in context — since if scurvy alone sufficed to prove repulsive garav a fortiori, why repeat garav explicitly? Being free, the analogy stands unchallenged by logic.
Why not write it all just once? The school of Rabbi Yishmael: every repeated passage teaches something new — here, the blemishes unique to each side (hooves/gums for animals; hunched back/sunken nose for people).

📜 Rava's Three Necessary Verses

Why 'blemish disqualifies' had to be written separately for a person, sacrifices, and a firstborn
CaseCan't be derived from the other two because...
A person (priest)He is uniquely obligated in mitzvot generally — unlike an animal
Sacrificial animalsThey are themselves offered on the altar, and consecration is voluntary — sanctity applies to many types
A firstbornIts sanctity begins automatically from birth (from the womb), unlike animals consecrated by a person's choice
Each pairing tested and rejected: The Gemara systematically shows no single case's blemish-disqualification can be logically inferred even from the OTHER TWO combined — each has some unique stringency the others lack.

👨 Head-Shapes and Baldness That Disqualify a Priest

Blemishes unique to a person, since a priest must look 'equal to the seed of Aaron'

Head Shapes

  • Kilon: pointed, narrow above/wide below (like a barrel lid)
  • Lapton: turnip-shaped, wide above/narrow below
  • Makkavan: hammer-shaped, protruding forehead
  • Shakut/Sekifat: indented forehead / protruding back of head
  • Short or overly narrow neck

Back and Baldness

  • Hunched back WITH a bone: disqualified by all
  • Hunched back with only flesh: disputed (R' Yehuda: fit; Rabbis: not equal to Aaron's seed)
  • Kere'ach (bald): disqualified only if missing hair specifically at the BACK of the head (Rava)
Rabbi Yoḥanan's addition: Bald priests, dwarves, and those with 'leaky eyes' are disqualified by TORAH law (not equal to Aaron's seed) — against a baraita (Rabbi Yehuda's view) that would include bald priests via 'the priests' (הַכֹּהֲנִים) in Leviticus 1:8.

👁️ New Mishnayot: Gibben (Eyebrows) and Ḥarum (Nose)

Torah terms defined — and disputed
TermStandard definitionAlternate view
Gibben (Lev. 21:20)No eyebrows, or only one eyebrowR' Dosa: eyebrows so long they lie flat and cover the eyes; R' Ḥanina b. Antigonus: a crooked spine appearing as two backs
Ḥarum (Lev. 21:18)Sunken nose (can paint both eyes as one brushstroke)Also includes: retreating, blocked, or drooping nose ('or ḥarum' includes all); Rabbi Yosei's narrower view was rejected as 'going too far'
A near-fatal clarification: When Rav Shimi bar Ḥiyya challenged that 'two spines' should mean the person couldn't even survive, Rav clarified: it means a crooked spine only APPEARING doubled — a livable, disqualifying condition, not literally two spines.
