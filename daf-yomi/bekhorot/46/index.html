📖 Overview: Closing Priestly Blemishes, Opening the Laws of the Firstborn

Bekhorot 46a–46b
יֵשׁ בְּכוֹר לַנַּחֲלָה, וְאֵינוֹ בְּכוֹר לַכֹּהֵן; בְּכוֹר לִכְהֵן, וְאֵינוֹ בְּכוֹר לַנַּחֲלָה
The scene: The daf closes the long chapter on priestly blemishes with a final distinction between two kinds of correctable disqualification: a vow (for a forbidden marriage) versus a mere personal commitment (for corpse-impurity), the difference tracing to whether temptation is actively at play. A new chapter then opens with human firstborn status itself, splitting it into two independent tracks — the double portion of inheritance, and the requirement of redemption from a priest (pidyon haben) — since a child can qualify for one, both, or neither. The Gemara works through what counts as a disqualifying prior 'opening of the womb,' and wrestles at length with whether a fetus's head emerging (and retreating) counts as a birth for these purposes.

🤐 Two Paths to Correction, Closing the Blemish Chapter

הָתָם יִצְרוֹ תְּקָפוֹ
Why a vow here, but only a commitment there? A priest who married a forbidden woman must take a formal VOW to derive no benefit from her — because his desire (יֵצֶר) actively pulls him toward staying with her, requiring the extra force of a vow to ensure compliance. A priest exposed to corpse-impurity, by contrast, need only ACCEPT UPON HIMSELF a commitment to avoid it going forward — no comparable temptation is at work. Ameimar adds: a vow taken 'with the public's awareness' cannot be dissolved at all for an optional matter, though it can be for a matter of mitzva (as when Ravina reinstated a strict children's teacher whose skill outweighed the public vow against him).

👶 New Mishna: Four Types of Firstborn Status

Inheritance and priestly redemption are independent tracks
CategoryExample
Firstborn for inheritance, NOT for redemptionBorn after a stillbirth/miscarriage that already 'opened the womb' (disputed definition — see next slide)
Firstborn for redemption, NOT for inheritanceSon of a father who already had other sons — he 'opens the womb' but isn't legally his father's firstborn heir
Firstborn for BOTHThe straightforward case: first child born to a father who had no prior sons, from a mother who never gave birth before
Firstborn for NEITHERVarious uncertain-parentage cases: e.g., a widow remarrying within 3 months and giving birth, unclear whose child it is
Rabbi Yosei HaGelili's addition: A son born to a woman who conceived as a maidservant/gentile but gave birth after conversion/emancipation IS a firstborn for both purposes — since 'a womb opened in Israel' (Exodus 13:2) is satisfied once she is part of the Jewish people at the birth itself.

🤰 What Prior Birth Disqualifies the Next Son From Redemption?

Rabbi Meir and the Rabbis disagree on how developed the earlier 'opening' must be
Rabbi Meir
ANY prior emergence counts — even an underdeveloped fetus (nefel) whose head merely emerged alive, or something with only an ANIMAL's appearance.
The Rabbis
Not so — it only counts if that prior birth had the FORM OF A PERSON. A sandal-shaped mass, an afterbirth alone, or an unformed sac doesn't disqualify the next son from redemption.
Both agree
Either way, the SUBSEQUENT son remains a firstborn for INHERITANCE regardless — since the disputed cases only affect the priestly-redemption requirement.
The stakes: Whether a father must redeem his son from a priest (pidyon haben) hinges entirely on whether an EARLIER pregnancy already counted as 'opening the womb.'

👤 Does an Emerged-Then-Retracted Head Count as Birth?

Shmuel's distinction meets a conclusive refutation
1️⃣ Shmuel: for a NON-viable fetus (nefel), a head that emerges and retracts does NOT count as birth — based on Genesis 7:22, 'breath of life in its nostrils' only applies to a viable being
↓
2️⃣ Objection: the mishna itself says a nefel whose head emerged ALIVE still exempts the next child — implying head-emergence DOES count even for a nefel!
↓
3️⃣ Attempted rescue: reinterpret 'head' in the mishna to mean 'head AND MOST of the body' — a much larger threshold than mere head-emergence
↓
4️⃣ Final blow: but a separate mishna (Nidda) explicitly defines birth as occurring once 'most of the head' (from the forehead) emerges — directly matching the original reading and confirming Shmuel's view is refuted. תְּיוּבְתָּא — a conclusive refutation!
Why work so hard to save Shmuel's view? The Gemara tries multiple readings before conceding defeat — a reminder that Amoraic rulings are defended vigorously before being overturned.

👁️ Forehead Alone: How Much Recognition Is Enough?

Reish Lakish and Rabbi Yoḥanan disagree about inheritance specifically
Reish Lakish
A forehead's emergence counts as 'birth' everywhere — EXCEPT for inheritance, since Deuteronomy 21:17's 'he shall recognize' requires true facial recognition, which a bare forehead doesn't provide.
Rabbi Yoḥanan
Even for inheritance, forehead-emergence suffices.
Objection
But baraitot define 'recognition of the face' as requiring the face WITH THE NOSE — for both firstborn status and testimony permitting a widow to remarry!
Resolution
Read those baraitot as 'up to, but not including, the nose' — and testimony for remarriage is intentionally held to a STRICTER standard than ordinary birth-order questions, so it can't prove the general rule.
Underlying value: The Sages permit lenient hearsay for the END of testimony about a husband's death (even from a slave or the wife herself), but never relax the BEGINNING — genuine, careful identification of the person.
