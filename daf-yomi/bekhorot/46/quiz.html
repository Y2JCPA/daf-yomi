QUESTION 1 OF 5
Why does the mishna require a full VOW from a priest who married a forbidden woman, but only a personal commitment from one exposed to corpse-impurity?
A. There is no real difference between the two cases
B. Because his desire (yetzer) actively draws him to remain with the woman, requiring the stronger force of a vow; no comparable temptation exists regarding corpse-impurity
C. Because marriage is a more severe sin than impurity
D. Because only priests who are married require vows
Correct: B — The Gemara explains that active temptation ('his desire seizes him') justifies requiring the stronger commitment of a formal vow specifically in the marriage case.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
According to the new mishna, is it possible for a child to be a firstborn for inheritance purposes but NOT require redemption from a priest?
A. No, the two are always identical
B. Yes — for example, a son born after a prior stillbirth/miscarriage that already 'opened the womb,' disqualifying him from redemption but not from inheritance rights
C. Only daughters can have this status
D. This only applies to firstborn animals, not people
Correct: B — The mishna explicitly establishes four categories, including firstborn-for-inheritance-only, illustrated by cases where an earlier pregnancy already satisfies the 'opening of the womb' requirement for priestly redemption purposes.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
What is the core dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Rabbis regarding what prior birth exempts a later son from priestly redemption?
A. They agree completely on this issue
B. Rabbi Meir says any prior emergence (even with an animal's form) counts; the Rabbis require the prior birth to have had the form of an actual person
C. Rabbi Meir requires a fully developed baby; the Rabbis accept any tissue at all
D. The dispute only concerns twins
Correct: B — Rabbi Meir is lenient, counting even an animal-shaped prior emergence as 'opening the womb'; the Rabbis require that the earlier birth have had a human form to count.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
How does the Gemara ultimately resolve the challenge to Shmuel's ruling that a retracted head doesn't count as birth for a nonviable fetus?
A. It fully upholds Shmuel's ruling without difficulty
B. After attempting to reinterpret the mishna's language, the Gemara concludes with a conclusive refutation (teyuvta) of Shmuel's position, based on a parallel mishna in Nidda defining birth as emergence of most of the head/forehead
C. It says the question remains permanently unresolved (teiku)
D. It rules that heads never count as birth under any circumstances
Correct: B — After trying to reinterpret 'head' as 'head and most of the body,' the Gemara cites a Nidda mishna that clearly defines birth via forehead-emergence, delivering a definitive refutation of Shmuel's view.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
What is the key difference between Reish Lakish's and Rabbi Yoḥanan's positions on whether a forehead's emergence alone determines firstborn status for inheritance?
A. They fully agree with each other
B. Reish Lakish holds forehead-emergence counts everywhere except for inheritance (which requires fuller facial recognition per Deut. 21:17); Rabbi Yoḥanan holds it counts even for inheritance
C. Rabbi Yoḥanan says forehead-emergence never counts for anything
D. The dispute concerns only animals, not people
Correct: B — Reish Lakish limits the sufficiency of mere forehead-emergence, excluding inheritance since 'he shall recognize' demands real facial recognition; Rabbi Yoḥanan extends the leniency to inheritance as well.
