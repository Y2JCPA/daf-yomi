📖 Overview: Feeding, Skinning, and Saving a Blemished Firstborn

Bekhorot 33a–33b
בְּכוֹר שֶׁאֲחָזוֹ דָּם, אֲפִילּוּ מֵת — אֵין מַקִּיזִין לוֹ דָּם, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי יְהוּדָה
The scene: The Gemara continues analyzing Beit Hillel's ruling that non-priests, and even gentiles, may share a blemished firstborn — tracing Rabbi Akiva's source to the phrase 'like the gazelle and the deer,' and extending the discussion to whether a menstruating woman may eat it. A related baraita then bars skinning a firstborn or other disqualified consecrated animal 'from the feet,' a technique that keeps the hide intact but is judged undignified for sacred meat. The daf's new mishna asks a sharper question: if a firstborn becomes dangerously congested with blood, may one let its blood to save it, even though the incision itself may cause a permanent blemish? Rabbi Yehuda, the Rabbis, and Rabbi Shimon each draw the line differently, and the Gemara compares their dispute to an unrelated case of doubtfully-impure teruma.

🦌 Why Rabbi Akiva Includes the Gentile

Deuteronomy 15:22 compares disqualified sacrifices to undomesticated game
Gemara
What is Rabbi Akiva's reason for saying even a gentile may eat a blemished firstborn?
Rabbi Akiva
The verse says כַּצְּבִי וְכָאַיָּל — 'like the gazelle and like the deer.' Just as a gentile may eat a gazelle or deer, he may eat a disqualified consecrated animal.
Gemara
But the phrase 'gazelle and deer' appears three times regarding disqualified offerings (12:15, 12:22, 15:22) — what do the other two teach?
Answer
One teaches Rabbi Yitzḥak and Rabbi Oshaya's rulings; the third teaches that just as a gazelle and deer are exempt from having their firstborn count as bekhor, so too disqualified consecrated animals are exempt from that status.
Underlying logic: Once an animal is disqualified from the altar, the Torah repeatedly likens it to ordinary wild game — open to anyone, priest or not, Jew or gentile.

🩸 May a Menstruating Woman Eat the Blemished Firstborn?

Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel argue from the same 'breast and thigh' comparison as before
❓ Beit Shammai forbid feeding it to a niddah; Beit Hillel permit
↓
📜 Beit Shammai: 'their flesh shall be yours' (Num. 18:18) likened to the priestly breast/thigh, which a niddah may not eat
↓
✅ Beit Hillel: that verse is only about an unblemished firstborn; for the blemished one, 'the impure and pure alike may eat it' (Deut. 15:22)
↓
🔀 Beit Shammai's limit: that leniency covers only impurity from an outside source (e.g. a corpse), not impurity from the body itself, like a niddah — proven from the Paschal offering, where only 'impure through a corpse' is included
↓
↩️ Beit Hillel reply: the Paschal verse specifies 'impure through a corpse'; here the Torah writes 'impure' with no qualifier — no distinction applies
Bottom line: Beit Hillel's plain reading of the unqualified word 'impure' wins out — every type of impurity is included.

🐄 Skinning a Firstborn 'From the Feet'

A baraita bans this hide-preserving technique — but why, and according to whom?
CaseRulingAttributed to / Reason
Any animal on a FestivalMay not skin from the feetIt is unnecessary extra effort not needed for the Festival
A firstborn (bekhor)May not skin from the feet, even on a weekday, even if blemishedRav Ḥisda: follows Beit Shammai — a blemished firstborn keeps its sanctity, so it must be handled as sacrificial meat
Other disqualified consecrated animalsMay not skin from the feetRav Ḥisda: follows Rabbi Elazar b'Rabbi Shimon, who holds their sanctity survives slaughter
Other reasons offered: Rav Mari: the gain to the hide is offset by damage to the meat. Ravina (in the West): it looks like performing sacrificial labor. Rabbi Yosei bar Avin: a decree lest people raise flocks while waiting to sell the hides.

🩹 New Mishna: Bloodletting to Save a Congested Firstborn

וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: יַקִּיז, וּבִלְבַד שֶׁלֹּא יַעֲשֶׂה בּוֹ מוּם... רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: יַקִּיז, אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁהוּא עוֹשֶׂה בּוֹ מוּם
Three positions: Rabbi Yehuda: even if the firstborn will die, one may never let its blood, since the incision might cause a blemish. The Rabbis: one may let blood only in a spot that will not cause a permanent blemish; if it does cause one, the animal may not later be slaughtered on the strength of that particular blemish. Rabbi Shimon: one may let blood even where it will cause a blemish, and the animal may then be slaughtered on that very blemish.

🔗 Rabbi Elazar's Parallel to the Barrel of Teruma

The Gemara maps the bloodletting dispute onto an unrelated mishna about doubtfully-impure oil — then tests the fit
1️⃣ Terumot mishna: a barrel of teruma with doubtful impurity — Rabbi Eliezer says safeguard it; Rabbi Yehoshua says no need; Rabban Gamliel says leave it untouched
↓
2️⃣ Proposed match: Rabbi Meir (blood only where no blemish results) ~ Rabbi Eliezer; the Rabbis (blood even where it causes a blemish) ~ Rabbi Yehoshua; Rabbi Yehuda (never let blood) ~ Rabban Gamliel
↓
3️⃣ Gemara objects, repeatedly: each sage might rule differently here versus there — direct action versus indirect cause, certain death versus possible salvation (Elijah may yet purify the barrel)
↓
4️⃣ Final answer: the two disputes rest on entirely different verses, so no firm parallel can be drawn at all — though 'all agree' on separate points, e.g. that repeated leavening of a meal offering or repeated castration each carries its own liability
Takeaway: A tempting structural parallel between two unrelated disputes can still fail once each sage's true reasoning is examined case by case.
