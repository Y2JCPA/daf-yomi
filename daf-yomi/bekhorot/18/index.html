📖 Overview: Twin Firstborns, Priestly Gifts, and a New Mishna

Bekhorot 18a–18b
שְׁתֵּי רְחֵילָיו שֶׁלֹּא בִּיכְּרוּ וְיָלְדוּ שְׁנֵי זְכָרִים — שְׁנֵיהֶם לַכֹּהֵן
The scene: The daf opens by closing the question left hanging on 17b: does a baraita about a corpse found equidistant between two towns prove that human measurements can be exact? After testing and rejecting several readings, the Gemara concludes the Rabbis hold that even matters in human hands cannot coincide with true precision. It then returns to the twin-firstborn mishna, with Rabbi Yoḥanan clarifying that Rabbi Akiva's 'they assess between them' actually means the priest takes the leaner animal, and a dense set of rulings works out exactly when the owner must give the priest gifts of the priesthood from the uncertain second lamb. Two parables — sheep deposited with a shared shepherd versus with a private homeowner — explain why Rabbi Tarfon and Rabbi Akiva disagree only in one specific scenario. The daf closes with a new mishna cataloguing every combination of two ewes giving birth together.

⚖️ אפשר לצמצם: The Rabbis' Final Position

Continuing from 17b: can two towns be truly equidistant from a corpse?
1️⃣ Suggestion: the baraita's first tanna (no heifer brought at all) disputes Rabbi Eliezer (two heifers brought) over whether precise measurement is possible
↓
2️⃣ Rejected: if the first tanna truly held precision impossible, he should still bring one heifer in partnership — not refuse to bring any!
↓
3️⃣ Real dispute: both tannaim agree precision is possible; they disagree only whether the singular 'קְרוֹבָה' (nearest) excludes multiple equally-near towns
↓
4️⃣ So what do the actual Rabbis (who oppose Rabbi Eliezer) hold? A baraita reports: they rule to bring one heifer in partnership
↓
5️⃣ Conclusion: had they held precision possible with 'קרובה' including multiple, they'd bring two; had they excluded multiple, they'd bring none. Bringing exactly one proves they hold precision impossible even in human hands
Result: This settles the question raised on 17b about whether two events — including simultaneous births — can ever truly coincide.

🐑 'They Assess Between Them' — What Did Rabbi Akiva Mean?

Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba questions Rabbi Yoḥanan's reading
Mishna
Rabbi Tarfon: the priest chooses the better of the two lambs — presumed that the healthier one emerged first.
R' Yoḥanan
But Rabbi Akiva's 'they assess [מְשַׁמְּנִין] between them' actually means the priest takes the leaner of the two.
R' Ḥiyya b. Abba
Didn't we learn 'they assess the value between them' — meaning they divide the value?
R' Yoḥanan
While you were eating dates in Babylonia, we in Eretz Yisrael explained it from the mishna's latter clause.
Proof
If one lamb dies: Rabbi Tarfon says divide the survivor; Rabbi Akiva says the owner keeps it (burden of proof on the claimant). If 'assess' meant splitting value, that case should split too — it doesn't!
Conclusion
Rather, 'מְשַׁמְּנִין' means the extra fat [שׁוּמָּן] of the better lamb is disputed: the owner tells the priest, 'bring proof it is the firstborn, and take it.'
Result: Rabbi Akiva's ruling gives the leaner lamb outright to the priest, while the better one stays with the owner unless the priest can prove his claim.

🎁 Priestly Gifts on the Uncertain Second Lamb

When must the owner give the foreleg, jaw, and maw from the grazing lamb he eventually slaughters?
RulingPositionReasoning
Rabbi MeirObligates the giftsThe priest 'comes at him from two sides': if it is the firstborn, it's entirely his; if not, at least give the gifts
Rabbi YoseiExempts — only where a replacement lamb is already in the priest's possessionThe Sages treat one who never actually acquired it as though he did — the priest 'sold' it back to the owner
Rabbi Elazar's caveatEveryone agrees the owner is obligatedWhen no replacement ever reached the priest at all (e.g. a male and female twin) — Rabbi Yosei's reasoning doesn't apply
Rav Pappa's caveatEveryone agrees the owner is exemptFor an animal of uncertain tithe status — even Rabbi Meir agrees, since there the priest never 'comes at him from two sides'
Key phrase: חֲלִיפִין בְּיַד כֹּהֵן — 'a replacement in the priest's possession' — is the hinge Rabbi Yosei's entire exemption turns on.

🤝 Two Parables Explain Tarfon and Akiva

Rabbi Ḥiyya: their real dispute is narrower than it first appears

Two Depositors, One Shepherd

  • Two people each deposit a sheep with a shepherd; one animal dies, unclear whose
  • Everyone agrees: the shepherd places the survivor between them and withdraws — they divide its value
  • Maps to Rabbi Tarfon's case: the owner's courtyard is partly transferred to the priest, since he wants the mitzva performed

One Depositor, a Homeowner

  • One person deposits a sheep with a homeowner who has flocks of his own; unclear whose animal died
  • Everyone agrees: the burden of proof rests on the claimant — the depositor
  • Maps to Rabbi Akiva's case: the owner never transfers courtyard rights, since it could cost him money

Where They Truly Disagree

  • Only about a birth in the owner's own courtyard, with a priest as the usual recipient
  • Rabbi Tarfon: the owner effectively 'deposits' with the priest by wanting the mitzva done there
  • Rabbi Akiva: since it could cost the owner, he transfers nothing — it stays purely his courtyard
Retraction: Rabbi Ami reports that Rabbi Tarfon actually retracted his 'priest chooses the better one' ruling and holds, like the parable, that they always divide the value — the mishna's text was simply never updated.

📋 Two Ewes Give Birth: The New Mishna's Cases

Every combination of two never-yet-given-birth ewes bearing together
CaseRuling
Both ewes bear malesBoth lambs go to the priest — each is its own mother's firstborn
One bears a male, one a femaleThe male goes to the priest
Two males and one female (three offspring)One male kept by owner, one given to priest — subject to the Tarfon/Akiva dispute
Two females and a male, or two males and two femalesThe priest gets nothing — perhaps both ewes bore females first
One ewe had given birth before, one had not, and both bear malesOne to owner, one to priest — same Tarfon/Akiva dispute and Rabbi Yosei's gifts exemption apply
Gemara's question: Why does the mishna repeat the Tarfon/Akiva dispute across three near-identical cases? Because in each, one might have thought Rabbi Akiva would concede to Rabbi Tarfon that the presumed-better lamb came from a particular mother.
