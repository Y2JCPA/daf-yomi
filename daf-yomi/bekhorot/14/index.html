📖 Overview: When Did the Blemish Come First?

Bekhorot 14a–14b
שֶׁלֹּא יְהֵא טָפֵל חָמוּר מִן הָעִיקָּר
The scene: A new mishna draws a sharp line between two kinds of blemished consecrated animals: those blemished before consecration, which never carry inherent sanctity and are only sanctified in value, and those blemished after consecration, which remain fully sanctified despite the blemish. The Gemara traces the far-reaching consequences of that distinction — for firstborn status, priestly gifts, shearing and labor, offspring, substitutes, and burial after death — and along the way works through a five-candidate chain of possibilities for a single verse in Deuteronomy before settling on its true subject: the prohibition against sacrificing a blemished animal on a private altar.

🐑 Two Categories of Once-Blemished Consecrated Animals

The order of blemish and consecration changes everything

Blemish Preceded Consecration

  • Only value is sanctified, never the body itself
  • After redemption: subject to firstborn law and priestly gifts; may be sheared and worked
  • Offspring conceived before redemption: permitted
  • Slaughtered outside the Temple: no liability; creates no substitute
  • If it dies: may still be redeemed and fed to dogs

Consecration Preceded Blemish

  • The animal itself was once fully, inherently sanctified
  • Even after redemption: exempt from firstborn law and priestly gifts; may not be sheared or worked
  • Offspring conceived before redemption: forbidden
  • Slaughtered outside the Temple: liable for karet; does create a substitute
  • If it dies: must be buried, not redeemed

📜 Chasing a Verse Through Five Candidates

What does 'you shall not sacrifice a blemished ox or sheep' (Deuteronomy 17:1) really teach?
1️⃣ Rabbi Elazar: it teaches that slaughtering a blemished animal on a permitted private altar violates a prohibition
↓
2️⃣ Why not apply it to a firstborn instead? Rejected — a firstborn's blemish-disqualification is already explicit elsewhere (Deuteronomy 15:21)
↓
3️⃣ Or to animal tithe? Rejected — already derived by verbal analogy ('passing') from the firstborn's own rule
↓
4️⃣ Or to a substitute (temurah) animal? Rejected — already derived from 'it and its substitute' (Leviticus 27:10)
↓
5️⃣ Rabbi Zeira: apply it to the offspring of consecrated animals instead! Rava counters: the school of Rabbi Yishmael already derives that from a separate verse — confirming the verse means the private altar after all

🐣 Can a Newborn Be Redeemed While Still Unblemished?

A baraita's answer, and its logical limit
❓ The offspring of a blemished, value-consecrated mother (conceived before her redemption) — must it wait for its own blemish before redemption, like offspring of an inherently sanctified mother?
↓
📜 Baraita: one who dedicates permanently blemished animals for the altar — if they give birth, the offspring may be sold and redeemed without needing a blemish
↓
✅ Reasoning: 'the subsidiary should not be more severe than the primary' — the mother herself never carried inherent sanctity, so neither can her offspring
↓
⚖️ Contrast: if one deliberately consecrates an unblemished male animal 'for its value,' Rava rules it DOES receive full inherent sanctity — there is no subsidiary relationship limiting it

⚰️ If a Consecrated Animal Dies Before Redemption

Does 'standing and valuation' apply to every consecrated animal?
R' Shimon
Animals consecrated for Temple maintenance (like a red heifer) were never subject to 'standing and valuation' — so even after death, they may still be redeemed and fed to dogs. The same applies to any animal blemished from the outset.
Source
Proven from the doubled word 'אוֹתָהּ / אוֹתָהּ' (Leviticus 27:11–12), which excludes an animal blemished from the start from that requirement.
School of Levi
Disagree — all consecrated animals, even undomesticated ones or birds that could never be sacrificed, were included in 'standing and valuation.' Once dead, they must be buried.
Gemara
But then what do they do with that same exclusionary word 'אוֹתָהּ'? The Gemara concedes this reading is difficult for the school of Levi's position, and leaves it open.

🔥 When Does an Animal 'Make' a Substitute?

Only what was itself sanctified can pass sanctity onward
Original animal's statusCreates a valid substitute (temurah)?
Good and unblemished from the start, later disqualifiedYes — 'he shall not exchange a good for a bad' implies a good original animal does create sanctity in its substitute
Blemished from the very outset (never inherently sanctified)No — since the animal itself never carried inherent sanctity, nothing transfers to a proposed substitute
The daf's throughline: What an animal never itself acquired, it cannot pass on — not to its offspring, and not to its substitute.
