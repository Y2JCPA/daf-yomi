📖 Overview: Layers of Trust in Certifying a Blemish

Bekhorot 36a–36b
נֶאֱמָן הַכֹּהֵן לוֹמַר: הֶרְאֵיתִי בְּכוֹר זֶה וּבַעַל מוּם הוּא
The scene: Building on the question of whose testimony is trusted about a firstborn's blemish, the Gemara asks whether hearsay evidence suffices, tells the dramatic story of Rabban Gamliel publicly overruling Rabbi Yehoshua on a credibility question, and examines exactly which of a priest's claims are believed and which are suspect. A mishna then broadens the picture: unlike priests regarding a firstborn, everyone — even the owner — is trusted about a blemish on an animal tithe offering, since he could have achieved the same result legitimately. The daf closes with a new mishna asking whether three ordinary laymen, or only a trained expert, may certify that a blemish is severe enough to permit slaughter.

🗣️ Is Hearsay Testimony Valid for a Firstborn?

Rav Asi and Rav Ashi disagree over 'a witness in the name of a witness'
Rav Asi
Hearsay testimony (עֵד מִפִּי עֵד) may not permit a blemished firstborn's slaughter — a baraita limits such testimony to a woman testifying her husband died.
Rav Ashi
Emend the baraita: hearsay is valid for any matter where a woman's own testimony would be valid — and a firstborn's blemish is exactly such a matter.
Rav Yeimar
Ruled leniently in practice — earning the nickname from Mareimar, 'Yeimar who permits firstborns.'
Halakha: The Gemara concludes that hearsay testimony is indeed valid for certifying a firstborn's blemish.

⚖️ The Public Dispute: Did We Distinguish Scholar from Ignoramus?

Rabbi Tzadok's split lip becomes a test of rabbinic authority itself
1️⃣ Rabbi Tzadok's firstborn split its lip while eating barley. He asks Rabbi Yehoshua: was it ruled that a learned priest (חָבֵר) is trusted about his own blemish, unlike an ignoramus? Rabbi Yehoshua says yes
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Tzadok then asks Rabban Gamliel the same question — Rabban Gamliel says no such distinction was made, contradicting what Rabbi Yehoshua said
↓
3️⃣ Before the assembled scholars, Rabban Gamliel confronts Rabbi Yehoshua directly, demanding he stand and be held to his earlier ruling
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Yehoshua concedes: 'Now that I am alive and he is alive, how can the living contradict the living?' He stands in submission until the crowd, offended by Rabban Gamliel's treatment of him, stops the lecture
Underlying question: Whether a learned priest's greater credibility about his own firstborn's blemish is an established halakha or not — a dispute that spilled into a test of communal authority.

🐑 Which of the Priest's Claims Are Believed?

The mishna and Rav's extension, tested against real cases
ClaimRulingReasoning
"I showed it to an expert; he ruled it blemished"Believed (mishna)He would never risk eating sacrificial meat outside the Temple without real proof — karet is at stake
"An Israelite gave me this firstborn already blemished"Believed (Rav) — halakha follows this viewAny claim easily checked against the named Israelite tends not to be a lie
Rafram's storyA priest lied with this very claim, but Rafram (the actual giver) recognized the animal and exposed himThe Gemara concludes this was one impudent exception, not grounds to distrust all priests generally
Clear-cut exception: For an obvious, natural-looking blemish (like one eye larger than the other), Rav Ashi rules there is no concern at all — priest or Israelite, the blemish speaks for itself.

🌾 New Mishna: Everyone Is Trusted About the Tithe

הַכֹּל נֶאֱמָנִין עַל מוּמֵי מַעֲשֵׂר
Why the tithe is different: Unlike a firstborn, where a priest benefits from a blemish and is therefore suspect, even the owner of an animal tithe offering is believed when he reports its blemish — because he could have legitimately caused a blemish in his entire flock before the tithing process began. Since 'He shall not distinguish between good and bad' (Leviticus 27:33) bars picking out the tenth animal in advance, there is no special incentive to lie about any one animal specifically.

👥 New Mishna: Who May Certify a Clear-Cut Blemish?

A blind eye, severed leg, or broken leg — obvious blemishes, but who may rule on them?
❓ A firstborn's eye is blinded, foreleg severed, or hind leg broken — clearly permanent blemishes. Who may permit its slaughter?
↓
First tanna
Three ordinary synagogue-goers suffice for such clear-cut blemishes
Rabbi Yosei
Even a court of twenty-three is not enough — only a true expert may rule
↓
🌍 Extended outside Eretz Yisrael: three laymen may permit a firstborn there too — but only for clear-cut blemishes (Rava)
↓
📍 Further refined: three laymen suffice specifically where no expert is available; where an expert IS present, his ruling is required
Parallel drawn: Rav Ḥiyya bar Amram compares this to dissolving a vow: three laymen suffice where no qualified Sage is present.
