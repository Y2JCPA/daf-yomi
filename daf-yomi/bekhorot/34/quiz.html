QUESTION 1 OF 5
Why is placing dough on a firstborn's ear so a dog bites it off also forbidden, according to the Gemara?
A. It is not forbidden, since the owner did not act directly
B. The verse 'there shall not be any blemish in it' includes blemishes caused indirectly, not just directly
C. Dogs are inherently forbidden near consecrated animals
D. Only priests may cause blemishes
Correct: B — The word 'any' (כׇּל) in 'there shall not be any blemish in it' is read to include indirect causation of a blemish, not only a direct act.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
What is the basis of the dispute between Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua over safeguarding a barrel of doubtful teruma?
A. Whether teruma may ever be eaten by non-priests
B. Whether 'My terumot' (Numbers 18:8) refers to two types of teruma, including doubtful teruma, or only to teruma that is definitely pure and thus 'fit for you'
C. Whether the barrel belongs to the priest or the farmer
D. Whether teruma requires a blessing before eating
Correct: B — Rabbi Eliezer reads the plural as including doubtful teruma (since Elijah may yet purify it); Rabbi Yehoshua limits the safeguarding requirement to teruma definitely fit to eat.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
In the new mishna, what does Rabbi Eliezer rule about a firstborn whose ear was deliberately slit?
A. It may be slaughtered immediately
B. It may never be slaughtered, even if another blemish later develops naturally
C. It must be given to a gentile
D. It becomes permanently non-sacred
Correct: B — Rabbi Eliezer imposes a permanent penalty: the animal may never be slaughtered on account of any blemish, even one that develops naturally afterward.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
How do Rabba and Rav Yosef explain why Rabbi Eliezer's penalty for blemishing a firstborn is permanent, while his penalty for excising a leprous mark is limited?
A. There is no real difference between the two rulings
B. A penalty on property (like a firstborn) must be severe to prevent abuse, while a penalty on the body (like leprosy) does not need to be, since no one wants to remain sick
C. Leprosy is a more severe sin than blemishing an offering
D. Rabbi Eliezer changed his opinion over time
Correct: B — Since a person suffers no personal harm from blemishing his firstborn (only benefiting financially), the deterrent must be permanent; but no one seeks to remain leprous, so a lesser deterrent suffices there.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
How does the Gemara resolve Rabbi Yirmeya's dilemma about whether the ear-slitting penalty passes to the transgressor's son after his death?
A. It remains permanently unresolved (teiku)
B. By analogy to a Sabbatical Year law: one who unlawfully improves (fertilizes) a field and dies — his son may still sow it — showing the penalty binds only the transgressor, not his heir
C. By ruling that the son is always penalized like his father
D. By comparing it only to the Canaanite slave case, concluding the son is penalized
Correct: B — The Sheviit case demonstrates that such penalties are personal to the transgressor: the son of one who unlawfully improved his field may sow it, so the son of one who slit the ear likely is not bound by the penalty either.
