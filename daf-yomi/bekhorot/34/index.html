📖 Overview: Causing and Penalizing a Blemish

Bekhorot 34a–34b
הַצּוֹרֵם אוֹזֶן בַּבְּכוֹר — הֲרֵי זֶה לֹא יִשְׁחוֹט עוֹלָמִית, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר. וַחֲכָמִים אוֹמְרִים: נוֹלַד בּוֹ מוּם אַחֵר — יִשְׁחוֹט עָלָיו
The scene: The Gemara first closes out the previous discussion, extending the ban on blemishing a firstborn to indirect methods, and using the same tannaitic dispute to explain a parallel disagreement about safeguarding doubtfully-impure teruma. A new mishna then asks: if someone deliberately slits a firstborn's ear to disqualify it, may the animal ever be slaughtered? Rabbi Eliezer says never — even if a genuine blemish later appears naturally — while the Rabbis allow slaughter once an unrelated blemish develops. The Gemara compares this penalty to a similar one in the laws of leprosy, works out a subtle textual question about a bridegroom, and finally asks whether the penalty on a firstborn passes down to the transgressor's son after his death.

🐕 Even an Indirect Blemish Is Forbidden

מִנַּיִן שֶׁלֹּא יָבִיא דְּבֵילָה וּבָצֵק וְיַנִּיחֶנָּה עַל הָאוֹזֶן כְּדֵי שֶׁיָּבֹא כֶּלֶב וְיֹאכְלֶנָּה? תַּלְמוּד לוֹמַר ״כׇּל מוּם״
The question: From where do we know one may not place pressed figs or dough on a firstborn's ear so that a dog comes and bites it off, causing a blemish without the owner's own hand doing the deed? The verse's phrase 'there shall not be any blemish in it' (Leviticus 22:21) is read to include a blemish caused indirectly, not only one caused directly.

📜 'My Terumot' — One Word, Two Readings

The same textual method resolves the earlier dispute about the barrel of doubtful teruma
Verse
Numbers 18:8: 'And I, behold, I have given you the charge of מִשְׁמֶרֶת תְּרוּמֹתָי — My terumot' — written in the plural.
R' Eliezer
Two terumot are meant: one definitely pure, one in doubt (teruma teluya). Both must be safeguarded from impurity.
R' Yehoshua
Read it as terumati, 'My teruma' — singular. Only teruma fit for you, i.e. definitely pure, need be guarded.
Gemara
Does Rabbi Eliezer really rely on pronunciation over spelling? A baraita on בְּבִגְדוֹ בָּהּ shows the opposite: there, Rabbi Akiva reads by pronunciation, Rabbi Eliezer by spelling!
Resolution
Not a contradiction — the real dispute here is different: R' Yehoshua says safeguard only what is 'fit for you' (definitely pure); R' Eliezer says doubtful teruma is also 'fit', since Elijah may yet come and declare it pure.
Takeaway: The dispute is resolved not by a rule about vocalization, but by what counts as teruma 'fit for you' to safeguard.

✂️ New Mishna: Slitting a Firstborn's Ear

How permanent is the penalty for deliberately causing a blemish?
❓ One deliberately slits (צורם) a firstborn's ear to disqualify it. May it ever be slaughtered?
↓
Rabbi Eliezer
Never — the animal may never be slaughtered, even if a genuine new blemish later develops naturally
The Rabbis
Once another, unrelated blemish develops on its own, the animal may be slaughtered on account of that one
↓
🤔 Objection: does Rabbi Eliezer really impose penalties forever? In the laws of leprosy (נגעים) he limits the penalty for one who excised a leprous mark to heal only once a new mark appears
↓
✅ Rabba and Rav Yosef: a penalty on property (the firstborn) must be indefinite, or people would blemish freely; a penalty on the body (leprosy) needs no such severity, since no one wants to be sick again
Rava's follow-up: The Rabbis' own two rulings also seemed to conflict (limited for bechor, stricter for leprosy) — resolved the same way: in each case the Sages penalize precisely the means the transgressor used to try to profit.

👰 One Letter, One Practical Difference

לִכְשֶׁיִּוָּלֵד לוֹ נֶגַע אַחֵר יִטְהַר הֵימֶנּוּ / וְיִטְהָר
Rav Pappa's dilemma: Did Rabbi Eliezer's ruling on the excised leprous mark say yit'har (he becomes pure immediately once the new mark appears) or veyit'har (he stays impure from the old mark until the new mark is itself confirmed pure)? The practical difference: a bridegroom is granted seven days of wedding celebrations before any new mark is examined. If 'yit'har' is correct, he is fully pure the whole week; if 'veyit'har,' he remains impure from the old mark throughout — even though the new mark isn't checked yet. The Gemara leaves the question unresolved: תֵּיקוּ.

👨‍👦 Does the Penalty Reach the Son?

Rabbi Yirmeya asks Rabbi Zeira: if the father dies, is the son bound too?
1️⃣ Case 1 for comparison: one who sells his Canaanite slave to gentiles and dies — the son IS penalized (must redeem the slave), since every day the slave is denied mitzvot
↓
2️⃣ Case 2 for comparison: one who intends prohibited labor on Chol HaMoed but dies before doing it — the son is NOT penalized, since no prohibited act was ever performed
↓
3️⃣ Neither case matches cleanly: does the penalty attach to the man himself (who is now gone) or to his property (the animal, still owned by his heirs)?
↓
4️⃣ Resolution from Shevi'it: a field improved (fertilized) unlawfully during the Sabbatical Year may not be sown afterward — but if the improver dies, his son may sow it. The penalty binds only the transgressor, not his heir
Conclusion: By analogy, the son of one who slit the firstborn's ear is not bound by his late father's penalty.

↓
📜 'My terumot' (plural/singular) settles whether doubtfully-impure teruma must be safeguarded
↓
✂️ R' Eliezer: slitting the ear bars slaughter forever
✂️ Rabbis: a later, natural blemish permits slaughter
↓
👨‍👦 The penalty attaches to the transgressor's property, not to his son as heir
↓
🎓 Key Principle: penalties track intent and means — property penalties must be severe enough to deter, but they end with the transgressor himself
