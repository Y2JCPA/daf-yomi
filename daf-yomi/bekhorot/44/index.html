📖 Overview: Eyes, Proportion, and the Limits of 'Blemish'

Bekhorot 44a–44b
כֹּל מֵחֲמַת כַּהְיוּתָא — אָתְיָא מֵ״אִישׁ״, מַחְסוֹרַיְיתָא — מִ״דַּק״, מְבַלְבְּלִיתָא — מִ״תְּבַלּוּל״, מְשַׁנְּיוּתָא — מִ״בְּעֵינוֹ״
The scene: The Gemara works out, word by careful word, exactly which eye conditions Leviticus 21:18–20 means to disqualify — total blindness, impaired vision, mixed coloring, or simple deviation from the norm — with Rava showing that each of four terms was individually necessary. A new mishna lists disqualifying disproportions of eyes, body, nose, and ears, prompting a story about how tall Moses really was. A tangent on the danger of holding back urination follows, before the Gemara turns to animal characteristics that only LOOK like blemishes (a hornless goat, a horned ewe) but are not, and closes with three sages offering three different readings of the obscure phrase mero'aḥ ashekh.

👁️ Rava's Four-Layer Derivation of Eye Blemishes

Each term in the verse rules out a narrower interpretation of the last
📖 Leviticus 21:18–20 lists: 'man,' 'blind,' 'cataract (dak),' 'tevallul,' 'in his eye' — why all five terms?
↓
1️⃣ 'Blind' alone → might mean only TOTAL blindness (eyes removed). 'Man' (אִישׁ) is added to include pale spots/constant tears, which impair sight but leave eyes in place
↓
2️⃣ 'Man' alone → might mean only total inability to see. 'Cataract (dak)' is added to include merely impaired vision
↓
3️⃣ 'Dak' alone → might mean only vision-impairing conditions. 'Tevallul' is added to include colors simply MIXED, without impairing sight
↓
4️⃣ 'Tevallul' alone → might mean only mixing. 'In his eye' is added to include mere deviation from normal appearance (unusual size or position)
Rava's summary: Dimness ← 'man'; impaired vision ← 'dak'; mixed coloring ← 'tevallul'; deviation ← 'in his eye' — four distinct categories, four distinct textual sources.

📏 New Mishna: Disproportion Disqualifies

Eyes, body, nose, and ears must all be in normal proportion
BlemishDefinition
Eyes too large or smallLarge like a calf's, small like a goose's
Body disproportionate to limbsToo large or too small relative to the rest
Nose disproportionateBy the measure of a small finger's width, per the baraita
Tzome'a / Tzomem (ears)Ears too small / ears shaped like a sponge
Lips protruding, teeth fallen outLip overlap is a full blemish; missing teeth disqualify due to appearance only
Moses' height resolved: Rav said Moses was 'ten cubits tall' (from the Tabernacle's dimensions) — but when challenged that this would make him disproportionate and thus 'blemished,' Rav clarified he meant cubits of the SANCTUARY's measure, not Moses' own forearm; his limbs remained fully proportional.

🚽 A Digression: The Danger of Holding It In

Torah scholars may urinate in public rather than risk their health
1️⃣ Rabbi Abba: scholars may urinate in public (holding back causes harm) but may not drink water in public (a matter of dignity, not danger)
↓
2️⃣ Shmuel's father offers him 400 dinars to publicly retract a lenient ruling on privacy — reasoning that important people CAN arrange privacy, but ordinary people who can't shouldn't be told to endanger themselves waiting for it
↓
3️⃣ Mar bar Rav Ashi, mid-bridge, urinates despite his mother-in-law approaching: 'I would do this even in her ear' rather than risk harm
↓
4️⃣ Reish Lakish and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel: holding back urine causes jaundice; holding back blood, semen, or feces causes their own respective ailments — all stemming from one verse about avoiding barrenness (Deuteronomy 7:14)
Why it's here: This tangent explains the mishna's disqualification of a priest with a swollen belly — sometimes caused by exactly this kind of retention.

🐐 Not Every Unusual Feature Is a Real Blemish

Some animal traits look irregular but don't disqualify — at least not fully
FeatureStatus
A female goat with no hornsNot a true blemish — fit for the altar, though not for a firstborn's outside slaughter
A female lamb with hornsSame — an unusual trait, not a disqualifying blemish
Horns removed WITH their boneUnfit for the altar, but NOT redeemable (not a full blemish) — unless the bone was truly uprooted, not merely cut
Hooves removed WITH their boneUnfit AND redeemable — a genuine full blemish
Cutting only the horn's outer sheath (not the bone)No blemish at all — as with a red heifer's black-tipped horns, which may be trimmed
Underlying distinction: The difference between disqualifying an animal from the altar and creating a REDEEMABLE blemish hinges on whether the damage reaches the bone itself, and whether that bone is merely cut or fully uprooted.

⚕️ Mero'aḥ Ashekh: Three Readings of One Obscure Term

Each sage's definition is driven by a close textual difficulty with the term itself
Mishna
No testicles, or only one, is the מְרוֹחַ אָשֶׁךְ of the Torah (Lev. 21:20).
R' Yishmael
It means crushed testicles — because if it meant 'lacking,' the Torah would say חֲסַר אָשֶׁךְ, not this term.
R' Akiva
It means testicles swollen with 'wind' — because if it meant 'crushed,' the Torah would say מִמְרוֹחַ אָשֶׁךְ, a different grammatical form.
R' Ḥanina b. Antigonus
Neither — it means one whose complexion is unusually dark! By rearranging the letters (מְרוֹחַ אָשֶׁךְ → מַרְאָיו חֲשׁוּכִין), since Sages may add, subtract, and interpret letters homiletically.
A challenge resolved: Isn't 'dark complexion' the same as the mishna's separate disqualification for extremely dark skin? No — R' Ḥanina ben Antigonus derives dark complexion specifically from THIS term; the other mishna is a separate, independent teaching.
