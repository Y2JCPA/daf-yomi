📖 Overview: Closing the Firstborn Donkey Dispute

Bekhorot 10a–10b
וְעָרַפְתּוֹ... נֶאֱמַר כָּאן עֲרִיפָה וְנֶאֱמַר לְהַלָּן עֲרִיפָה
The scene: The daf continues probing whether an item's prohibition alone can render it 'food' susceptible to impurity, using a crow slaughtered only for practice as a test case. It then works to prove, through several independent routes, that even Rabbi Shimon — who generally permits benefit from an unredeemed firstborn donkey — concedes that once its neck is broken, all benefit is forbidden. A close reading of one baraita distinguishes benefit from the donkey's value from benefit from its body, before the daf turns to a betrothal puzzle and closes with the mechanics of redemption: how soon, and for how little, a firstborn donkey may be redeemed.

🐦 Does a Forbidden Item Count as 'Food'?

A crow slaughtered only to practice slaughtering
Nimos
One who slaughters a crow just to practice — its blood still renders other foods susceptible to impurity, but the crow itself needs separate intent to count as food.
R' Eliezer
No — slaughter-blood always renders food susceptible, and even the crow itself needs no extra intent. Its very prohibition is its intent.
Gemara
Does this prove the general rule 'its prohibition is its intent'? Maybe not — perhaps Rabbi Eliezer's leniency is special to a crow, which has some kosher-bird features.
Rava
Resolved: everyone agrees we do not say 'its prohibition is its intent.' The real issue is whether kosher-like features alone create food-status.

⚰️ Rava's Proof: Even Rabbi Shimon Forbids Benefit After the Neck Is Broken

An argument from a baraita's silence
❓ Rabbi Shimon generally treats forbidden-benefit items as not susceptible to food-impurity — shouldn't a neck-broken donkey be exempt too?
↓
📜 A baraita lists Rabbi Shimon's exceptions — orla, diverse kinds, a stoned ox, an eglah arufah, a metzora's birds, meat-in-milk — but a broken-necked donkey is not among them
↓
✅ Silence is proof: if benefit were still permitted, the baraita would list the donkey too. Since it doesn't, Rabbi Shimon must hold benefit is forbidden at that stage
↓
🔁 Rav Naḥman independently confirms this: 'breaking the neck' (עֲרִיפָה) here matches the same word used for the eglah arufah, whose carcass is forbidden

📜 One Baraita, Two Kinds of Benefit

Why the dispute is stated twice, for two different forms of benefit
ClauseWhat's disputed
First clause: if not redeemed, its neck is broken and it's buried; 'forbidden benefit' (R' Yehuda) vs. permitted (R' Shimon)Benefit from the donkey's monetary value — renting or selling it — while alive
Second clause: one may not kill it any other way; shearing and labor forbidden (R' Yehuda) vs. permitted (R' Shimon)Benefit from the animal itself — shearing, labor — while alive
Why state both? Each ruling alone might be mistaken as the case where the other sage concedes — stating both closes that gap and confirms the dispute spans both kinds of benefit.

🐴 A Betrothal Puzzle

Which opinion does 'she is not betrothed' follow?
Gemara
A mishna rules: one who betroths a woman with a firstborn donkey — she is not betrothed, since benefit from it is forbidden. Does the mishna reject Rabbi Shimon entirely?
Rav Naḥman
No — it's speaking about after the neck is broken, where even Rabbi Shimon agrees benefit is forbidden.
Alt. view
But maybe it follows neither sage as stated: if Rabbi Shimon, she should be betrothed with the full value; if Rabbi Yehuda, with the difference between the donkey's and the lamb's value.
Rabba bar Avuh
Resolved: it follows Rabbi Yehuda, in a case where the donkey is worth only a shekel — exactly the lamb's minimum value, leaving no difference to betroth with at all.

💰 Redeeming Immediately, For Any Amount — Or Not Less Than a Shekel?

Two readings of the redemption verse
1️⃣ The doubled verb 'תפדה...תפדה' (you shall redeem) teaches two things: redeem the donkey immediately after birth, and redeem it for any amount, however small
↓
2️⃣ Why not learn instead from a firstborn son — redeemed after 30 days, for a fixed 5 shekels? The doubled verb overrides that comparison entirely
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Yosei bar Yehuda disagrees: no redemption is valid for less than a shekel's worth, from 'all your valuations shall be by the sacred shekel' (Leviticus 27:25)
↓
4️⃣ This shekel-minimum applies even though Rabbi Yosei bar Yehuda does not compare the donkey's redemption to the human firstborn's five-shekel price
