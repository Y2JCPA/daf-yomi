📖 Overview: Real Blemishes, Temporary Ones, and Animals Barred Outright

Bekhorot 41a–41b
וְאֵלּוּ שֶׁאֵין שׁוֹחֲטִין עֲלֵיהֶן, לֹא בַּמִּקְדָּשׁ וְלֹא בַּמְּדִינָה
The scene: A summarizing mishna confirms that every blemish taught in the preceding chapter — including the disputed opinions of Ila and Rabbi Ḥanina ben Antigonus — is accepted halakha, permitting both slaughter of a firstborn and redemption of other disqualified consecrated animals. The Gemara then turns to the opposite list: conditions that do NOT count as blemishes at all, like temporary pale spots, certain boils, old age, sickness, and foul odor, alongside animals disqualified from the altar entirely because of a transgression involving them. The daf closes with the complex case of a tumtum (indeterminate sex) and an androginos (both sexes) — animals whose firstborn status itself is thrown into question.

These conditions permit slaughter neither in the Temple nor anywhere else — the animal simply isn't fit at all

Why repeat the permitting list? The Gemara explains the earlier summarizing mishna was needed specifically to confirm the DISPUTED opinions (Ila's additions, R' Ḥanina ben Antigonus) as accepted halakha, not just the unanimous ones.

🩹 Garav and Ḥazazit: Three Kinds of Boils

גָּרָב — זֶה הַחֶרֶס, יַלֶּפֶת — זוֹ חֲזָזִית הַמִּצְרִית
Resolving an apparent contradiction: The Torah itself lists garav and yalefet (ḥazazit) among disqualifying blemishes (Leviticus 22:22) — so why does the mishna say they DON'T permit outside slaughter? The Gemara distinguishes three types: (1) the Torah's garav — dry inside and out, incurable, a true blemish; (2) the mishna's garav — moist inside and out, curable, not a permanent blemish; (3) the garav of Egypt (Exodus 9:10) — dry inside, moist outside, also incurable. Similarly, ḥazazit in the Torah refers specifically to the Egyptian plague-boil; the mishna's ḥazazit is an ordinary, curable boil.

🐂 Disqualified From the Altar by the Word 'From'

Leviticus repeats 'min' (from) to exclude several categories entirely — not because of a physical blemish
Excluded animalDerived fromWhy exclusion needed
Old animal"From the flock" (Lev. 1:10)Won't become young again — needed lest one think only illness disqualifies
Sick animal"From the sheep"Could recover — needed lest one think only permanent conditions disqualify
Foul-smelling animal"From the goats"Not weak, just repulsive — needed as a separate exclusion
Animal used in bestiality (either role)"From the cattle" (Lev. 1:2)Though liable to be killed by court, this teaches disqualification even absent two witnesses
Animal worshipped as, or set aside for, idolatry; one that gored a person to death"From the herd"/"or from the flock"Same principle — disqualified on testimony of even one witness or the owner
Why the repetition matters: Each case needs its own exclusion because none can be logically derived from another — a sick animal might recover, an old one is merely natural, a smelly one isn't necessarily weak.

⚥ Is a Hermaphrodite's Extra Organ a Blemish?

Rabbi Shimon vs. the Rabbis — and a deeper question about whether 'cracked' applies to flesh
Mishna
One may not slaughter a tumtum or androginos (hermaphrodite) anywhere — Rabbi Shimon dissents: 'You have no greater blemish than that!' The Rabbis: it isn't even a firstborn at all — just a regular animal.
Gemara
Why not treat the female organ as a 'crack' (חָרוּץ), a Torah-listed blemish (Lev. 22:22)?
Abaye
'Cracked' is grouped with 'broken' — just as a break requires a BONE, so does a crack. The female organ isn't near bone, so it doesn't count.
Rava
Even without that comparison: since garav (a fleshy boil) needed its own verse to be EXCLUDED, that proves a crack in mere flesh (which would otherwise seem worse) is NOT a blemish on its own.
Rabbi Yishmael
Rejects both — I hold a visible crack, even in flesh, counts as 'an ill blemish' (Deut. 15:21).
Halakha: The Rabbis' view prevails — a hermaphrodite has no firstborn status at all, so the question of its 'blemish' is moot.

🤔 Was Rabbi Yishmael Even Certain?

Rava probes what Rabbi Yishmael actually meant — with real practical stakes
1️⃣ Rava's dilemma: did Rabbi Yishmael mean 'it's definitely a firstborn male, with its blemish,' or merely 'IF it's a firstborn, then it comes with its blemish' — leaving the sex itself uncertain?
↓
2️⃣ Practical difference: if definitely a firstborn, one is flogged for shearing or working it; if merely uncertain, no flogging applies
↓
3️⃣ Gemara tries to prove certainty from Rabbi Ilai's report ('a hermaphrodite IS a firstborn, with its blemish') — but this too could be read as conditional
↓
4️⃣ Rav Ḥisda's distinction: the dispute is only about an androginos (definitely a mixed, separate category, per the Rabbis); a tumtum, by contrast, is agreed by all to be a genuine doubt — definitely male OR female — and therefore consecrated 'due to the doubt' itself
Rava's challenge to Rav Ḥisda: If a tumtum is a certain doubt between two fixed categories, shouldn't its valuation (in vows) simply follow whichever value applies? The question is left for further analysis.
