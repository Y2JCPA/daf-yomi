📖 Overview: Nursing, Mercy, and a New Mishna on Firstborns

Bekhorot 24a–24b
דָּבָר בְּחֶזְקָתוֹ
The scene: The daf probes exactly what Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel meant when he said a nursing animal's status 'stands in its presumptive status' — does an animal simply refuse to nurse at all unless it has given birth, or does it refuse to nurse anyone else's young? Three attempts to settle the question from the mishna's own wording are each proposed and dismantled. The stakes surface vividly in Rabbi Yoḥanan's case of a pig-like creature trailing after a ewe, which is simultaneously exempted from firstborn law and forbidden to eat — a combination the Gemara must reconcile, ultimately leaving a related dilemma unresolved. The daf then turns to a new mishna: Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam's method for clearing hair from a firstborn's neck before slaughter without violating the Torah's ban on shearing it, and the parallel question of whether the same technique is permitted on a Festival.

🐑 Does a Mother Nurse Only Her Own Young?

Unpacking Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's ambiguous phrase
❓ 'The matter stands in its presumptive status' — presumptive of what, exactly?
↓
Reading A
An animal won't nurse at all unless it gave birth — but a mother who gave birth MIGHT still nurse another's young
Reading B
An animal nurses only its OWN young — nursing itself proves certain maternity
↓
⚖️ The stakes: only Reading B makes slaughtering mother and nursling together a certain violation of 'itself and its offspring' (אוֹתוֹ וְאֶת בְּנוֹ), punishable by lashes
↓
🔍 Three attempts to settle the question from the mishna's own wording — each is proposed and rejected
Left open: The Gemara moves on to a new case without ever settling which reading is correct.

🔍 Three Failed Proofs

Testing whether the mishna's wording reveals RSbG's true position
Proof AttemptWhy It Fails
The opening ruling: a buyer needn't worry the nursling is another's calfDoesn't prove certain maternity — either way, the future offspring isn't a firstborn, since nursing at all proves a prior birth
The flock scenario: no concern that first-time and repeat mothers' young were swappedEven granting that cross-mother mercy is possible in general, a mother with her own young present wouldn't abandon it for another's
'And similarly' (וְכֵן) linking the mishna's two clausesThe clauses describe different scenarios — one with all offspring physically present (certain), one with a single purchased nurser (uncertain); the link refers only to their shared exemption from firstborn status
Pattern: Each proof looks compelling until the Gemara finds an alternative reading that removes its force.

🐷 A Pig-Like Creature Trailing a Ewe

Rabbi Yoḥanan's ruling combines two seemingly opposite conclusions

Exempt From Firstborn Status

  • Follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel: an animal nurses only its own young
  • So the ewe must have already given birth — its next offspring is not a firstborn

Forbidden to Eat

  • Follows the Rabbis: an animal might nurse young that isn't its own — even across species
  • So the pig-like creature might genuinely be a pig — forbidden 'until Elijah comes and resolves it'

The Resolution

  • Rabbi Yoḥanan is certain the halakha follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel throughout the Mishna
  • What's uncertain: does RSbG's 'mercy' apply only within a species, or across species too?
  • Aḥai the Distinguished frames the dilemma on both fronts — firstborn status AND permissibility — and it is left standing, unresolved (תֵּיקוּ)
Why a pig, not a lamb? A same-species case (ewe nursing a lamb) couldn't test whether mercy crosses species lines — only the pig case can.

✂️ Plucking Hair Before Slaughtering a Firstborn

New mishna: Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam solves a conflict between two mitzvot
DetailRuling
The problemThe Torah forbids shearing a firstborn (Deuteronomy 15:19), yet the neck must be cleared of hair for proper slaughter
Rabbi Yosei's solutionClear space with a cleaver, uprooting hair from both sides of the neck
Key conditionThe plucked hair must not be moved from its place — it stays intermingled so the act doesn't look like deliberate shearing
A second useThe same plucking method lets a Sage examine the area for a blemish, to rule on slaughtering the firstborn outside the Temple
Underlying principle: An act forbidden in one form (organized shearing) can be permitted when performed differently (incidental plucking for a permitted purpose).

🎊 Rav Huna's Festival Dilemma

Does the same plucking technique apply to an ordinary animal slaughtered on a Festival?
1️⃣ Rav rules: the halakha follows Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam
↓
2️⃣ Question: may one pluck hair the same way from a non-sacred animal's neck before slaughtering it on a Festival (Yom Tov)?
↓
3️⃣ Possibility 1: if 'plucking is not shearing,' then on a Festival it's still forbidden as 'uprooting something from its growth'
↓
4️⃣ Possibility 2: if 'plucking normally IS shearing,' but permitted for the firstborn only as an unintended byproduct of preparing to slaughter, then on a Festival an unintentional act is generally permitted too
↓
5️⃣ Rav Huna defers judgment until Rav Ḥananel confirms Rav's ruling firsthand
↓
6️⃣ Once confirmed, Rav Huna rules: plucking hair before slaughtering an ordinary animal on a Festival is permitted
Method: Rav Huna refuses to resolve the Festival question until he is certain what Rav actually said — a case study in careful transmission.
