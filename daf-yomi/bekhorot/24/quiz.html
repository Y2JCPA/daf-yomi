QUESTION 1 OF 5
According to the Gemara, what is the practical difference between the two readings of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's phrase 'the matter stands in its presumptive status'?
A. Whether the offspring may ever be eaten
B. Whether one is flogged for slaughtering the mother and nursling together on the same day, under the prohibition against slaughtering an animal and its offspring
C. Whether the mother animal must be examined for blemishes
D. There is no practical difference at all
Correct: B — If an animal is merciful only to its own young, the nursling is certainly the mother's offspring, making slaughter of both on the same day a certain violation punishable by lashes; if mercy can extend to others' young, the relationship is uncertain and lashes would not apply.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
Why does the Gemara reject the proof from the mishna's opening ruling (that a buyer needn't worry the nursling is another's calf)?
A. Because the ruling doesn't actually appear in the mishna
B. Because either way — whether the nursling is its own or another's — the future offspring isn't a firstborn, since nursing at all proves a prior birth
C. Because the ruling only applies to gentile sellers
D. Because Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel later retracted this ruling
Correct: B — The Gemara explains that the mishna's ruling is true regardless of which reading is correct: an animal that nurses at all must have given birth previously, so the future offspring is not a firstborn either way — the wording doesn't prove certain maternity specifically.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
In the case of a pig-like creature trailing after a ewe, what does Rabbi Yoḥanan rule about the ewe's firstborn status and about eating the creature?
A. The ewe remains obligated in firstborn law, and the creature is permitted to eat
B. The ewe is exempt from firstborn law, and the creature is forbidden to eat until Elijah resolves the matter
C. Both rulings are left completely undetermined
D. The ewe is exempt, and the creature is definitely non-kosher with no doubt
Correct: B — Rabbi Yoḥanan rules the ewe is exempt from having a future offspring counted a firstborn (following Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel), while the pig-like creature itself is forbidden to eat pending a definitive ruling, since it might genuinely be of a forbidden species.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
According to the new mishna, what condition must be met when Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam's method is used to clear hair from a firstborn's neck before slaughter?
A. The hair must be completely removed from the animal
B. The plucked hair must not be moved from its place, remaining intermingled so it doesn't look like shearing
C. The hair may only be plucked by a priest
D. The animal must first be examined by two witnesses
Correct: B — Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam permits clearing space with a cleaver by plucking hair, provided the plucked hair is not moved away — it stays where it fell so the act does not resemble deliberate shearing, which the Torah forbids for a firstborn.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
What are the two possible reasons Rav Huna considers for Rabbi Yosei ben HaMeshullam's ruling, and what do they imply for plucking hair on a Festival?
A. Both reasons lead to the same conclusion regardless
B. If plucking is not shearing, it's still forbidden on a Festival as uprooting growth; if plucking is normally shearing but permitted here as unintentional, then on a Festival an unintentional act is generally allowed
C. The two reasons only concern the firstborn case and have no bearing on Festivals
D. Rav Huna rejects both possible reasons
Correct: B — Rav Huna considers that if plucking is fundamentally different from shearing, it should still be forbidden on a Festival as 'uprooting from growth'; but if plucking normally counts as shearing and is permitted for the firstborn only because it's unintentional, then the general Festival leniency for unintentional acts would apply.
