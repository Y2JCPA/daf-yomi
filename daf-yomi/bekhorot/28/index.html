📖 Overview: Verses, Verdicts, and a Famous Cow

Bekhorot 28a–28b
רַבִּי טַרְפוֹן, אַתָּה מוּמְחֶה לְבֵית דִּין, וְכׇל הַמּוּמְחֶה לְבֵית דִּין פָּטוּר מִלְּשַׁלֵּם
The scene: The daf closes out the derivation of the firstborn's two-day-one-night eating period with a final round of exchanges between Rav and the school of Rav, then tackles a genuine dilemma: does an owner get thirty extra days after a firstborn's first year only if the blemish appeared after the year, or also when it appeared during the year? A new mishna shifts to a slaughtered firstborn examined only after the fact, distinguishing eye blemishes (which can change after death) from body blemishes, and setting compensation for a non-expert's mistaken ruling. The daf's second mishna widens the lens to judicial error generally, exempting a recognized expert from having to pay for his mistakes — illustrated vividly by Rabbi Tarfon's famous misjudgment of a cow whose womb had been removed, and Rabbi Akiva's spirited defense of his colleague.

⏳ Thirty More Days — Extra, or Exclusive?

A genuine dilemma about the mishna's timing rules, tested and ultimately refuted
❓ If a blemish appears within the firstborn's first year, does the owner get 12 months PLUS 30 more days — or are the two timeframes mutually exclusive?
↓
🔍 A baraita: a blemish appearing on day 15 of the year lets the owner 'complete' 15 more days after the year ends — suggesting a 30-day total IS granted
↓
❔ New sub-question: are those extra days counted from the END of the year, or from the DAY the blemish first appeared?
↓
❌ Rabbi Elazar's claim — that the 30 days run from the moment the blemish appears — is directly refuted: the baraita only ever 'completes' days already owed, never grants a fresh 30
Outcome: The Gemara explicitly declares this a תְּיוּבְתָּא — a conclusive refutation of Rabbi Elazar's position.

👁️ Cornea Blemishes vs. Body Blemishes

New mishna: examining a blemish only AFTER the firstborn has already been slaughtered

Cornea Blemishes (דּוּקִּין שֶׁבָּעַיִן)

  • Everyone agrees: forbidden if examined only after slaughter
  • Such blemishes can visibly change after death, making retroactive verification impossible

Body Blemishes — the Real Dispute

  • Rabbi Meir forbids: a decree, lest people confuse them with the unreliable cornea case
  • Rabbi Yehuda permits: no such decree is needed, since body blemishes do not change after death

Not All Corneas Are Equal

  • Some cornea blemishes change after death and some do not, as Rabbi Yoshiya of Usha demonstrated firsthand
  • This matters for evaluating witnesses who testify about a blemish's appearance before death
Rabbi Meir's real motive: Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak infers from the mishna's own wording that Rabbi Meir's ruling is a penalty for skipping the expert, not genuine doubt about the blemish itself.

⚖️ Payment for a Wrongly-Slaughtered Firstborn

When a non-expert's ruling leads to an improper slaughter
DetailRuling
Non-expert's errorThe animal must be buried, and he pays the priest compensation from his own property
Payment for a small animal (sheep or goat)One-quarter of its value
Payment for a large animal (bull)One-half of its value
Rav Pappa's reasoningA large animal represents a proportionally greater loss, justifying the higher share
Rav Huna bar Manoaḥ's additionSmall-livestock owners already face a rabbinic decree restricting where they may graze their flocks, sparing the priest some effort — hence only a quarter-share is owed
Why not pay the full loss? The Gemara treats these fractions as fixed rabbinic amounts, not a strict accounting of actual damages.

🐄 Rabbi Tarfon's Cow and the Expert's Exemption

New mishna: judicial error, illustrated by a famous incident
Mishna
A cow's womb was removed. Rabbi Tarfon ruled it a tereifa; the owner fed it to dogs. The Sages of Yavne later ruled such an animal IS permitted.
Todos
The physician confirms: cows and pigs leaving Alexandria routinely have their wombs removed to prevent breeding elsewhere, yet live on normally.
R' Tarfon
'Your donkey is gone, Tarfon!' — believing he must now sell his donkey to compensate the owner.
R' Akiva
Rabbi Tarfon, you are an expert accepted by the court — and any such expert is exempt from paying for his errors!
Gemara
Why not simply say his ruling contradicted an explicit Mishna, making it automatically void — so the owner's loss was self-inflicted?
Answer
Rabbi Akiva gave two reasons: an error against an explicit Mishna is void regardless, AND even an error in judgment is covered by the court-expert exemption.
Why give two reasons? The second reason extends protection even to errors of pure legal reasoning, not just clear-cut mishnaic mistakes — a broader principle worth stating on its own.
