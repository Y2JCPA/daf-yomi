📖 Overview: Intent, Incentive, and Credibility

Bekhorot 35a–35b
זֶה הַכְּלָל: כֹּל שֶׁהוּא לְדַעְתּוֹ — אָסוּר, שֶׁלֹּא לְדַעְתּוֹ — מוּתָּר
The scene: Two mishnayot open the daf with real-world stories: a Roman official slits the ear of an aging, unslaughtered firstborn ram, and children playing in a field accidentally sever a lamb's tail. In both cases the Sages permit that first, unintended blemish — but once people start deliberately imitating it, they forbid the practice for anyone who acts 'with knowledge.' A further mishna asks about kicking an animal that is chasing you, and a side discussion allows causing a blemish on a firstborn fetus before birth. The daf closes with a major new mishna: since priests benefit financially from a firstborn's blemish, are priest-shepherds trusted to testify that a blemish occurred naturally, the way Israelite shepherds are?

🗡️ The Quaestor's Ram and the Children's Lambs

Two mishnaic stories teach the same rule from different angles
1️⃣ A Roman quaestor asks why an old, shaggy ram (a firstborn) hasn't been slaughtered; told it needs a blemish, he slits its ear himself
↓
2️⃣ The Sages permit that ram's slaughter — but once the quaestor starts slitting other firstborns' ears on purpose, they forbid those
↓
3️⃣ Separately, children tying lambs' tails together accidentally sever one tail (a firstborn's); the Sages permit it — but forbid it once people start tying tails deliberately
↓
4️⃣ Both stories are needed: the gentile case alone might not extend to a Jewish minor (who could form a habit), and the minor's case alone might not extend to a gentile adult (who could be confused with a Jewish adult)
The rule: "Whatever is done with intent (לְדַעְתּוֹ) is forbidden; whatever is done without intent is permitted" — including even indirect causation, but excluding a gentile who blemished after hearing an offhand remark, not a direct question.

🗣️ 'It Had a Blemish' vs. 'A Blemish Was Made'

Does the bystanders' phrasing to the quaestor change the halakha?
Rav Ḥisda
The ram was permitted only because they told him 'unless it has a blemish' — implying a natural one. Had they said 'unless a blemish is made on it,' that is like telling him outright: go and blemish it!
Rava
Not so — both phrasings are passive and refer to something that happens on its own. There is no difference between them; either way the animal remains permitted.
Why it matters: The exact words used to describe a blemish can be read as an invitation to cause one — or as a neutral description of a natural event.

🐏 New Mishna: Kicking a Pursuing Firstborn

If a firstborn animal chases its owner and he kicks it, causing a blemish, may he slaughter it?
ScenarioRulingReasoning
Kicked during the pursuitPermitted (both versions agree)Presumed to be self-defense, not intent to blemish
Kicked after the pursuit ends (version 1)ForbiddenRav Pappa: presumed intent to render it slaughterable, not mere anger
Kicked after the pursuit ends (version 2)PermittedRav Pappa (alt.): presumed to be delayed anger recalling the earlier distress, not intent to blemish
Underlying test: Every case in this sugya turns on the same question: was the blemish caused with intent to render the animal slaughterable, or incidentally?

🤰 Blemishing Before Birth

מוּתָּר לְהַטִּיל מוּם בִּבְכוֹר קוֹדֶם שֶׁיָּצָא לַאֲוִיר הָעוֹלָם
Rav Yehuda's ruling: It is permitted to cause a blemish in a firstborn fetus before it emerges into the air of the world, since its sanctity begins only at birth. Rava applies this practically: a kid's long ears emerge first from the birth canal, so its ear can be blemished in utero; a lamb's short ears do not, so its lip is the practical target instead (some say a lamb's ears can work too, if it emerges through its temples).

👥 New Mishna: Whose Word Can Be Trusted?

Shepherds may testify a blemish was natural — but priests have a motive to lie

Israelite Shepherds

  • Trusted to testify the blemish was not caused intentionally
  • No personal financial stake in the outcome

Priest Shepherds

  • Not trusted — they may profit if the firstborn becomes blemished and is given to them
  • Two amora'im (R' Yoḥanan/R' Elazar) dispute whether this means priests employed by an Israelite, or Israelites employed by a priest

Two Refinements

  • Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel: a priest IS trusted about another priest's firstborn, just not his own
  • Rabbi Meir: anyone suspect on this matter may not judge or testify at all, even for someone else
Halakha: The Gemara ultimately rules like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel — a priest's own son or daughter may testify for him, but not his wife, 'since a wife is like himself.'
