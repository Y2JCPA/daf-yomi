⏰ Reconciling 'Immediately' and 'After Thirty Days' — Round Two

A contradiction between baraitot, resolved twice over
1️⃣ Rav Sheshet: 'immediate' means one may redeem right away and it's valid; 'after thirty days' means only then does failing to redeem become an active transgression
↓
2️⃣ Rami bar Ḥama objects: a baraita says 'the mitzva applies throughout the thirty days; from then on, EITHER redeem it OR break its neck' — doesn't this mean the ideal is to wait the full thirty days?
↓
3️⃣ Reinterpreted: no, it means there is a mitzva to redeem it at any point during the thirty days, and only afterward does failing to redeem become a transgression
↓
4️⃣ Rava's cleaner resolution: the two baraitot are simply two opinions — Rabbi Eliezer compares donkey-redemption to a firstborn SON's (wait until 30 days); the Rabbis reject that comparison and require immediate redemption

📜 When One Mitzva Takes Precedence — Closing the First Chapter

A mishna listing which of two options comes first
Competing mitzvotWhich comes first, and why
Redeeming a firstborn donkey vs. breaking its neckRedemption first — 'if you will not redeem it, THEN you shall break its neck' (Exodus 13:13)
Designating a Hebrew maidservant vs. redeeming her with moneyDesignation first — 'who has not betrothed her...then he shall let her be redeemed' (Exodus 21:8)
Levirate marriage (yibum) vs. ḥalitzaOriginally yibum first (Deuteronomy 25:7) — but once performed without pure motive, the Sages reversed it: ḥalitza now comes first
An owner redeeming his own consecrated non-kosher animal vs. anyone else redeeming itThe owner first — 'and if it is not redeemed, it shall be sold' (Leviticus 27:27) implies he gets first opportunity

🐄 Why Donkey Before Cow?

A new mishna opens chapter two with the same structure, now for a cow
Gemara
The new mishna repeats the same five gentile-partnership cases, now for a cow's fetus (inherent sanctity, offered on the altar) instead of a donkey's (mere value-sanctity). Shouldn't the more significant case have been taught first?
Answer 1
Donkeys are dear to the tanna — recall Rabbi Ḥanina's teaching that donkeys assisted Israel at the Exodus, carrying Egypt's silver and gold.
Answer 2
Or: the laws of a non-kosher animal are relatively few, so the tanna completes that smaller topic before moving to the larger one.
New ruling
The new mishna also adds: priests and Levites are obligated for a firstborn kosher animal — unlike their exemption from a firstborn son or firstborn donkey.

💰 Acquiring an Animal From a Gentile: Money Alone Suffices

Reish Lakish's ruling, in the name of Rabbi Oshaya
❓ A Jew who pays a gentile for an animal 'according to their laws' — is the sale complete with MONEY ALONE, without pulling? This decides whether the firstborn offspring is obligated or exempt.
↓
🤔 First guess: like acquiring a gentile SLAVE, where money alone works — rejected, since even between two Jews, a slave's body is bought with money but PROPERTY still needs pulling
↓
✅ Abaye's source: 'buy of your counterpart's hand' (Leviticus 25:14) — from a fellow Jew you need PULLING; by implication, from a gentile's hand, money ALONE suffices
↓
🔄 The reverse holds too: a gentile who pays a Jew for an animal likewise acquires it with money alone, exempting its firstborn

🗿 The Idol Test Case: Four Ways to Read One Baraita

Scrap metal from gentiles conceals an idol — when was it acquired?

Abaye

  • Pulled before paying (returnable): a genuine mistaken sale — the buyer never wanted an idol
  • Pulled after paying (must discard): not mistaken — he should have inspected before pulling once he'd already paid

Rava

  • Both clauses are mistaken sales
  • The real difference is optics: before payment, an idol doesn't yet 'look like' it's the Jew's; after payment, it does

Rav Ashi / Ravina

  • Disagree about which clause is the anomaly
  • Rav Ashi: neither clause shows pulling as effective
  • Ravina: both do — the first clause is reread as merely retracting a verbal promise
