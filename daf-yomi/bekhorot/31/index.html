📖 Overview: Trust Regained, and a New Chapter on Disqualified Offerings

Bekhorot 31a–31b
שׁוּבוּ בָּנִים שׁוֹבָבִים
The scene: The daf closes the extended discussion of chaver status by asking whether someone who abandoned those commitments can ever be trusted again — with the halakha ultimately following the most forgiving view, illustrated vividly by Rav Huna bar Ḥiyya's brief stint as a tax collector. A baraita then draws a sharp line between the one case where self-interest disqualifies a person from ruling on his own property — examining his own firstborn animal for a blemish — and several cases where it does not, because he has no real incentive to lie. A new chapter opens with a mishna distinguishing how disqualified consecrated animals are sold: most go to the public market to maximize the Temple's profit, but a firstborn or tithe offering may only be sold quietly in the owner's home, since that profit is his alone. The daf closes with a careful account of when a tithe animal's meat may legally be sold at all, softened for orphans by the principle of returning a lost item.

🔄 Can a Backslid Chaver Return?

Three positions on someone who abandoned their commitment to chaver status
R' Meir
If any of them retract, they are never accepted back as a chaver — ever.
R' Yehuda
If they retracted only privately, while still keeping up appearances in public, they are not trusted. But if they retracted openly, they may be accepted back.
Alt. Version
Some report it reversed: accepted if they kept the practice privately even before retracting; not accepted if it was only ever a public performance.
R' Shimon & R' Yehoshua
Either way, they ARE accepted back — 'Return, you backsliding children; I will heal your backslidings' (Jeremiah 3:22).
Halakha
Rabbi Yoḥanan rules in accordance with this final, most lenient pair — a retracted chaver may always return.
Underlying value: A biblical verse about repentance ultimately tips the scale toward welcoming people back rather than permanently excluding them.

💰 Rav Huna bar Ḥiyya's Chairs

A vivid illustration of the retraction dispute in practice
1️⃣ The Sages needed Rav Huna bar Ḥiyya for a matter; Rabba, Rav Yosef, and four hundred pairs of scholars set out to see him
↓
2️⃣ Hearing they were coming, he had four hundred chairs prepared for his guests
↓
3️⃣ Word then reached the Sages that he had become a tax collector; they sent a message: 'Go to your important position — we no longer need you'
↓
4️⃣ He replied: 'I have already repented and resigned'
↓
5️⃣ Rav Yosef still refused to visit, following Rabbi Meir's strict view; Rabba went to him anyway, following the later, more lenient ruling
Real consequences: A halakhic dispute about trust translated directly into whether two leading scholars would even set foot in a colleague's home.

👁️ What You May Never Examine for Yourself

A baraita distinguishes genuine conflicts of interest from apparent ones
ItemMay You Rule On Your Own?Why
Your own firstborn animal's blemishNoObvious self-interest — you would be tempted to falsely permit it in order to keep the animal
Your own animal dedicated to the TempleYesIf he regrets the dedication, he can simply have a Sage annul the vow — no incentive to lie about a blemish instead
Your own animal titheYesIf he wanted to avoid the tithe, he could simply blemish his whole herd beforehand — a false claim now gains him nothing extra
Your own food's purity statusYesThe food remains usable to him even while impure, during his own days of impurity — so there is little incentive to lie
Pattern: Self-interest disqualifies a ruling only where lying would actually gain the person something he couldn't get another, honest way.

🥩 Disqualified Consecrated Animals: Market vs. Owner's House

New mishna opens a new chapter on selling blemished sacred animals

Ordinary Disqualified Sacrifices

  • Sold and slaughtered in the public butchers' market, weighed like non-sacred meat
  • Maximizing market value matters because the profit ultimately funds the Temple treasury

Firstborn and Tithe Offerings

  • Sold only in the owner's home, by estimate rather than weight
  • The profit belongs to the owner — the priest for a firstborn, the owner himself for a tithe — not the Temple

Resolving an Apparent Contradiction

  • 'Benefit belongs to the Temple' doesn't describe profit from the later market sale
  • It refers to the ORIGINAL redemption price, which rises because buyers know they can resell profitably afterward in the market
Same principle, different owners: Wherever the profit ultimately lands determines how — and where — the animal may be sold.

📜 Selling a Tithe's Meat — Torah Law vs. Rabbinic Decree

A careful accounting of when an animal tithe offering's meat may be sold
1️⃣ Torah law (from Leviticus 27:33): an animal tithe offering cannot be sold only while ALIVE — after slaughter, selling its meat is technically permitted
↓
2️⃣ The Sages decreed against selling it even AFTER slaughter, as a precaution, lest people begin selling it BEFORE slaughter too
↓
3️⃣ Exception: an orphan's inherited tithe animal may be sold in the ordinary way outright, under the principle of returning a lost item, so its value isn't lost to spoilage
↓
4️⃣ For an adult owner's slaughtered tithe, the meat may only be sold indirectly, by inflating the price of its hide, fat, sinews, or horns to quietly cover the meat's value
↓
5️⃣ Why allow this indirect method here but not elsewhere? Because unlike a lulav's naturally fluctuating price (which can plausibly hide an etrog's value), an inflated price for hide or horns would be too obviously an artifice on its own — yet the Sages permitted it anyway for the tithe
Rava's proof: The Torah's own juxtaposition of substitution and non-redemption — both tied to when the animal is still alive — shows the sale ban is meant only for a living animal, by Torah law.
