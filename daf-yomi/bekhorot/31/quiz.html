QUESTION 1 OF 5
According to Rabbi Meir, can an am ha'aretz who retracted from chaver status ever be accepted back?
A. Yes, always, without any conditions
B. No, never — once retracted, always excluded
C. Only if he retracted in public
D. Only if three witnesses approve
Correct: B — Rabbi Meir holds the strictest position: anyone who retracts from chaver status is permanently barred from ever being accepted back, regardless of the circumstances of the retraction.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
Whose position does the halakha ultimately follow regarding a retracted chaver, and on what basis?
A. Rabbi Meir's, based on the need for strict standards
B. Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korcha's, based on the verse 'Return, you backsliding children'
C. Rabbi Yehuda's original version only
D. No halakha was ever established on this question
Correct: B — Rabbi Yitzḥak of the village of Akko, citing Rabbi Yoḥanan, rules that the halakha follows Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korcha, who accept a retracted chaver back in every case, grounded in the verse about welcoming back 'backsliding children.'
QUESTION 3 OF 5
Why is a person trusted to rule on whether his own dedicated (consecrated) animal has a blemish, but not on his own firstborn animal's blemish?
A. Because dedicated animals are never subject to blemish laws
B. Because if he regrets dedicating the animal, he can simply have a Sage annul the vow instead of falsely claiming a blemish, removing any incentive to lie
C. Because firstborns are worth less money than dedicated animals
D. Because only priests may own dedicated animals
Correct: B — The Gemara explains that a person has no real incentive to lie about a blemish on his dedicated animal, since he could achieve the same result honestly by having his vow annulled — unlike a firstborn, where falsely claiming a blemish is his only way to keep the animal for himself.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
According to the Gemara's resolution, what does the mishna's phrase 'the benefit belongs to the Temple treasury' actually refer to?
A. Profit from the animal's later sale in the public market
B. The higher price the animal fetches at its original redemption, since buyers know they can resell it profitably afterward
C. A special tax collected from butchers
D. It refers to nothing practical and is purely theoretical
Correct: B — The Gemara concludes that this phrase refers to the initial redemption price paid to the Temple treasury, which rises because the animal's resale value in the market increases what a buyer is willing to pay to redeem it in the first place.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
Why does the Torah technically permit selling a tithe animal's meat after slaughter, and why did the Sages forbid it anyway, with an exception for orphans?
A. The Torah never permits it under any circumstances
B. The Torah's ban on redemption applies only while the animal is alive; the Sages extended the ban after slaughter too, as a precaution, but allowed selling an orphan's tithe animal to prevent loss of its value
C. The Sages permitted selling all tithe animals without restriction
D. Orphans are exempt from tithe law entirely
Correct: B — Rava derives from the Torah's wording that the prohibition against selling (redeeming) a tithe animal applies only while it is alive; the Sages later extended this as a precautionary decree even after slaughter, but made an exception for an orphan's inherited tithe animal under the principle of returning a lost item, preventing its value from being lost to spoilage.
