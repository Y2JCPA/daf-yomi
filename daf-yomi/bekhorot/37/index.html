📖 Overview: Closing One Chapter, Opening the List of Blemishes

Bekhorot 37a–37b
עַל אֵלּוּ מוּמִין שׁוֹחֲטִין אֶת הַבְּכוֹר: נִפְגְּמָה אׇזְנוֹ מִן הַחַסְחוּס, אֲבָל לֹא הָעוֹר
The scene: The daf closes the chapter on disqualified consecrated animals with a mishna on selling meat that turns out to have been forbidden all along — a slaughtered firstborn never shown to a Sage, or a cow that turns out to be a tereifa — and works out exactly how much of a refund the buyer is owed. A new chapter then opens with the Torah's own list of disqualifying blemishes, beginning with the ear: torn cartilage, a split, a hole the size of a legume, or desiccation. The Gemara derives, through the exegetical method of generalization-detail-generalization (כְּלָל וּפְרָט וּכְלָל), exactly which kinds of blemishes the Torah means to include, then works out precise measurements for each one.

🤢 How Much of a Refund? Repulsive vs. Non-Repulsive

Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar qualifies the baraita
Baraita
Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar: for repulsive items (נְבֵילוֹת, טְרֵיפוֹת, שְׁקָצִים) — full refund, since one is assumed not to have benefited. For non-repulsive items (בְּכוֹרוֹת, טְבָלִים, יֵין נֶסֶךְ) — only a deduction for benefit received.
Gemara
But why deduct anything for firstborn meat? The buyer can say: 'What loss did I cause you? Without me, you had no right to eat it at all — it was unexamined and forbidden!'
Resolution
This applies specifically where the cut sold came from a spot with the blemish itself: the seller can retort, 'Had you not eaten it, I would have shown the animal to a Sage and had it permitted' — following Rabbi Yehuda, who allows examination even after slaughter.
Parallel logic: Untithed produce: 'I would have tithed it and eaten it myself.' Idolatrous wine mixed in: sellable to a gentile per Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, so the seller lost real value.

📜 Deriving the Blemishes: 'Lame, Blind... Any Ill Blemish'

The exegetical method of generalization, detail, and generalization again
❓ Deuteronomy 15:21 lists only 'lameness' and 'blindness' — how does the mishna permit slaughter for ear damage, splits, and holes too?
↓
📖 The verse actually reads: 'if there be ANY blemish... lameness or blindness... ANY ill blemish' — a generalization, then a detail, then a generalization again
↓
🔑 Rule: כְּלָל וּפְרָט וּכְלָל includes anything similar to the detail — like lameness/blindness, blemishes must be visible and non-regenerating
↓
Included
Visible, permanent blemishes — even ones that don't stop the animal from working, since 'any ill blemish' amplifies further
Excluded
Hidden blemishes (need visibility) and temporary blemishes (logic: if even redemption isn't allowed for a temporary blemish, certainly slaughter isn't)
Key proof text: A baraita on Leviticus 27:11 shows that 'of which they may not bring an offering' teaches that only animals never fit for the altar (not temporarily unfit) may be redeemed — the same logic bars slaughtering a firstborn for a merely temporary blemish.

📏 Pinning Down the Size of a Karshina

The Gemara cross-checks its measurements against unrelated impurity laws
1️⃣ Rav Hoshaya asks Rav Huna the Great: does 'the size of a karshina' mean a vetch that fits snugly, or one that can pass in and out of the hole?
↓
2️⃣ Answer, by analogy from a similar measure: it means a hole large enough for the item to enter and exit freely
↓
3️⃣ Elsewhere, Rav Ḥisda asks about the size of a skull-fragment deficiency (from the laws of corpse impurity) — told it is 'like a sela coin' — and objects that this collapses Beit Shammai's and Beit Hillel's measures into one
↓
4️⃣ The comparison method itself is the point: precise halakhic measurements are pinned down by cross-referencing known measures from elsewhere in the Torah's system
Takeaway: Even a small technical term like 'karshina' gets careful, cross-referenced definition, since the exact size determines whether a firstborn may be slaughtered.
