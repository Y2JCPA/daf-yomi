📖 Overview: Signs, Minorities, and a Goat's Triplets

Bekhorot 20a–20b
עֵז שֶׁטִּינְּפָה בַּת שִׁשָּׁה — יוֹלֶדֶת בְּתוֹךְ שְׁנָתָהּ
The scene: The daf continues analyzing when the offspring of a gentile-purchased animal is certainly, or only uncertainly, a firstborn. Rava and Ravina each explain why Rabbi Yishmael's age-based rule doesn't simply follow the statistical majority of animals that give birth within a year. Rabbi Yehoshua then corrects Rabbi Yishmael in the name of the Rabbis: an unseen murky discharge (טִינּוּף) can also indicate a prior pregnancy, and the Gemara works through three separate technical explanations — involving Ze'eiri's thirty-day rule, premature birth, and whether part of a day counts as a full day — for why Rabbi Yehoshua's own position is more lenient than the view he transmits. A parallel dispute follows over whether producing milk alone, without a discharge, exempts the next offspring, complete with a baraita that reverses who says what. The daf closes with a puzzle about a kid and her triplet daughters reaching the animal-tithe quota, revealing along the way that an animal does not give birth twice within a single year.

⚖️ Which Majority Counts?

Why doesn't Rabbi Yishmael's rule simply follow 'most animals give birth within a year'?

Rava's Explanation

  • Rabbi Yishmael follows Rabbi Meir's principle: one must be concerned for the minority (חוֹשְׁשִׁין לְמִיעוּטָא)
  • So the statistical majority of animals that give birth within a year does not remove the doubt

Ravina's Explanation

  • Even the Rabbis, who normally do follow a majority, agree here
  • This majority is action-dependent (תָּלוּי בְּמַעֲשֶׂה) — whether the animal mated at all — not a fixed feature of reality
  • An action-dependent majority is not followed, unlike an examinable majority such as nine kosher shops
Shared conclusion: Both explanations preserve Rabbi Yishmael's uncertainty rule without forcing him to adopt Rabbi Meir's general concern for minorities.

🐐 Does a Hidden Discharge Still Exempt the Offspring?

Rabbi Yehoshua, in the name of the Rabbis, corrects Rabbi Yishmael's age-based certainty
❓ A murky discharge (טִינּוּף) is a sign of a completed pregnancy. But what if no one actually saw one?
↓
✅ Everyone agrees: if a discharge is seen, it exempts the next offspring from firstborn status
↓
Rabbi Yishmael
Not concerned about an unseen discharge — assume none occurred, so the age-based rule stands
Rabbi Yehoshua
Concerned even for an unseen discharge — the next offspring remains uncertain
↓
🔑 Rava's resolution: Rabbi Yishmael is concerned for the minority — but only when it leads to a stringency (giving to the priest), never to a leniency (freeing the owner)
Alternative: Or they disagree over whether a discharging animal can return to fertility and give birth within the same year at all.

📅 Three Ways to Explain Rabbi Yehoshua's Own Leniency

Both his transmitted 'Rabbis' opinion and his own view agree a discharge doesn't rule out a same-year birth — so what's the practical difference?
1️⃣ Case: a goat discharges at six months, then gives birth before its first year ends
↓
2️⃣ Answer 1 — Ze'eiri's rule: a real discharge blocks pregnancy for 30 days. The transmitted 'Rabbis' view accepts this (so a birth within a month proves it wasn't a real discharge); Rabbi Yehoshua's own view rejects Ze'eiri entirely
↓
3️⃣ Answer 2 — Can an animal give birth prematurely (מְקוּטָּעִין)? The transmitted view says no; Rabbi Yehoshua's own view says yes, allowing a shorter pregnancy after the discharge
↓
4️⃣ Answer 3 — Is part of a day counted as a full day (מִקְצָת הַיּוֹם כְּכוּלּוֹ)? Rabbi Yehoshua's own view says yes, letting pregnancy begin on day 30; the transmitted view says no
Why three answers? Each 'if you wish, say instead' (וְאִיבָּעֵית אֵימָא) offers an independent way to locate the real practical gap — the Gemara leaves all three live.

🥛 Does Milk Alone Exempt the Next Offspring?

Rabbi Ḥanina of Sura finds a practical difference between Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Yehoshua — with a twist
R' Ḥanina
There is a difference: an animal that already produces milk, with no discharge seen at all.
R' Akiva
Milk exempts the offspring — follow the majority of animals, and most do not produce milk unless they have given birth.
R' Yehoshua
A minority of animals do produce milk without having given birth — so the next offspring stays uncertain.
Gemara
But does Rabbi Yehoshua really worry about a minority? A mishna rules a widow need not worry her late mother-in-law bore a levir, even though a minority of pregnancies could yield one!
Correction
Rather, reverse the two opinions. A baraita confirms it: Rabbi Yehoshua says milk does exempt; Rabbi Akiva says it does not.
Lesson: Attributions transmitted through intermediate reports can be reversed once a direct baraita is found — the Gemara trusts the more direct source.

🐑 A Kid, Her Daughters, and the Tithe Pen

A baraita's numbers reveal an unstated halakha
Detail of the BaraitaExplanation
A kid bears triplet daughters, and each daughter also bears tripletsMother + 3 daughters + 9 granddaughters = 13 animals, all entering the pen to be tithed within one year
Why does every daughter bear exactly three, not a mix of two and three?One daughter alone cannot reach the ten-animal quota with the mother unless she bears three — the baraita states three for all, for consistency
Why not instead have the daughters bear two each, and let the original mother give birth again alongside them?That route is never proposed — implying the halakha that a goat does not give birth twice within the same year
Rabbi Shimon's addendumHe personally saw a single kid whose descendants reached the tithe quota within its very first year
Hidden principle: The baraita's silence about a repeat birth from the same mother is itself evidence that a goat cannot give birth a second time within one year.
