📖 Overview: What Counts as Valid Redemption

Bekhorot 51a–51b
אֵין פּוֹדִין לֹא בַּעֲבָדִים, וְלֹא בִּשְׁטָרוֹת, וְלֹא בְּקַרְקָעוֹת, וְלֹא בְּהֶקְדֵּשׁוֹת
The scene: After a brief real-world coda about the striking-fine (Ḥanan the wicked), the Gemara turns to a new mishna: a firstborn son cannot be redeemed with slaves, promissory notes, land, or consecrated items — only with money or movable items of real value. Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi permits everything except promissory notes, and the Gemara traces this to competing exegetical methods, amplification-and-restriction versus generalization-and-detail, applied to the same verses. The daf closes with the mishna's rulings that a father who merely wrote a promissory note has not redeemed his son, though the priest may return the sum as a gift, and that a father who designates money for redemption but loses it before paying remains financially responsible.

🥊 A Real Case: Ḥanan the Wicked

חָנָן בִּישָׁא תְּקַע לֵיהּ לְהָהוּא גַּבְרָא
The story: Ḥanan struck a man and was brought before Rav Huna, who ruled: pay half a dinar (the provincial sela). Ḥanan had only an unusable, damaged dinar with him, and the victim refused it — so Ḥanan struck him again, incurring a second half-dinar fine, and then paid the full dinar for both strikes at once.

🚫 Four Things That Cannot Redeem a Firstborn

The mishna's list, and why each fails

Excluded

  • Canaanite slaves — halakhically compared to land
  • Promissory notes — movable, but no intrinsic monetary value
  • Land — not movable property
  • Consecrated items — not truly one's own

Rabbi's Exception

  • Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi: everything works EXCEPT promissory notes
  • He allows slaves and land, unlike the first tanna

What Actually Works

  • Anything movable with genuine intrinsic monetary value
  • Silver, gold, other valuable movable goods

📐 Two Methods, One Verse

Why Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and the Rabbis read Numbers 18:16 so differently
Rabbi
I read by amplification and restriction: 'its redemption... you shall redeem' amplifies broadly, 'according to the valuation of five silver shekels' restricts narrowly, then 'you shall redeem' amplifies again — result: everything EXCEPT what's most dissimilar to silver, namely notes.
Rabbis
We read by generalization and detail: the detail (silver shekels) defines the category — only items that, like silver, are movable AND have intrinsic value. That excludes land, slaves, AND notes.
Ravina's challenge
But doesn't Rabbi normally use generalization-and-detail, as with the awl for a Hebrew slave's ear-piercing?
Ameimar's answer
Here the two generalizing phrases come BEFORE the detail — the school of Rabbi Yishmael teaches that order requires amplification-and-restriction instead.

✍️ A Promissory Note Doesn't Redeem

The mishna's ruling and Ulla's explanation
QuestionAnswer
Father writes a note promising 5 sela to the priestHe must pay it, but his son remains unredeemed
Why isn't the son redeemed once payment is made?Ulla: by Torah law he WOULD be redeemed — it's a rabbinic decree, lest people think a note itself can redeem
What can the priest do about it?If the priest wishes, he may give the five sela back as a gift
Does the halakha follow the view that the son becomes redeemed once he pays?No — Rav Naḥman rules that the accepted halakha is that the son is NOT redeemed
Rabbi Tarfon's practice: He would take the redemption money and immediately return it as a gift — praised by the Sages as fulfilling the halakha properly, since taking and returning shows the transaction was genuine.

💸 Lost Redemption Money Is the Father's Loss

Two derivations for why the father bears responsibility
1️⃣ Mishna: one who designates five sela for his son's redemption, then loses it before paying, remains obligated to pay again
↓
2️⃣ Reish Lakish: derived by verbal analogy of 'valuation' from the laws of personal valuations (arakhin) — one bears responsibility there too
↓
3️⃣ Rav Dimi (citing R' Yonatan): derived from 'none shall appear before Me empty' — just as one bears responsibility for a lost pilgrimage offering, so too for redemption
↓
4️⃣ Rav Pappa's alternative: no extra verse needed — the mishna's own language ('yours... you shall redeem') already implies the money must actually reach the priest
