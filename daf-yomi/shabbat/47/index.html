📖 Overview: Bases, Broken Vessels, and Beginning to Insulate

Shabbat 47a–47b
בַּמֶּה טוֹמְנִין וּבַמָּה אֵין טוֹמְנִין
The scene: The daf opens by confirming that even Rabbi Shimon agrees a lamp, oil, and wick become a 'base for a prohibited object' while lit, so none of it may be moved. Rabbi Romanus then reports that Rebbi permitted him to carry an ash-filled coal pan, prompting a long chain of objections from Abaye about what small residue (frankincense, broken sticks) can render a vessel movable, resolved through Rava's comparison to ashes usable for covering filth. The Gemara turns to reassembling collapsible objects like a weaver's loom, asking when doing so incurs a sin-offering, and resolves the question through Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's leniency for loosely-joined pieces. Perek Kira closes with the mishna about catching lamp sparks (but never adding water) and opens a new mishna, and chapter, on which materials may insulate a pot of food before Shabbat.

🔥 A Base for a Forbidden Object

הַנַּח לְנֵר שֶׁמֶן וּפְתִילָה, הוֹאִיל דְּנַעֲשָׂה בָּסִיס לַדָּבָר הָאָסוּר
The opening principle: Leave the candle, oil, and wick as they are, since while lit they have become a base for a prohibited object (basis l'davar ha'asur). Even Rabbi Shimon, who is generally lenient about set-aside, agrees that a burning flame on Shabbat may not be moved — and so neither may anything supporting it.

🪔 Rebbi's Coal Pan: A Chain of Objections

Rabbi Romanus reports a leniency, and Abaye keeps pressing to explain it
1️⃣ Rabbi Romanus: Rebbi permitted me to carry a coal pan full of its ashes
↓
2️⃣ Objection: compare to carrying a child holding a stone, or a basket of fruit with a stone inside — permitted only because of the fruit. What in the pan makes it permitted?
↓
3️⃣ First answer: bits of unburned frankincense inside — rejected by Abaye, since such tiny bits in Rebbi's house were not significant enough to matter
↓
4️⃣ Rava's final answer: the ashes themselves are usable to cover filth, so the pan is movable on their account — even broken sticks resting on it are nullified by the ashes
One more distinction: A baraita that broken wick fragments make a lamp unmovable is explained as taught specifically in the Galilee, where oil was so cheap that broken wicks were never nullified relative to it.

🪑 Reassembling Collapsible Objects

When does putting pieces back together count as forbidden building?

Liable (chayav chatat)

  • Reassembling a weaver's loom — Rav and Shmuel
  • Reassembling a candelabrum's branch
  • A round horn (complex to assemble) — Rabbi Simai

Exempt but forbidden

  • A plasterer's pole
  • Bed frame, bed legs, or archer's tablets — if forced together

Fully permitted

  • Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel: if the pieces fit loosely, with ease — permitted even ab initio
  • A straight horn (simple to assemble) — Rabbi Simai
Resolving the tension: Rabbi Abba and Rav Huna bar Chiyya relied on Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's leniency; Rava applied the same logic to his own family's collapsible bed.

💧 Sparks May Be Caught, Water May Not

The mishna's rule, tested against Rabbi Yosei's principle of indirect causation
📜 Mishna: place a vessel to catch sparks from a lamp (fine — sparks have no substance), but do not add water to it, since that extinguishes them
↓
❓ Is this the unattributed view of Rabbi Yosei, who forbids even indirectly causing something to be extinguished?
↓
🚫 Rejected: a baraita states the same rule applies even on Shabbat eve — but Rabbi Yosei's concern is a Shabbat-only issue!
↓
✅ Rav Ashi's answer: even the Rabbis (who normally permit indirect causes) forbid this, since placing water there hastens the extinguishing rather than merely causing it eventually
