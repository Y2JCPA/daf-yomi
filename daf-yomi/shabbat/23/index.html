📖 Overview: Obligation, Blessing, and Priority

Shabbat 23a–23b
אָסוּר לְהַרְצוֹת מָעוֹת כְּנֶגֶד נֵר חֲנוּכָּה
The scene: The daf opens with proof that lighting, not placement, accomplishes the Chanukah mitzva — settled from the practice of extinguishing and relighting a lantern that burned all Shabbat, and from the wording of the blessing itself. From there the Gemara maps who is obligated: not a deaf-mute, imbecile, or minor, but certainly a woman, since 'they too were included in the miracle,' and even a guest lodging with a host. It works out the blessing formula and how many blessings are said on the first night versus later nights, establishes a strict order of priority when a poor person can afford only one lamp, and closes with a cluster of teachings on the reward for lighting generously — plus the correct timing, neither too early nor too late.

🔦 Proof: Lighting Accomplishes the Mitzva

A lantern already burning, and the wording of the blessing itself
🏮 Case: a lantern burns through all of Shabbat; at Shabbat's end, one extinguishes it and relights it as the Chanukah light
↓
❓ If placing accomplishes the mitzva, the halakha should read 'extinguish, lift it, set it down, and light' — but it doesn't say that
↓
🙏 Further proof: the blessing recited is '…to light the Chanukah lamp' — over the act of lighting itself
↓
✅ Conclusion: hadlaka oseh mitzva — lighting, not placement, accomplishes the mitzva
Immediate consequence: If a deaf-mute, an imbecile, or a minor lights the lamp, nothing has been accomplished — even if a competent adult later moves it into place. A woman, however, is certainly fit to light, since she too was saved in the miracle.

🙏 The Blessing Formula, Night by Night

Three sages weigh in on who blesses and how many blessings
1️⃣ Rav (via Rav Chiyya bar Ashi): one who lights must recite a blessing
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Yirmeya: one who merely sees a burning Chanukah light must also bless
↓
3️⃣ Rav Yehuda's formula, night one: the one who lights says three blessings; the one who only sees says two
↓
4️⃣ From night two onward: the lighter says two, the seer says one — the blessing dropped is shehecheyanu ('who has given us life'), since it applies only to the first time each year, while the miracle blessing applies every night
Where is the command? Since Chanukah isn't in the Torah, the blessing 'who commanded us' is grounded homiletically: Rav Avya derives it from 'you shall not turn aside' (Deut. 17:11), the general obligation to heed the Sages.

⚖️ When Oil Money Is Scarce: What Comes First

Rava's ranked priorities for a poor household

Shabbat Home Lamp Wins

  • Home lamp beats Chanukah lamp — because of shalom bayit, peace in the home
  • Home lamp beats kiddush wine — same reason: a family shouldn't eat in darkness

Chanukah Lamp vs. Kiddush

  • Rava's dilemma: does kiddush win because it's frequent (tadir), or Chanukah win for publicizing the miracle?
  • Rava resolves it himself: Chanukah wins — pirsumei nisa, publicizing the miracle, takes precedence
Also on this daf: A courtyard with two entrances facing different directions needs a lamp at each, to avoid the suspicion of passersby who see only one door lit — echoing the four reasons the Torah requires pe'a specifically at the end of a field.

🌟 Diligence in Small Mitzvot, Rewarded in Children

הָרָגִיל בְּנֵר הָוְיִין לֵיהּ בָּנִים תַּלְמִידֵי חֲכָמִים
Rav Huna teaches that one accustomed to kindling lights merits children who are Torah scholars. Passing the home of Rabbi Avin the carpenter and seeing him light many lamps, Rav Huna predicted: 'Two great men will emerge from here' — and indeed Rav Idi bar Avin and Rav Chiyya bar Avin did. Rav Chisda made the same prediction about Rav Sheizvi's family. But timing still matters: when Rav Yosef's wife lit too early, an Elder corrected her from the verse about the pillars of cloud and fire — light neither too early nor too late.
