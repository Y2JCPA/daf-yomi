📖 Overview: Mapping Every Position Around the Stove

Shabbat 37a–37b
מִצְטַמֵּק וְיָפֶה לוֹ — אָסוּר, מִצְטַמֵּק וְרַע לוֹ — מוּתָּר
The scene: The Gemara works through the unresolved question from the previous daf — does the mishna's 'he may not place' mean 'return' or 'leave'? — by reconstructing the mishna's missing text and testing it against parallel Tosefta traditions attributed to Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehuda. It then maps out every physical position relative to a stove (inside, on top, leaning against), addresses a stove whose ashes reignite on Shabbat, and closes with a detailed dispute over which cooked foods may safely be left on the fire — resolved by Rav Nachman's rule about shriveling and flour.

🪢 Four Positions Around an Unswept, Uncovered Stove

Inside, on top, leaning against, and returning to it — each has its own rule
ActionStatus on unswept/uncovered stoveBasis
Place inside the stoveForbiddenRav in the name of Rabbi Chelbo — coals inside risk being stoked

Lean a pot against the outsidePermitted, even unsweptTosefta: one may lean against a pomace/wood stove; only placing inside requires sweeping or ash

Ash-like coals: Coals that have dimmed, or that are covered with a strip of fine flax, count as though the stove were properly ashed — no additional ash is required.

🔥♻️ When a Covered Stove Reignites on Shabbat

Rav Oshaya's ruling, extended by Rabbi Yochanan
קְטָמָהּ וְהוּבְעֲרָה מְשַׁהִין עָלֶיהָ חַמִּין שֶׁהוּחַמּוּ כׇּל צוֹרְכָּן
The ruling: If a stove was properly covered with ash before Shabbat and then reignites on its own during Shabbat, Rav Oshaya rules one may still leave on it hot water that was already fully heated and food already fully cooked — since nothing more is needed from the fire, there is no worry that anyone will stoke the coals. Rabbi Yochanan extends this even to unusually hot broom-wood coals, and Rav Safra transmits in Rav Chiyya's name that one may even lean against, leave on, remove from, and return to such a reignited stove — though the Gemara stresses this leniency flows specifically from the ash-covering done before Shabbat, not from any general rule about fully-cooked food.

🥘 May Fully-Cooked Food That Still Improves Be Left on the Fire?

A dispute over 'mitztamek v'yafeh lo' — food that shrivels and gets better
R. Shmuel b.Y.
In Rabbi Yochanan's name: one may leave fully-cooked food and fully-heated water on a pomace/wood stove — even food that shrivels and improves the longer it sits.
A colleague
But didn't Rav and Shmuel both rule that food which shrivels-and-improves is forbidden to leave on the fire, precisely because one might be tempted to stoke the coals?
R. Shmuel b.Y.
I know that ruling well — but what I told you follows Rabbi Yochanan's opinion, not Rav and Shmuel's.
Rav Ukva
To Rav Ashi: you who live near where Rav and Shmuel taught should follow them; we will follow Rabbi Yochanan.
An unresolved memory: Rav Ashi recalled that fried fish (kasa deharsena) was once left for Rav Huna on Shabbat, but wasn't sure if that was because shrivel-and-improve is permitted, or simply because that floury dish actually shrivels-and-deteriorates.

🌰 Rav Nachman's Rule: What Shrivels and Improves?

A practical guide to which dishes may be left on the fire
1️⃣ General principle: mitztamek v'yafeh lo (shrivels and improves) is forbidden to leave; mitztamek v'ra lo (shrivels and deteriorates) is permitted
↓
2️⃣ Any dish containing flour shrivels-and-deteriorates — with one exception: turnip stew, which improves even though it has flour
↓
3️⃣ This 'improves' rule for turnip stew applies only when meat is in it; without meat, it deteriorates like other floury dishes
↓
4️⃣ Even with meat, it only improves when not intended for guests — food meant for guests is considered to deteriorate, since overcooked food is unappetizing to serve
↓
5️⃣ Figs, porridge, and dates all shrivel and deteriorate when left on the fire, and so may be left
