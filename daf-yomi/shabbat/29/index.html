📖 Overview: Broken Vessels, a Dripping Eggshell, and a New Mishna on Extinguishing

Shabbat 29a–29b
לֹא יִקּוֹב אָדָם שְׁפוֹפֶרֶת שֶׁל בֵּיצָה וִימַלְּאֶנָּה שֶׁמֶן וְיִתְּנֶנָּה עַל פִּי הַנֵּר בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁתְּהֵא מְנַטֶּפֶת
The scene: The daf continues examining the folded-wick mishna's underlying dispute, testing why Rav Yosef's 'three by three exactly' matters and why Rabbi Yehuda restricts kindling to whole, not broken, vessels — extending this to date pits and nutshells, each teaching a case that could not be derived from the others. A second explanation, from Rav Hamnuna, roots Rabbi Eliezer's and Rabbi Akiva's dispute in a Kelim mishna about small cloths used for menial tasks, and whether tossing, hanging, or boxing a rag negates its garment-status. A new mishna then forbids indirect methods of feeding a Shabbat lamp more oil — a pierced eggshell or an adjacent oil bowl — which Rabbi Yehuda permits; the Gemara illustrates with Rabbi Yehuda's own story from the house of Nitza in Lod, and a related discussion of dragging furniture on Shabbat. The daf ends with a fresh mishna on extinguishing a lamp: exempt when done from fear or for a sick person's sake, but liable when done to spare the lamp, oil, or wick.

🪵 Rabbi Yehuda's Three 'Nolad' Cases

Each teaches something the others could not
CaseRabbi YehudaRabbi Shimon
Broken vesselsForbidden — became 'newly created' (nolad) when they brokePermitted
Date pits (after eating the dates)Forbidden — were hidden in the fruit, now exposedPermitted
Nutshells (after eating the nuts)Forbidden — even though shells were always exposedPermitted
Why all three were needed: Vessels alone might suggest only 'transformed' items are forbidden; pits alone might suggest only 'newly exposed' items are forbidden; shells prove the prohibition is broader still, since they were exposed all along.

🧦 A Small Cloth: When Does It Stop Being a 'Garment'?

Rabbi Eliezer, Rabbi Yehoshua, and Rabbi Akiva's shifting position
Mishna
A cloth under three-by-three handbreadths, used to plug a bath or wipe a millstone: R' Eliezer says it can become impure whether or not it was set aside for that use.
R' Yehoshua
It is pure either way — such use always negates its status as a significant garment.
R' Akiva
It depends: prepared for the task, impure; not prepared, pure.
Gemara
Everyone agrees: thrown in the trash, it is pure. Kept in a box, everyone agrees it is impure. They disagree only over hanging it on a peg or leaving it behind a door.
Rava
The wording 'wick of a garment' shows Rabbi Akiva later retracted toward Rabbi Yehoshua's view — it remains a garment only when clearly still valued.

🥚 New Mishna: No Indirect Oil-Drip on Shabbat

The Rabbis and Rabbi Yehuda disagree on three parallel cases
לֹא יְמַלֵּא אָדָם קְעָרָה שֶׁל שֶׁמֶן וְיִתְּנֶנָּה בְּצַד הַנֵּר וְיִתֵּן רֹאשׁ הַפְּתִילָה בְּתוֹכָהּ בִּשְׁבִיל שֶׁתְּהֵא שׁוֹאֶבֶת
The mishna: One may not pierce an eggshell (or an earthenware tube), fill it with oil, and set it over the lamp so it drips extra oil in — the Rabbis forbid this even with disgusting earthenware, fearing one might siphon oil directly from it. Rabbi Yehuda permits it. If the vessel-maker attached the tube to the lamp from the start, however, all agree it is permitted, since it is one continuous vessel. Likewise, the Rabbis forbid placing a bowl of oil beside the lamp with the wick's end dipped in to draw more oil; Rabbi Yehuda again permits it.

🏛️ Rabbi Yehuda's Proof — and Its Rejection

A story from the house of Nitza in Lod
1️⃣ Rabbi Yehuda: 'We once spent Shabbat in the upper story of the house of Nitza in Lod, and pierced an eggshell of oil over the lamp — Rabbi Tarfon and the Elders present said nothing.'
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Yehuda takes their silence as agreement that this is permitted
↓
3️⃣ The Rabbis reject the proof: 'That house's Elders were unusually vigilant' — not representative of ordinary people who might siphon the oil
↓
4️⃣ Lesson drawn (Rabbi Yitzḥak ben Elazar, re: a dragged bench): silence in one exceptional case can wrongly be generalized into leniency — so one must object even when the letter of the law might allow it

🕯️ New Mishna: Extinguishing the Lamp

Motive determines liability
❓ Why did he extinguish the Shabbat lamp?
↓
Exempt
Fear of gentiles or thieves, an 'evil spirit' (depression), or so a sick person can sleep — not a constructive act
Liable
To spare the lamp, the oil, or the wick — a constructive, purposeful act
↓
⚖️ Rabbi Yosei's dissent: exempt in ALL these cases except sparing the wick — since extinguishing it does create something, charcoal
