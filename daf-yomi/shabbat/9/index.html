📖 Overview: From the Threshold to the Barber's Chair

Shabbat 9a–9b
לֹא יֵשֵׁב אָדָם לִפְנֵי הַסַּפָּר סָמוּךְ לַמִּנְחָה עַד שֶׁיִּתְפַּלֵּל
The scene: Daf 9 closes out the sugya on domains by finishing the analysis of the threshold as an exempt domain — distinguishing an object that merely passes through its airspace from one that actually comes to rest there — and by explaining why a threshold's status flips between 'inside' and 'outside' depending on whether the door is open or locked. It also cites Rabbi Meir's decree against resting a burden atop a private-domain pillar, modeled on a parallel danger in the public domain. The daf then turns a corner entirely: a new mishna opens a fresh topic, listing activities — a haircut, the bathhouse, the tannery, a meal, a court case — that one may not begin close to the afternoon prayer, and the Gemara works out exactly which 'mincha' is meant and why.

🚪 Landing In vs. Passing Through

Why an exempt domain can break a transfer into two harmless halves
❓ Rava ruled that carrying an object above the ten-handbreadth airspace of an exempt domain is still one continuous, liable act. Does the threshold, itself an exempt domain, work the same way?
↓
Merely passes through
The object never rests in the exempt domain — the transfer stays ONE continuous act, and Rava's ruling makes him LIABLE
Comes to rest there
The object actually lands in the exempt domain — the transfer splits into TWO separate actions, neither of which is a Torah violation, so he is EXEMPT
↓
✅ This is exactly why a threshold can serve as a safe 'rest stop': an object set down there breaks the chain between private and public domain
Distinction: It is not the exempt domain itself that grants safety — it is whether the object actually comes to rest inside it.

🏠 Why an Open Door Makes the Threshold 'Like Inside'

Two different accounts of Acherim's ruling
SageType of thresholdExplanation
Rav Yehuda, citing RavAn alleyway's threshold, half covered toward the inside and half uncoveredOpen door: the roofing's edge counts as a lowered partition, sealing it as 'inside.' Locked door: no longer sealed, so it reverts to 'outside'
Rav AshiA house's threshold, covered by two beams (each under 4 handbreadths wide, less than 3 handbreadths apart), with a door between themOpen door: lavud joins the beams into one sealed roof-edge, treated as 'inside.' Locked door: the door separates them, so it reverts to 'outside'
Common ground: Both answers preserve Rav Chama bar Gurya's rule that an opening needs its own post (לֶחִי) to be treated as sealed — they simply locate that 'post' differently.

🏛️ Two Domains That Are Legally One

Rabbi Meir's decree against a pillar standing inside a private domain

A Pillar Inside a Private Domain

  • Ten handbreadths high, four wide — by measurement, a full private domain of its own
  • Rabbi Meir: rabbinically forbidden to rest a burden atop it anyway

A Mound of the Same Size in the Public Domain

  • Lifting an object from the ground and placing it atop such a mound is a genuine Torah violation (carrying from public to private domain)
  • The Sages decreed against the pillar precisely so people would not confuse the two cases
The principle: "Any place where you find two domains that are [legally] one" — even where Torah law would permit an action, the Sages sometimes forbid it by analogy to a genuinely forbidden parallel case.

🕒 Which Mincha Does the Mishna Mean?

Reconciling the new mishna with Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's stricter rule
Gemara
Which mincha does the mishna mean — mincha gedola (about half an hour after noon) or mincha ketana (about two and a half hours before sunset)?
Gemara
Not mincha gedola — there is still plenty of daylight left! It must mean mincha ketana.
Gemara
But that clashes with Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: once the time for the afternoon prayer arrives, one may not taste anything before praying — implying he must stop even if he already began!
Gemara
Rather, the mishna DOES mean mincha gedola — but only for especially long versions of each activity: ben Elasa's elaborate haircut, a full bathing session, a large tannery's work, a big feast, the opening of a lengthy trial.
Rav Acha bar Yaakov
Actually, the mishna means even an ORDINARY haircut, still near mincha gedola — forbidden lest the scissors break, he faint in the bath, he spot a loss in the tannery and grow anxious, a small meal stretch on, or a verdict get overturned.
Marking the start: The Gemara pins down exactly when each activity 'begins' — from placing the barber's wrap on one's knees, removing one's cloak, tying the tanner's apron, or (per Rav and Rabbi Chanina, reflecting Babylonian and Eretz Yisrael custom) washing hands or loosening one's belt for the meal.
