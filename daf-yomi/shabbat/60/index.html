📖 Overview: A Sandal's Nails and the Weight of a Tragic Memory

Shabbat 60a–60b
לֹא יֵצֵא הָאִישׁ בְּסַנְדָּל הַמְסוּמָּר
The scene: The daf opens by settling the previous amud's ring contradiction with three different resolutions, then explains why an unperforated needle — seemingly just a hair tool, forbidden to use on Shabbat itself — may still be worn out. A new mishna then lists six items a man may not wear into the public domain: a spiked sandal, a single sandal without a wound to justify it, tefillin, an unauthenticated amulet, and war-gear — none of which carry sin-offering liability even if worn out. The Gemara devotes the rest of the daf to the spiked sandal, tracing its ban to a historic tragedy in a cave of refugees, and then works out exactly how many nails turn a sandal from a forbidden 'reinforced' shoe into a permitted, merely decorated one.

🔧 The Ring Contradiction, Resolved Three Ways

Does a sealed ring count by its ring, or by its seal?
Mishna rule
With a hook-board, follow its nails; with a ladder, follow its rungs; with a scale, follow its chains. The Rabbis: for all of these, everything follows the support (מַעֲמִיד).
Gemara
Then a ring should follow the ring itself, not the seal — so why does Rabbi Neḥemya treat a sealed ring differently for Shabbat, while the Kelim mishna calls a sealed ring an impure ornament just like a plain one?
Rava
No contradiction at all — the two mishnayot speak 'in respective terms': a sealed ring is a MAN's ornament, an unsealed ring is a WOMAN's ornament.
Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak
Don't compare impurity to Shabbat! Impurity follows the verse כְּלִי מַעֲשֶׂה ('an implement with which work is done') — a ring is that either way. Shabbat forbids only a burden: unsealed, it's a woman's ornament; sealed, it's a burden for her.
Underlying idea: 'Everything follows the support' decides which PART defines a vessel's material for impurity — a separate question from whether a woman is likely to slip an item off and carry it on Shabbat.

📌 An Unperforated Needle — Why Permitted?

Three answers before the Gemara lands on one
1️⃣ Rav Yosef: a woman uses it to gather her hair and pin it to her hairnet
↓
2️⃣ Abaye objects: then it's like a (impurity-)pure garter — permitted simply because modesty stops her from removing and showing it. Why did the mishna need to list it at all?
↓
3️⃣ Rav Adda of Naresh answers instead: she uses it to part her hair
↓
4️⃣ New problem: parting hair is itself forbidden on Shabbat — so what use does the needle serve ON Shabbat?
↓
5️⃣ Rava: the needle has a gold plate on its other end. Weekdays, she parts her hair with it; Shabbat, she tucks it into her head-covering with the gold plate resting on her forehead — purely as an ornament

⚔️ The Cave Tragedy Behind the Spiked-Sandal Decree

Refugees from religious persecution, undone by the sound of nails
1️⃣ Shmuel: Jews fleeing religious persecution hid in a cave, agreeing — whoever wants to enter may enter, but no one may leave (a leaver risks revealing the hideout)
↓
2️⃣ One man's sandal turned backward; his footprints looked like someone leaving. Fearing an enemy had spotted them, the group panicked, pushed one another, and killed more of their own than any enemy had
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Ilai ben Elazar's version: they merely heard the SOUND of a spiked sandal above the cave, and panicked the same way
↓
4️⃣ Rami bar Yeḥezkel's version: it happened in a synagogue, hearing the sound from behind the building
↓
5️⃣ In every version it happened on Shabbat — so the ban applies to Shabbat (and, since 'assemblage' applies equally, to Festivals too), but not to a fast day, a day of permitted labor unlike the original incident

🔨 Structural Nails vs. Decorative Nails

How many nails, and what kind, keep a sandal off the forbidden list

Forbidden — For Strength

  • Nails placed to reinforce the sole
  • Rabbi Yoḥanan: more than five per sandal
  • Rabbi Ḥanina: more than seven per sandal
  • Rav Ḥisda: four is the limit for a small sandal, five for a large one

Permitted — For Beauty

  • A small, decorative number of nails, arranged evenly on both sides and the strap
  • Sewn from inside: it becomes a 'shoe', not a 'sandal' — no decree at all (Rav Ashi)
  • Bent like tongs, or the whole sole covered in nails so the ground won't wear it down (Rav Sheshet)

Edge Cases

  • An uneven sole needs extra straightening nails — Rabbi Natan: up to seven; Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi: up to thirteen — not treated as a spiked sandal at all
  • If most nails fell out and only four or five remain, it is permitted
