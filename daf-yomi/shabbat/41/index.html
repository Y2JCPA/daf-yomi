📖 Overview: Modesty, Health, and Two More Heating Vessels

Shabbat 41a–41b
מוּלְיָאר הַגָּרוּף שׁוֹתִין הֵימֶנּוּ בְּשַׁבָּת. אַנְטִיכֵי, אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁגְּרוּפָה אֵין שׁוֹתִין הֵימֶנָּה
The scene: The daf closes out the discussion of bathing with stories about Rabbi Abbahu's modest conduct in the river and Rabbi Zeira's chance encounter with his teacher, Rav Yehuda, in the bathhouse. Two new mishnayot then introduce further heating vessels and their Shabbat rules: the mulyar and antikhi urns used for hot water, and the case of an emptied urn into which cold water may or may not be poured, depending on whether the goal is a gentle warming or something closer to actually heating — or even inadvertently 'tempering' — the metal itself.

🙈 May One Cover Oneself for Modesty in the River?

Descending versus emerging makes all the difference
Challenge
Rav taught: anyone who places his hands over his genitals is as if he denies the covenant of Avraham — it looks like he's hiding his circumcision!
Resolution
No contradiction: while descending into the river, facing no one, covering oneself looks like denial — forbidden. While emerging, facing people on the bank, covering for modesty is permitted.
In practice
Rava would bend over when emerging; Rabbi Zeira stood upright even then.
Rav Ashi's academy
Stood upright while descending, and bent over only while emerging — matching the rule precisely.
Why floating was allowed at all: Rabbi Abbahu's pool had embankments, making it look like an enclosed vessel rather than an open river — a baraita otherwise forbids floating even in a courtyard pool that lacks such borders.

🚿 Overhearing Rav Yehuda in the Bathhouse

Rabbi Zeira's search for one last teaching before leaving for Eretz Yisrael
פִּתְחוּ פּוּמַּיְיכוּ וְאַפִּיקוּ הַבְלָא, וְאִשְׁתוּ מִמַּיָּא דְּבֵי בָאנֵי
What he heard: Rabbi Zeira, planning to move to Eretz Yisrael against his teacher's view that doing so violated a positive commandment (Jeremiah 27:22), wanted to hear something from Rav Yehuda first without confronting him directly. He found him in the bathhouse instructing his attendants: 'Bring me natron, bring me a comb, open your mouths and let the steam out, and drink from the bathhouse water.' Rabbi Zeira said this alone was worth the visit. The Gemara explains each instruction: mundane requests may be spoken in the sacred tongue even in a bathhouse; releasing steam speeds sweating (הַבְלָא מַפֵּיק הַבְלָא); and drinking while bathing prevents the digestive harm of eating without drinking, walking, or later rinsing in cold water and oiling the skin.

🫖 Two Heating Vessels: Mulyar and Antikhi

New mishna — same swept status, opposite rulings
VesselConfigurationMay one drink from it, once swept of coals?
MulyarWater on the inside, coals on the outsideYes — permitted
Antikhi (Rabba's view)A stove-like recess (bei chirei) built into a wallNo — forbidden even when swept
Antikhi (Rav Nachman b.Y.'s view)Two stacked cauldrons: coals below, water above (bei dudei)No — forbidden, because the copper itself continues to heat the water
Why the difference matters: A baraita supports Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak's identification, teaching explicitly that an antikhi is forbidden even swept and covered with ash, since its copper walls retain and conduct heat directly to the water.

🔥 The Emptied Urn: How Much Water May Be Added?

The mishna's apparent self-contradiction, resolved by quantity
❓ Mishna: one may not add cold water to an emptied hot urn to heat it — yet one may add water to warm it. Which is it?
↓
💡 Rav Adda bar Mattana resolves: it depends on the amount of water added
↓
Small quantity
Forbidden — a small amount of cold water in a still-hot urn would itself become fully hot
Large quantity
Permitted — a large amount only loses its chill (l'hafshir), without truly heating up

⚒️ Does Adding Water 'Temper' the Urn Like Metalwork?

Abaye reframes the mishna, and Rav and Shmuel debate the resulting question
Challenge
Doesn't pouring cold water into a hot metal urn count as 'tempering' it — a form of the prohibited labor of finishing metal?
First answer
The mishna follows Rabbi Shimon: an unintended outcome (tempering) is permitted when one's actual intent was only to warm the water.
Abaye
That can't be right — the mishna's wording is 'an urn that was removed' (still holding hot water), not 'an urn emptied of water.' Properly read: adding water to an emptied urn is forbidden outright, and this follows Rabbi Yehuda, who forbids even unintended outcomes.
Rav vs. Shmuel
Rav: the leniency applies only up to the measure needed to warm the water, not to fill it enough to temper the metal. Shmuel: even filling to the tempering measure is permitted.
