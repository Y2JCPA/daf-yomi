📖 Overview: The Long History of Bathing on Shabbat

Shabbat 40a–40b
בַּתְּחִילָּה הָיוּ רוֹחֲצִין בְּחַמִּין שֶׁהוּחַמּוּ מֵעֶרֶב שַׁבָּת
The scene: Having established that hot water heated before Shabbat may sometimes be used afterward, the Gemara asks exactly how much washing that permits — the whole body or just exposed limbs? A closely-argued dispute between Rav and Shmuel is settled against Rav, and the Gemara then tells the remarkable history of how bathhouse customs in Tiberias were tightened again and again as people exploited each new leniency. The daf closes with the boundary between merely warming an object (water, oil, a towel) and actually cooking it — including oil's own uncertain status — illustrated by a story of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi teaching halakha from inside the bathhouse itself.

📈 The Escalating History of Tiberias Bathing Decrees

Each new leniency was exploited, prompting a stricter one
1️⃣ Originally: people bathed in hot water heated before Shabbat — permitted
↓
2️⃣ Bathhouse attendants began secretly heating water on Shabbat, claiming it was pre-heated → Sages banned hot bathing, permitted only sweating
↓
3️⃣ People kept bathing in hot water, claiming 'we are only sweating' → Sages banned sweating too, but permitted the natural hot springs of Tiberias
↓
4️⃣ People bathed in ordinary fire-heated water, falsely claiming it was spring water → Sages banned even the Tiberias springs, permitting only cold water
↓
5️⃣ The public could not sustain such strictness, so the Sages restored permission for the Tiberias springs — while the ban on sweating in a heated bathhouse remained

🫒 Is Oil Subject to the Prohibition of Cooking?

The first tanna's silent position, reconstructed two opposite ways
Rabba & R. Yosef
The first tanna holds oil is not subject to cooking at all — it may be warmed even to the point where a hand would recoil from it.
R. Nachman b.Y.
No — the first tanna holds oil is subject to cooking, and forbids warming it even below that threshold.
Rav Yehuda
In Shmuel's name, resolving the matter for both oil and water: heating to the point a hand recoils is forbidden; below that point, permitted.
Rachava
And the measure of 'a hand recoils' is: any heat that would scald a baby's stomach.

🛁 Three Lessons From a Bathhouse Instruction

Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi teaches halakha without saying a word of Torah
טוֹל בִּכְלִי שֵׁנִי וְתֵן
The incident: Rabbi Yitzchak bar Avdimi wanted to place a jar of oil directly into Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's bathtub to warm it. Rabbi told him instead: 'Take water in a second vessel and pour the oil in there.' The Gemara draws three halakhot from this single instruction: that oil is subject to the prohibition of cooking (or he wouldn't have objected), that a second vessel does not cook (so pouring the oil there was safe), and that warming oil to lukewarm counts as cooking it. Though Torah matters are normally not to be spoken in a bathhouse, this was permitted because Rabbi was preventing a transgression — just as a story about a student of Rabbi Meir shows the same principle applied elsewhere.
