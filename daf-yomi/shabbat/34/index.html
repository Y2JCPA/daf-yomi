📖 Overview: From a Purified Marketplace to the Boundaries of Twilight

Shabbat 34a–34b
שְׁלֹשָׁה דְּבָרִים צָרִיךְ אָדָם לוֹמַר בְּתוֹךְ בֵּיתוֹ עֶרֶב שַׁבָּת עִם חֲשֵׁכָה
The scene: The daf opens with the dramatic end of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai's purification of the Tiberias marketplace, a story of both careful halakhic method and a fatal rebuke. The mishna then turns to a wholly different concern: what a person must say at home just before Shabbat, and what happens when nightfall itself is in doubt. The Gemara traces the mishna's checklist to a verse in Job, explains why the mishna's own two halves seem to contradict each other, and finally maps out the disputed physical signs and durations of twilight (bein hashemashot) — the razor's edge between the last permitted acts of Friday and the first prohibitions of Shabbat.

🪦 Purifying the Marketplace of Tiberias

Ground hard or soft, and a rebuke that cost two lives
כָּאן קִיצֵּץ בֶּן זַכַּאי תּוּרְמְסֵי תְּרוּמָה
The method and its cost: Priests were troubled by suspected corpse-impurity in the Tiberias marketplace. An Elder told Rabbi Shimon that Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai once grew teruma lupines there, proving parts were pure. Rabbi Shimon then examined the ground itself: wherever it was hard he declared it pure (no burial possible), wherever soft he marked it as doubtful. When that same Elder later mocked him publicly — 'Ben Yochai purified the cemetery!' — Rabbi Shimon answered that since the Elder had been counted among those who ruled on it, mocking it now would make Torah scholars look like feuding rivals, worse than prostitutes who still help each other look presentable. He fixed his gaze on the Elder, who died; then, seeing Yehuda ben Gerim, who had caused the whole affair, in the marketplace, he fixed his gaze on him too and turned him into a pile of bones.

Have you tithed?Ask about definitely-untithed produceMay not tithe definitely-untithed produce; may tithe demai (doubtfully-tithed)

🤔 Why Does the Mishna Seem to Contradict Itself?

It bars action at 'doubtful dusk' — yet permits placing an eiruv at exactly that time
Gemara
The mishna says these things must be said 'at dusk,' implying that during doubtful dusk one may not act. But then it teaches that one may place an eiruv during doubtful dusk!
Rav
No contradiction. The first clause speaks of עֵירוּבֵי תְּחוּמִין (Shabbat-boundary eiruvin), a Torah-level matter requiring certainty. The second speaks of עֵירוּבֵי חֲצֵרוֹת (courtyard eiruvin), a lighter rabbinic stringency.
Rabba b. R. Huna
Even so — these three things must be said calmly, so the household will actually accept them and not be provoked into ignoring the warning.
Rav Ashi
I never heard that teaching stated explicitly, but I arrived at the very same practice on my own, through plain reasoning.
Takeaway: A Torah-level eiruv needs certainty before dusk; a rabbinic one tolerates the uncertainty of twilight itself.

🎲 Rava's Case: Two People, One Agent, One Twilight

Both acquire the eiruv — no matter which way twilight actually falls
❓ An agent places an eiruv while it is still day for person A, and at twilight for person B
↓
🍽️ Both eiruvin get 'eaten' — A's during twilight, B's after nightfall
↓
If twilight = day
B's eiruv (placed at twilight) should count; A's should not, since it was consumed while still day
If twilight = night
A's eiruv should count; B's should not, since it was placed too late
↓
✅ Rava's ruling: twilight's status is unresolved doubt, and rabbinic-level doubt is decided leniently — both acquire their eiruv
Principle: סְפֵקָא דְרַבָּנַן לְקוּלָּא — uncertainty in a rabbinic law is resolved leniently.

⏳ How Long Does Twilight Last?

Three tannaim, three different clocks for bein hashemashot

Rabbi Yehuda

  • From sunset while the eastern sky still reddens
  • Lower sky faded, upper still red = still twilight
  • Upper sky faded and matches lower = night

Rabbi Nechemya

  • The time it takes to walk half a mil after sunset
  • Test: watch the sun atop Mount Carmel, then descend and immerse in the sea
  • That interval is his measure

Rabbi Yosei

  • Twilight is like the blink of an eye
  • Night enters and day leaves in that instant
  • Its duration cannot really be measured at all
Why it matters: The baraita imposes the stringencies of both days on this uncertain window — relevant, for example, to a zav who saw an emission during twilight, whose day-count for impurity and offerings becomes doubtful.
