📖 Overview: Coals, Water Temperature, and Set-Aside Vessels

Shabbat 42a–42b
הָאִילְפָּס וְהַקְּדֵרָה שֶׁהֶעֱבִירָן מְרוּתָּחִין — לֹא יִתֵּן לְתוֹכָן תַּבְלִין
The scene: The daf opens by analyzing Shmuel's ruling that one may extinguish a white-hot metal coal in a public area to prevent injury, asking whether this fits Rabbi Shimon's view that an unintended prohibited labor is permitted. The Gemara distinguishes unintentional acts from labor not needed for its own sake, then moves to a baraita on pouring hot and cold water together, where Beit Shammai, Beit Hillel, and Rabbi Shimon ben Menasya disagree, and the halakha is fixed like the latter. A new mishna then forbids adding spices to a boiling pot removed from the fire, sparking a dispute over whether Rabbi Yehuda is lenient or strict, and whether salt behaves like a spice. The daf closes with a mishna forbidding a vessel placed to catch lamp oil, which leads into Rav Chisda's ruling about receiving a hen's egg.

🔥 Why May One Extinguish a Dangerous Coal?

The Gemara tests Shmuel's ruling against Rabbi Shimon's principle
❓ Shmuel permits extinguishing a white-hot metal coal in public so people won't be hurt, but not a wood coal. Does this mean Shmuel rules like Rabbi Shimon, who exempts a labor not needed for its own sake?
↓
🚫 If so, wood should also be permitted — extinguishing it too is a labor not needed for its own sake, just to prevent injury
↓
Unintentional act
Shmuel agrees with Rabbi Shimon: no intent to do the prohibited labor at all, so it is permitted
Labor not for its own sake
Shmuel holds like Rabbi Yehuda: liable, since extinguishing a wood coal is Torah-level and metal is only rabbinic
↓
✅ Ravina: therefore a thorn in the public domain may be carried less than four cubits at a time; in a karmelit, even more
Key distinction: A white-hot metal coal is not yet 'fire' by Torah law, so extinguishing it is only rabbinic; the Sages did not decree where public safety is at stake.

🛁 Is a Wash-Basin Like a Bathtub?

Rav Yosef's assumption is corrected, then the Gemara worries it removes all hot washing on Shabbat
Rav Yosef
I would say a סֵפֶל (basin) has the same status as an אַמְבָּטִי (bath) — cold may not be poured into it.
Abaye
Rabbi Ḥiyya taught: a basin is not like a bath.
Gemara
But if a basin really were like a bath, and the halakha follows Rabbi Shimon ben Menasya who forbids cold into hot even for a bath — would that mean there is no way at all to wash with hot water on Shabbat?
Answer
No such sweeping prohibition was ever taught. Rabbi Shimon ben Menasya's stringency addresses only the first clause of the baraita, about hot into cold — not all washing.
Result: A basin retains the lenient status of a cup, not the stringent status of a bath.

🍲 Spices Into a Boiling Pot: Lenient or Strict?

The mishna forbids seasoning a still-hot pot; Rabbi Yehuda's exception is ambiguous

First Clause: Lenient?

  • Mishna forbids spices into any boiling pot
  • Rabbi Yehuda might loosen this: only pots with vinegar or brine are forbidden

Second Clause: Strict?

  • Rabbi Yehuda allows spices into a bowl or tureen (secondary vessel)
  • He might add a stringency there: except one with vinegar or brine

Resolution

  • Baraita: 'into all stew pots, into all boiling pots' one may place spices, except vinegar or brine
  • Proves Rabbi Yehuda is lenient on the first clause
Come and hear: The explicit baraita settles the dilemma raised before the Sages in favor of the lenient reading.

🪔 From Lamp Oil to a Hen's Egg

The mishna's oil ruling and Rav Chisda's parallel about eggs
1️⃣ Mishna: one may not place a vessel beneath the oil lamp to catch dripping oil; if placed before Shabbat, the vessel is fine but the oil is unusable — it was never prepared for Shabbat use
↓
2️⃣ Rav Chisda: though one may not place a vessel to catch a hen's egg as it is laid, one may overturn a vessel onto the egg so it won't break
↓
3️⃣ Rabba explains why: a hen usually lays in a garbage dump (common risk of breakage) rather than on a slope (uncommon risk) — the Sages permitted acting only for common cases of preservation
↓
4️⃣ Abaye objects twice: a barrel of untithed produce that breaks on the roof may be caught (even though uncommon), and a vessel may catch sparks from a lamp — both answered as themselves 'common' cases (new barrels break often; sparks are frequent)
Salt vs. spice (bonus dispute): Rav Yosef and Abaye also debate, in two conflicting versions, whether salt cooks in a primary vessel the way a spice does — tied to Rav Naḥman's rule that salt needs as much cooking as ox meat.
