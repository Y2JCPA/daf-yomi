📖 Overview: From Shifting Words to a New Chapter on Cooking

Shabbat 36a–36b
כִּירָה שֶׁהִסִּיקוּהָ בְּקַשׁ וּבִגְבָבָא — נוֹתְנִים עָלֶיהָ תַּבְשִׁיל
The scene: The daf closes out the discussion of moving a shofar or trumpet with a short excursus on how Hebrew and Aramaic terms drifted after the Temple's destruction — with real halakhic stakes for shofar-blowing, the four species, commerce, kashrut, and even bills of divorce. The first chapter then ends, and a new chapter opens with a fresh mishna: the laws of a stove (keirah) heated before Shabbat, and whether cooked food may be placed, left, or returned to it. Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel disagree, and the Gemara opens a basic question about how to read the mishna's very first line.

🍲 New Mishna: What May Sit on the Stove Before Shabbat

Chapter 3 opens with the laws of a keirah (stove)
1️⃣ Stove lit with straw or rakings (quick-burning fuel): a pot of cooked food may be placed on it, since the fire is certainly out by Shabbat
↓
2️⃣ Stove lit with pomace or wood (long-burning fuel): nothing may be placed until the coals are swept out or covered with ash
↓
3️⃣ Beit Shammai: even after sweeping, only hot water may be placed — not cooked food
↓
4️⃣ Beit Hillel: both hot water and cooked food may be placed
↓
5️⃣ Beit Shammai: a pot may be removed from the stove on Shabbat, but not returned
↓
6️⃣ Beit Hillel: a pot may even be returned to the stove on Shabbat

🤔 What Does 'He May Not Place' Actually Mean?

The Gemara's opening dilemma on the mishna's very first line
Option A
'He may not place' means he may not return a pot once removed. But leaving a pot from before Shabbat is fine — even on an unswept stove — since it was already cooking.
Source
This matches Chananya's view: any food cooked to the level of מַאֲכַל בֶּן דְּרוּסַאי ('ben Drosai's food' — minimally cooked) may be left on an unswept stove.
Option B
Or perhaps 'he may not place' means one may not even leave a pot on an unswept stove from before Shabbat — and returning one on Shabbat is certainly worse.
Stakes
Under this reading, sweeping or ashing the coals is required just to leave food on the stove overnight, not only to return it.

🔍 Testing the Two Readings Against the Mishna's Structure

An attempted proof from the mishna's two matching disputes
❓ The mishna records two Beit Shammai/Beit Hillel disputes back to back — hot water vs. cooked food, then remove vs. return
↓
💡 If the mishna's opening means 'leaving': the first dispute (what may be left) and the second (what may be returned) read as two separate, parallel questions — a clean fit
↓
🤨 If the opening means 'returning': the second dispute about returning becomes redundant, since the first already covered it
↓
⚖️ Neither reading is fully proven yet — the Gemara's inquiry continues
Still open: The proof from the mishna's parallel structure favors 'leaving,' but the Gemara has not yet closed the question.

🍳 Chananya's Standard: 'Ben Drosai's Food'

The minimal cooking threshold that anchors one side of the debate
The practical marker: Chananya teaches that any food cooked to the extent that a legendary quick-cooking bandit named ben Drosai would consider done — the bare minimum for edibility — may be left on an unswept stove into Shabbat, since it needs no further cooking and there is no worry that someone will stoke the coals. This standard becomes the practical yardstick throughout the coming discussion for distinguishing food that still needs the fire from food that does not.
