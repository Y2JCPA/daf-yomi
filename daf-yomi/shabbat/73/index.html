📖 Overview: The Mishna of the Thirty-Nine Melachot

Shabbat 73a–73b
אֲבוֹת מְלָאכוֹת אַרְבָּעִים חָסֵר אַחַת
The scene: The daf closes out a run of Abaye-Rava disputes about unwitting intent — cases where a person meant to do something permitted but a forbidden result occurred anyway, such as intending to throw an object two cubits but it travels four. It then reaches one of the most famous mishnayot in Shas: the list of the thirty-nine primary categories of labor (avot melachot) forbidden on Shabbat, grouped by function — grain processing, wool and cloth work, leather work, writing, and building/fire/carrying. The Gemara first asks why the mishna bothers to count them, then works through each family, showing how several distinct actions can count as a single melacha, how the same physical act can be classified differently depending on where and why it is done, and where two melachot can be violated by one act at once.

🎯 Aiming for Less, Landing on More

Abaye and Rava dispute several parallel cases
Case 1
One intends to throw an object two cubits in the public domain (no Torah violation) — but it lands four cubits away (a real violation). Liable?
Rava
Exempt — he never intended to execute a four-cubit throw, so he had no intent to perform a forbidden act.
Abaye
Liable — he intended to perform a standard throw, and a throw covering the prohibited distance did occur.
Case 2
One thinks he stands in a private domain and throws far — but it turns out to be the public domain. Liable?
Rava
Exempt — same logic: no intent to throw where it is forbidden.
Abaye
Liable — he intended a standard throw regardless of the domain.
Why teach both cases: A third similar dispute — intending to cut a detached plant but severing an attached one — is also needed; none of the three cases can be derived from the others, since the line between a permitted amount and a forbidden one works differently each time.

🔢 Why Count 'Forty Less One'?

The tally itself teaches a halakha about multiple sin-offerings
❓ Why does the mishna bother stating the number, 'forty-less-one'?
↓
💡 Rabbi Yoḥanan: to teach that if one performed ALL of them in a single lapse of awareness, he is liable to a separate sin-offering for EACH ONE
↓
Fits Abaye easily
He holds one is liable even for a mere error in MEASURE (like the two/four-cubit case) — so full awareness of all 39 categories, with slips only on amounts, is a simple scenario
Harder for Rava
He exempts measure-errors, so the scenario must be: aware Shabbat forbids labor in principle, yet fully unaware which acts are the forbidden labors — e.g., aware of the sin but not of its karet penalty (R' Yoḥanan), or aware only of the tehum boundary law, per Rabbi Akiva (Reish Lakish)

🌱 One Melacha, Many Actions — Or Two at Once?

The sowing-family baraita and its exceptions

One Category, Several Actions

  • Sowing, pruning, planting, bending a branch into the ground, grafting — all ONE melacha (sowing)
  • Plowing, digging, making a furrow — all ONE melacha (plowing)
  • Threshing, beating flax, beating cotton — all ONE melacha (threshing)
  • Doing several of these unwittingly still means only ONE sin-offering

Two Melachot in One Act

  • Pruning a tree needed for its wood: liable twice — reaping (severing) AND planting (Rav Kahana)
  • Reaping alfalfa for feed: liable twice — reaping AND planting (Rav Yosef)
  • Cutting beet leaves: liable twice — reaping AND sowing (Abaye)

Exempt

  • Throwing a clod at a palm to knock down dates: Rav Pappa says liable twice (severing + extracting); Rav Ashi exempts — it is not the typical MANNER of either act

🕳️ Same Action, Different Melacha

Location and purpose decide which prohibition applies
1️⃣ Rav Sheshet: had a mound of dirt and leveled it — in the HOUSE, liable for building; in the FIELD, liable for plowing
↓
2️⃣ Rava: had a hole and filled it in — in the HOUSE, liable for building; in the FIELD, liable for plowing
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Abba: dug a hole only because he needed its DIRT (not to make a hole) — exempt, since digging itself was destructive here, not constructive
↓
4️⃣ Even Rabbi Yehuda, who usually holds one liable for a labor 'not needed for its own sake', agrees here — his stringency applies only to a CONSTRUCTIVE unwanted result, not a destructive one
