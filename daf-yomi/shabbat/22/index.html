📖 Overview: Placement, Sanctity, and the Mechanics of the Mitzva

Shabbat 22a–22b
הַדְלָקָה עוֹשָׂה מִצְוָה, אוֹ הַנָּחָה עוֹשָׂה מִצְוָה?
The scene: The daf opens with the rule that a Chanukah lamp placed above twenty cubits is invalid, like a sukka roof or an alleyway beam beyond that height — people simply don't look up that far. A drash on Joseph's pit follows on the heels of the same chain of tradents. The Gemara then works out exactly where the lamp goes (within a handbreadth of the door, on the left so it and the mezuza flank the entrant), whether the lamp carries enough sanctity to forbid using its light for mundane purposes like counting coins, and a three-way dispute between Rav and Shmuel about lighting one Chanukah lamp from another. That last question opens onto the daf's central theoretical puzzle, tested against the Temple candelabrum: does the act of lighting accomplish the mitzva, or does setting the lit lamp in its proper place?

📏 Not Above Twenty Cubits

וְהַבּוֹר רֵק אֵין בּוֹ מָיִם — מַיִם אֵין בּוֹ, אֲבָל נְחָשִׁים וְעַקְרַבִּים יֵשׁ בּוֹ
A Chanukah lamp placed higher than twenty cubits is invalid — the same standard as an invalid sukka roof or an alleyway's symbolic beam, since people do not ordinarily raise their eyes above that height. In the same chain of transmission (Rav Kahana citing Rav Natan bar Manyumi in the name of Rav Tanchum), the Gemara adds an unrelated drash on Joseph's pit: וְהַבּוֹר רֵק אֵין בּוֹ מָיִם — 'the pit was empty, there was no water in it.' Since 'empty' already implies no water, the extra words teach: no water, but snakes and scorpions were in it.

💰 Does the Chanukah Lamp Have Sanctity?

Shmuel questions Rav's ruling; Rav Yosef defends it by analogy
Rav
It is forbidden to count money by the light of the Chanukah lamp.
Shmuel
Does the Chanukah lamp have sanctity, that using its light should be forbidden?
R' Yosef
Does the blood of a slaughtered animal have sanctity? Yet the baraita on covering blood teaches: don't cover it with your foot — so that mitzvot are not treated with contempt. Here too, that principle applies.
Query
What about using sukka decorations during the seven days of the festival?
R' Yosef
That's a poor comparison — sukka decorations are explicitly forbidden in a baraita; the Chanukah lamp is not. The real paradigm for both is the blood-covering law.
Underlying idea: Even without formal sanctity, an object of a mitzva may not be casually repurposed — 'so that mitzvot are not treated with contempt' (shelo yehu mitzvot bezuyot alav).

🕯️ Three Disputes Where Rabba Follows Shmuel

Abaye's summary: usually Rav, but not here

Rav's View: Forbidden

  • May not light one Chanukah lamp directly from another
  • May not untie tzitzit strings from one garment to move them to another
  • Halakha does NOT follow Rabbi Shimon's lenient view on dragging (gerira)

Shmuel's View: Permitted

  • May light lamp-to-lamp
  • May move tzitzit strings between garments
  • Halakha DOES follow Rabbi Shimon on dragging — a bed, chair, or bench may be dragged if a furrow is not intended
Abaye's report: In every other matter Rabba ruled like Rav — except these three, where he ruled like Shmuel. The reason for Rav's lamp-to-lamp ban is disputed: contempt for the mitzva, or 'weakening' it by using its oil for another task.

🤔 Lighting or Placing — Which Accomplishes the Mitzva?

Tested against the Temple menorah's western lamp
❓ Dilemma: does lighting the lamp fulfill the mitzva, or does placing the already-lit lamp in position fulfill it?
↓
🕎 Objection from the Temple menorah's western lamp: since the lamps were fixed in place, priests had no choice but to light lamp-to-lamp with a wood chip — a problem either way
↓
Rav Pappa
Answers: the wicks were long enough to reach directly, lamp to lamp — no wood chip needed
Still difficult
For the view that lamp-to-lamp 'weakens the mitzva,' the question remains unresolved: קַשְׁיָא
↓
✅ Rav Huna son of Rabbi Yehoshua's rule: if lighting accomplishes the mitzva, one may light lamp-to-lamp; if placing does, one may not
Rava's proofs (examined next): Holding a lit lamp in one's hand accomplishes nothing (suggesting placing matters) — but this is explained away: onlookers assume he's just holding it for his own use, not yet performing the mitzva.
