📖 Overview: Threads, Snails, and When a Melacha Is Truly 'Finished'

Shabbat 75a–75b
הַמּוֹתֵחַ חוּט שֶׁל תְּפִירָה בְּשַׁבָּת חַיָּיב חַטָּאת
The scene: The daf opens with three unrelated teachings Rav Zutra bar Toviya transmits in Rav's name — on tightening a loose stitch, on learning from a Persian magosh, and on the obligation to calculate astronomical seasons — before returning to the mishna's list of primary labors. It works through the ḥilazon (the dye-snail), trapping and slaughtering an animal, and salting and tanning a hide, in each case asking exactly which melacha, or how many, the act violates. It then gathers several 'finishing' actions under the single category of striking the final hammer blow, closes out the mishna's exclusions, and introduces a new mishna: an item is liable for being carried out of the house only if it is the kind of item people typically bother to store.

🧵 Tightening a Stitch, Learning from a Magosh, and the Stars

Three teachings Rav Zutra bar Toviya transmits in the name of Rav
Rav's three rulings: One who TIGHTENS a stitching thread on Shabbat — pulling two separating parts of a garment back together — is liable to a sin-offering, since it is equivalent to sewing itself; just as, when a worm tore a curtain, people would tear it further so it could be sewn without the mend showing. One who learns even a single matter from a magosh (a Persian priest) is liable to death. And one who knows how to calculate astronomical seasons and constellations but does not — people should not even speak with him, for the Torah calls that calculation 'your wisdom and understanding in the eyes of the nations' (Deuteronomy 4:6). Rav and Shmuel disputed whether a magosh is a sorcerer or a heretic; since the Torah permits merely LEARNING about sorcery (only practicing it is forbidden), Rav's severe ruling proves he understood magosh as heretics.

🐚 Cracking Open a Ḥilazon — One Violation or Two?

Trapping the dye-snail, and whether cracking its shell is also 'threshing'
Baraita
One who traps a ḥilazon (the snail used for sky-blue dye) and cracks its shell for the blood is liable to only ONE sin-offering.
Rabbi Yehuda
Liable to TWO — trapping, and also threshing! Extracting what he wants from its shell is the same act as threshing grain from its husk.
The Rabbis
No — threshing applies only to produce that GROWS from the ground, not to a creature's shell.
Gemara
But cracking it open while it's alive kills it — shouldn't he also be liable for taking a life?
Rava
Exempt — he has no intent to kill it. He actually prefers it stay alive as long as possible, since dye from a live ḥilazon is finer. Since death works AGAINST his own interest, even Rabbi Shimon exempts him here.
Rabbi Yoḥanan's alternative: Perhaps the whole question is moot — the case may simply be one where the ḥilazon was already dead before its shell was cracked.

🔨 Striking the Final Blow — Six 'Finishing' Acts

Rabba and Rabbi Zeira's rule: any act that completes a piece of work counts as makeh b'patish
ActionLiable for...
Rubbing a hide smooth between the pillarsSmoothing (Rabbi Aḥa bar Ḥanina)
Planing the tops of posts evenCutting (in Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's name)
Spreading a bandage onto a woundSmoothing
Chiseling a stoneStriking the final blow
Engraving a figure, or blowing glass into shapeStriking the final blow (Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish)

Also on this daf: Writing one large letter where two could fit is exempt — only one letter was written. But ERASING one large letter to make room for two is liable: 'a greater stricture in erasing than in writing' (Rabbi Menaḥem, son of Rabbi Yosei).

🔪 Slaughtering and Salting — Which Melacha Applies?

Two disputes over what an act 'really' accomplishes

Slaughtering an Animal

  • Rav: liable for DYEING — a butcher wants the blood to pool visibly at the block, to draw customers to fresh meat
  • Shmuel: liable for TAKING A LIFE

Salting / Tanning a Hide

  • Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish: salting and tanning are really one labor — replace one with 'drafting' (marking cut-lines) to keep 39 distinct categories
  • Rabba bar Rav Huna: salting MEAT to preserve it is liable as tanning
  • Rava: there is no 'tanning' at all with regard to food

The Compromise

  • Rav Ashi: even Rabba bar Rav Huna meant only heavy salting to preserve meat for a journey — not ordinary salting to eat at home, since no one turns his own food into 'wood'

📦 A New Mishna: 'Fit to Store' Decides Who's Liable

Carrying an item into the public domain on Shabbat
ItemTypically stored?Who is liable for carrying it out?
An item generally worth savingYesAnyone who carries it out — ordinary sin-offering liability
An item too insignificant to bother storingNoOnly someone who personally stored THAT item — his own act shows he values it
Two views on 'not fit to store': Rav Pappa: the blood of a menstruating woman (though some hold it could still be saved to feed a cat). Mar Ukva: wood from a tree used for idol worship, which has no permitted value at all. Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina adds that this whole principle is NOT Rabbi Shimon's view — he holds the mishna's measures apply only to one who actually stored the item, regardless of common practice.
