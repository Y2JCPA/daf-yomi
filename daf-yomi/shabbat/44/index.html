📖 Overview: Set-Aside Lamps and Designated Objects

Shabbat 44a–44b
מְטַלְטְלִין נֵר חָדָשׁ אֲבָל לֹא יָשָׁן. רַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אוֹמֵר: כׇּל הַנֵּרוֹת מִטַּלְטְלִין, חוּץ מִן הַנֵּר הַדּוֹלֵק בְּשַׁבָּת
The scene: The daf opens by confirming the halakha follows Rabbi Yehuda ben Lakish, who permits rescuing a corpse from a fire, and by noting that Rabbi Shimon permits using oil left over in a lamp. The mishna then presents a core muktzeh dispute: may a used oil lamp be moved on Shabbat? Rabbi Yehuda forbids an old lamp as repulsive; Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Shimon forbid only a lamp that was lit that Shabbat, out of concern the flame might be disturbed; Rabbi Eliezer ben Rabbi Shimon is even more lenient. The Gemara maps each amora's position precisely, asks why a cup or bowl is stricter than a lamp, and closes with Rav's ruling — refined through objection — about a bed set aside for holding money.

🔥 Two Quick Rulings Before the Mishna

מוֹתַר הַשֶּׁמֶן שֶׁבַּנֵּר וְשֶׁבַּקְּעָרָה אָסוּר. וְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן מַתִּיר
Setting the halakha: Rabbi Yehuda ben Sheila said in the name of Rav Asi, in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: the halakha follows Rabbi Yehuda ben Lakish, who permits rescuing a corpse from a fire on Shabbat through an atypical, indirect method. Separately, a baraita teaches that the oil remaining in a lamp or bowl after use is forbidden — but Rabbi Shimon permits it, since in his view there is barely any prohibition of set-aside (muktzeh) at all.

The Tosefta expands the mishna's brief dispute into four distinct positions

👨‍👦 Abaye Maps Rabbi Eliezer ben Rabbi Shimon's View

He follows his father in one point, and disagrees in another
1️⃣ Agrees with his father, Rabbi Shimon: there is no general prohibition of set-aside (muktzeh) for lamps
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Shimon's own limit: only once the flame is extinguished may the lamp be moved — while lit, no
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Eliezer ben Rabbi Shimon disagrees here: even while the lamp is still burning, one may draw oil from it or move it
↓
4️⃣ His reasoning: doing so is in no way considered an act of extinguishing the flame
Why 'however' (aval)? Ulla explains the Tosefta's stricter clause about cup/bowl/lantern returns to Rabbi Yehuda's position; Mar Zutra rejects this and says it is Rabbi Shimon's own distinction between a small lamp he expects to go out and a large vessel he doesn't.

🍷 Why Are a Cup and Bowl Stricter Than a Lamp?

Ulla and Mar Zutra propose different explanations
Ulla
The Tosefta's phrase 'however, a cup, bowl, and lantern' switches back to explaining Rabbi Yehuda's stricter opinion, not Rabbi Shimon's.
Mar Zutra
That can't be right — Rabbi Yehuda already forbids moving any used lamp! Why would the word 'however' be needed to add a bowl?
Mar Zutra
Rather, it is Rabbi Shimon speaking: he permitted only a small lamp, since he already expects it to go out that day. A large cup, bowl, or lantern holds too much oil to expect it extinguished — he sets it aside for all of Shabbat.
Rabbi Zeira
A metal candlestick (pamot) lit on Shabbat: everyone agrees it is forbidden — since it was actually kindled, both those set-aside due to disgust and those set-aside due to prohibition apply.

💰 A Bed Designated for Money

Rav's ruling, challenged and refined through the mishna's own logic
📜 Rav (via Rav Yehuda): a bed designated to hold money may not be moved on Shabbat
↓
❓ Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak objects: our mishna permits moving a new lamp — even though it's designated for lighting! Shouldn't a bed, not even designated that way, be more lenient?
↓
Designated + money on it
Forbidden to move — even if the money is later removed, if it was there during twilight
Designated, no money now
Permitted to move, so long as no money was there at twilight (bein hashemashot)
↓
✅ Refined rule: the bed's status is fixed by whether money sat on it during twilight, not merely by its designation alone
