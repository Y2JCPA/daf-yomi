📖 Overview: Candelabra, Lamps, and the Limits of Muktzeh

Shabbat 46a–46b
אֵין מוּקְצֶה לְרַבִּי שִׁמְעוֹן אֶלָּא שֶׁמֶן שֶׁבַּנֵּר בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁהוּא דּוֹלֵק
The scene: The daf works through why a candelabrum made of separable joints may not be moved — concern that it will fall apart and be reassembled, itself a Torah-level act of construction — and extends this by rabbinic decree even to a small candelabrum merely decorated with grooves that resemble joints. A string of anecdotes shows individual Sages moving or refusing to move a lamp depending on whose locale they were in, out of deference to Rabbi Yochanan. The Gemara then debates whether a naphtha lamp, unlike an oil lamp, may be moved, with Rav Avya defending the lenient view to Rava using the concept of 'the legal status of a vessel' (torat keli). The daf closes by resolving an apparent contradiction: Rabbi Shimon permits leftover lamp oil but seems to treat an unexpectedly blemished firstborn animal as muktzeh.

🔧 A Candelabrum of Joints: Building by Accident

Why moving it risks an unintended act of construction
1️⃣ Abaye: the concern is a candelabrum made of actual removable joints — it might fall apart while being moved, and reassembling it is prohibited construction
↓
2️⃣ Question: if so, why does Reish Lakish permit moving it at all?
↓
3️⃣ Resolved: 'joints' really means grooves that merely resemble joints for decoration — nothing will actually fall apart
↓
4️⃣ Rule: a candelabrum with real joints — large or small — may never be moved; a large grooved one is forbidden too, by rabbinic decree, so people won't confuse it with the jointed kind
Where they disagree: Only for a small, merely-grooved candelabrum: Rabbi Yochanan decrees against it (fearing confusion with the large kind), while Reish Lakish permits it, since a small candelabrum is not typically made of true joints.

🕯️ Moving a Lamp — Depending on Whose House You're In

A string of anecdotes about deference, not disagreement
Gemara
Rav Malkiya moved a lamp at Rabbi Simlai's house and was scolded; Rabbi Yosei HaGlili did the same at Rabbi Yosei b'Rabbi Chanina's and was scolded too.
Gemara
But Rabbi Abbahu moved a lamp at Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's place, and refrained from doing so at Rabbi Yochanan's place!
Question
Whichever way he personally rules, shouldn't he act consistently everywhere?
Answer
Rabbi Abbahu actually held like Rabbi Shimon (lenient) throughout — but out of honor for Rabbi Yochanan, he did not act against his host's ruling in his own place.

🛢️ May a Naphtha Lamp Be Moved?

Rav Yehuda's stricter view against Rabba and Rav Yosef, tested by Rav Avya
⚖️ Rav Yehuda: an extinguished oil lamp may be moved; a naphtha lamp may not — its smell is unpleasant, so it has no other use
↓
💡 Rabba and Rav Yosef: even a naphtha lamp may be moved, since it is still suitable to cover another vessel
↓
❓ Rava challenges Rav Avya: if 'suitable to cover a vessel' is enough, then pebbles in the yard should also be movable for the same reason!
↓
✅ Rav Avya answers: a lamp already carries 'the legal status of a vessel' (torat keli), so a secondary use suffices to permit it; raw pebbles have no such status to begin with
Supporting proof: A baraita teaches that bracelets, nose-rings, and rings may be moved in a courtyard precisely because 'the legal status of a vessel applies to them' — Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak thanks God that Rava did not embarrass Rav Avya.

🐑 Reconciling Two Rulings of Rabbi Shimon

Leftover lamp oil is permitted, but a blemished firstborn seems forbidden — why?

Leftover Lamp Oil

  • Rabbi Shimon permits using it
  • A person sits and anticipates exactly when the lamp will go out
  • The remaining oil is expected in advance

A Firstborn's Blemish

  • Rabbi Shimon: if the blemish wasn't visible before the Festival, the animal is 'not among the prepared'
  • Full of uncertainty: will a blemish appear? Will it be permanent? Will a Sage examine it?
  • Because it's so unexpected, it never entered anyone's mind — hence set aside
Rabba's resolution: The cases differ in predictability, not principle — expected leftover oil is never truly set aside, while a wholly unanticipated blemish is. A parallel question about nullifying vows on Shabbat is resolved the same way.
