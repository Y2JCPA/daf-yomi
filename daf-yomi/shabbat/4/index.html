📖 Overview: Fruit, Bread, and the Size of a 'Place'

Shabbat 4a–4b
יָדוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם חֲשׁוּבָה לוֹ כְּאַרְבָּעָה עַל אַרְבָּעָה
The scene: Continuing from the previous daf's discussion of a fruit-filled hand, Rava asks Rav Nachman a sharper question: does it matter whether he returns his hand to the same courtyard or a different one? The Gemara then works through Rav Beivai bar Abaye's dilemma about bread stuck to an oven wall — testing whether it describes an unwitting or intentional violator, and whether the Sages would ever permit breaking one prohibition to avoid a worse one. The daf's second half turns to a technical question: the mishna requires that lifting and placing occur over an area of four by four handbreadths, yet the poor person's tiny hand is nowhere near that size. The Gemara resolves this through Rabbi Akiva's principle that an object caught in mid-air is considered as if it were placed, then explores whether the mishna might instead reflect Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's opinion.

Rava's follow-up dilemma to Rav Nachman
Where he returns his handRulingReason
Back to the SAME courtyard he stood inPermittedHis original plan — to move the fruit elsewhere — was never achieved; requiring him to bring it back is itself a kind of penalty
To a DIFFERENT courtyardProhibitedHis original plan WAS achieved — he actually relocated the fruit, so the Sages forbid completing the transfer there
Rav Nachman's quip: When Rava pressed for the logic behind the distinction, Rav Nachman answered wryly, 'By the time you finish eating a kor of salt over it, you'll understand' — before giving the real answer above.

🔥 Bread Stuck to the Oven

Rav Beivai bar Abaye's dilemma, worked out step by step
1️⃣ Dilemma: one who stuck bread to the wall of a heated oven on Shabbat — may he remove it before it fully bakes, to avoid liability?
↓
2️⃣ If he remains unaware of his error, no one could ever ask this question — so he must have later remembered
↓
3️⃣ But if he remembered while it was still baking, a known rule exempts him already (liability requires unwitting action from start to finish) — so the case must involve someone who acted intentionally
↓
4️⃣ Rav Sheila proposed instead: still unwitting, but the dilemma is whether others may remove it for him — Rav Sheshet objected: do we tell a person to sin so his friend can benefit?
↓
5️⃣ Rav Ashi's resolution: the man acted intentionally, and the real question is whether he may remove the bread before he becomes liable to stoning — the Sages permitted it
Rav Acha's version: Rav Acha, son of Rava, taught this not as an open dilemma but as a settled ruling: one who stuck bread to the oven may remove it before incurring liability to stoning.

📏 Why Is the Poor Person Liable With Such a Small Hand?

Lifting and placing normally require a surface of four by four handbreadths
❓ A valid act of lifting or placing needs a surface of four by four handbreadths — but a hand is far smaller. Why is the poor person liable in the mishna at all?
↓
💡 Rabba: this mishna follows Rabbi Akiva, who holds that placing does NOT require an area of four by four handbreadths
↓
📜 Source: one who throws an object from a private domain, through the public domain, to another private domain
↓
Rabbi Akiva: liable
קְלוּטָה כְּמִי שֶׁהוּנְּחָה — an object caught in a domain's airspace is considered as if placed there, even without a 4×4 surface
The Rabbis: exempt
The object merely passed through and never truly came to rest — no valid placing occurred
↓
✅ Since Rabbi Akiva needs no defined surface for placing, even a hand can serve as a valid place of placing
Term: קְלוּטָה כְּמִי שֶׁהוּנְּחָה — 'caught [in the air] is as if it had been placed.'

🌳 Whose Opinion Is It, Really?

Rav Yosef proposes a different source than Rabbi Akiva
Gemara
Even granting Rabbi Akiva's principle — that shows only that placing needs no 4×4 space. Perhaps lifting still requires it!
Rav Yosef
Rather — whose opinion is the mishna following? It is Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's.
Gemara
Which ruling of his? The one where an object lands on a projection (זִיז) of any size, and he deems the thrower liable?
Gemara
No — that baraita is a special case: a tree in the private domain whose branches lean into the public domain. Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi holds 'cast its boughs after its trunk' — the branch counts as the private-domain trunk itself.
Gemara
Rather, it must be this ruling: one who throws from public domain to public domain with a private domain in between — Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi holds him liable twice, once for carrying in and once for carrying out!
Rav Yehuda in Shmuel's name: Since Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi imposes two separate liabilities on that single throw, neither the lifting nor the placing in his view requires an area of four by four handbreadths.

🔍 Two Candidate Sources for 'No 4×4 Required'

The Gemara tests, and rejects, one ruling of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi before settling on another

Rabbi Akiva

  • קְלוּטָה כְּמִי שֶׁהוּנְּחָה — airspace counts as placed
  • Source: throwing private→public→private
  • Directly explains the mishna's hand

Rejected: the Tree Branch

  • Object lands on a branch leaning into the public domain
  • Explained instead by 'cast its boughs after its trunk'
  • Doesn't actually prove 'no 4×4 needed'

Accepted: Double Liability

  • Public → private (middle) → public domain, one throw
  • Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi: liable twice, for carrying in AND out
  • Proves neither lifting nor placing needs a defined area
Still open: The Gemara is about to test whether Rav and Shmuel actually agree with this reading of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's position.
