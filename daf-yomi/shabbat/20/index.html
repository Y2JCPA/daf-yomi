📖 Overview: From Ben Drosai to a New Chapter

Shabbat 20a–20b
בַּמֶּה מַדְלִיקִין וּבַמָּה אֵין מַדְלִיקִין
The scene: The daf opens by quantifying the mishna's requirement that food be roasted 'while it is still day': Rav's standard is the partial roasting of the outlaw ben Drosai, who could never wait for food to fully cook. The Gemara then works out how much of a bonfire — or a single branch, or bundled reeds and seeds — must have caught fire before Shabbat, weighing Rav's and Shmuel's competing standards and testing them against four kinds of fuel (pitch, sulfur, cheese, fat) that ignite so easily no threshold is needed. This closes the first chapter, Yetziot HaShabbat, with the formal 'Hadran' marker. A new mishna and chapter, Bameh Madlikin, then opens with a detailed list of wicks and oils disqualified for the Shabbat lamp — the topic the Gemara will spend the next several dafim identifying and explaining.

🍖 How Much Roasting Counts as 'Done'?

The standard for letting food finish cooking after Shabbat begins
1️⃣ Rav (via Rabbi Elazar): sufficiently roasted means like the food of ben Drosai — a fugitive who never had time to fully cook his food, so 'partially roasted'
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Yochanan adds: once food reaches that stage by a Jew, a gentile finishing the cooking afterward creates no problem of 'gentile-cooked food'
↓
3️⃣ Chananya: once food is cooked like ben Drosai's, it may be kept on a stove on Shabbat even if the stove is not swept of coals or covered with ash
↓
4️⃣ Practical result: 'ben Drosai' becomes the recurring halakhic threshold for 'no longer a concern that someone will stoke the coals'
Same word, resolved question: The Gemara also settles a dilemma about the mishna's phrase 'until the bottom crust forms': it means the surface stuck to the oven wall, not the side nearest the fire — proven from Rabbi Eliezer's own more explicit wording.

🪵 What Fraction of the Wood Must Have Caught Fire?

Rival standards for reeds, seeds, and single branches
MaterialStandardSource / Reasoning
Loose reedsNo majority neededRav Huna: reeds catch easily on their own
Bundled reedsMajority must catchOnce tied, they behave like a solid beam
Loose date seedsNo majority neededBurn easily when scattered
Seeds in woven basketsMajority must catch (Rav Kahana reverses Rav Huna's cases)Rav Chisda: scattered items actually burn worse, not better
A single branchMost of its thickness AND its circumference (Rav Pappa, combining both versions of Rav's ruling)Ambiguity in how 'most' was transmitted led to the stricter combined standard
Four exceptions: Rav Yosef taught that bonfires of pitch, sulfur, dry cheese, or fatty material never need a majority to catch — and a further baraita adds straw and field rakings, since all of these ignite instantly once touched by flame.

🔥 Rav vs. Shmuel: What Counts as 'Most of the Bonfire'?

Two very different tests for the same mishna phrase
Rav
'Most of it' means most of the thickness of each individual branch in the pile must have caught fire.
Shmuel
It's enough that the fire has taken hold well enough that no one would say, 'Bring thinner branches and put them underneath to help it along.'
Rav Chiyya
Support for Shmuel, from the Temple candelabrum: it must be lit so the flame rises on its own, not because something else is propping it up.
Gemara
This mirrors a tannaitic dispute: Rabbi Chiyya says wood counts as 'burnt' once ruined for a craftsman's use; Rabbi Yehuda ben Beteira requires fire on both sides — with a scriptural allusion from Ezekiel 15:4.
The stakes: If not enough of the bonfire has caught before Shabbat, one might be tempted to stoke or rearrange the wood on Shabbat itself — a real melacha.

🎬 Hadran — The First Chapter Ends

בַּמֶּה מַדְלִיקִין וּבַמָּה אֵין מַדְלִיקִין
With the discussion of Babylonian cedar wood and hyssop needing no majority to catch fire, the Gemara reaches הדרן עלך יציאות השבת — 'we shall return to you, Yetziot HaShabbat' — the formal close of tractate Shabbat's first chapter, which opened with the laws of carrying objects between domains on Shabbat eve. A new mishna immediately opens the second chapter, Bameh Madlikin ('With what may one kindle'), turning from carrying to the laws of the Shabbat lamp.

🧵 The New Mishna's Two Lists: Forbidden Wicks and Forbidden Oils

Materials that may not be used to light the Shabbat lamp

Forbidden Wicks

  • Lekhesh — the woolly substance under cedar bark
  • Chosen — flax stalk that was crushed but not yet combed
  • Kalach — the cocoon of a silkworm (Rav Yitzchak bar Ze'ira)
  • Petilat ha'idan (willow) and petilat hamidbar (mullein) — both burn poorly
  • Green moss floating on the surface of standing water

Forbidden Oils

  • Zefet (pitch) and sha'ava (wax)
  • Shemen kik — oil from the castor-like plant of Jonah
  • Shemen sereifa — 'burnt oil,' impure teruma oil
  • Elya (tail fat) and chelev (forbidden animal fat)
  • Nachum HaMadi permits boiled chelev; the Sages disagree entirely
Why they fail: Wool and hair were added by a later baraita, though the mishna's own tanna didn't bother — wool shrinks and hair scorches when burned, so no one would think to try them as wicks anyway.
