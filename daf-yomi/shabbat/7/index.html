📖 Overview: The Textures of Public Space

Shabbat 7a–7b
אֵין כַּרְמְלִית פְּחוּתָה מֵאַרְבָּעָה, וְתוֹפֶסֶת עַד עֲשָׂרָה
The scene: Daf 7 examines the fine grain of what makes a space public, private, or something in between. It opens with the karpef, a large fenced enclosure that is a genuine Torah-level private domain despite the Sages treating it more strictly because no one lives there, and works outward from there to marginal urban spaces — a street corner, the gap between pillars, a merchant's bench — each a karmelit because its use, though real, is inconvenient. The Gemara then measures exactly how tall and how wide a protrusion in the public domain must be to count as part of it, sets the outer boundaries of karmelit status itself, and closes with Abaye's ruling about digging out a pit to complete a low-ceilinged house into a full private domain, and the resulting dispute over whether 'holes' in the public domain share its status.

🌾 The Karpef: A True Private Domain, Restricted Anyway

A large fenced enclosure not built for residence

By Torah Law

  • A karpef larger than a beit se'atayim, fenced but not built for residence — even the size of a kor or two
  • One who throws an object into it from the public domain is liable, because it has real partitions

By Rabbinic Law

  • Because it lacks residents, the Sages restricted carrying inside it as though it were a karmelit
  • This rabbinic leniency does not undo its Torah-level status as a private domain

A Reading Dispute

  • Ulla explains the mishna's 'valley' via this karpef concept — but then why would the mishna call it a 'valley' rather than an enclosure?
  • Rav Ashi instead reads the mishna's own words literally: it says 'private domain,' so he needs no such explanation
Underlying idea: Partitions create a private domain by Torah law regardless of whether anyone lives there — but the Sages still layer on restrictions where ordinary residential use is missing.

🧱 What Counts as Public Domain?

Measuring exactly how a protrusion in the street is treated
1️⃣ An upright brick in the public domain: an object thrown and stuck to its side makes one liable — the side is treated as part of the public domain itself
↓
2️⃣ An object landing atop the brick is exempt, so long as the brick is at least three handbreadths high — the multitudes don't step on top of it
↓
3️⃣ Thorns and shrubs are never part of the public domain, even under three handbreadths high, since people avoid walking on them
↓
4️⃣ Feces in the roadway: disputed — Chiyya bar Rav says it is not part of the public domain since people avoid it; Rav Ashi says it IS, since people rushing to work step on it anyway
Underlying test: Every case turns on the same question: does the ordinary crowd actually tread there, or not?

🕳️ Holes of a Domain

Abaye's dug-out pit, and a dispute over holes in the public domain
Abaye
If one digs a pit of four by four handbreadths in the floor of that roofless house, completing its depth to ten handbreadths, the WHOLE house becomes fit for carrying.
Gemara
Why? Because the dug-out spot is itself a private domain, and the rest of the house becomes its 'holes' (חוֹרֵי רְשׁוּת הַיָּחִיד) — and everyone agrees holes of a private domain share its status.
Abaye
The same logic should extend to holes of the public domain — treat them as public domain too.
Rava
No — holes of the public domain are NOT like the public domain itself. Its holes are governed by whether their use is convenient, the same test that makes a street corner only a karmelit.
Rava's consistency: This matches his earlier reasoning — a corner adjacent to the public domain stays a karmelit specifically because its use is inconvenient, proving that mere proximity to RH does not automatically confer RH status.
