📖 Overview: Reused Vessels, Gold Tiaras, and a Woman's Ring

Shabbat 59a–59b
לֹא תֵּצֵא אִשָּׁה בְּעִיר שֶׁל זָהָב, וְאִם יָצְתָה חַיֶּיבֶת חַטָּאת
The scene: The daf opens by resolving the previous amud's question — why a bell whose clapper was removed keeps its earlier ritual impurity — by weighing whether its reuse (for sound versus for water) must resemble its original function, a principle then tested on a metal animal-shoe repurposed by a fleeing soldier. The mishna's discussion then turns to what a woman may wear into the public domain on Shabbat: an 'ir shel zahav', a gold ornament engraved with the image of Jerusalem like the one Rabbi Akiva made his wife, and whether it is a forbidden burden or a permitted ornament. The Gemara works through parallel disputes about the kelila (a tiara) and the kamra (a belt), and closes by asking why a ring with an engraved seal is a burden for a woman while a plain ring is an ornament.

🔔 Why a Silent Bell Stays Impure

Two amoraim, two reasons — and a swap to resolve a contradiction
Gemara
A bell whose clapper was removed still carries its earlier impurity. It was stated: Rabbi Yosei bar Rabbi Ḥanina and Rabbi Yoḥanan each gave a reason.
Reason A
Since it is still fit to be struck against earthenware, producing a similar sound
Reason B
Since it is still fit to give a child water to drink
Gemara
But we know from elsewhere — the case of a metal animal-shoe — that Rabbi Yoḥanan requires a new use to resemble the vessel's original use (מֵעֵין מְלָאכָה רִאשׁוֹנָה). Giving a child water has nothing to do with ringing!
Resolution
So reverse the attributions: it was Rabbi Yoḥanan who said 'fit to strike on earthenware' — sound-producing, matching the bell's original use — and Rabbi Yosei bar Rabbi Ḥanina who permitted the unrelated water-use.
Why swap here and not elsewhere: The sandal-of-an-animal case (next slide) independently proves Rabbi Yoḥanan requires a same-type reuse — so this is the dispute that must be corrected.

🥾 The Metal Animal-Shoe — Fit for a Person How?

A shoe an animal can no longer use remains impure only if a person can reuse it
SageNew use for a personWhere the difference shows
RavDrinking water from it during a warIf the shoe is dirty and repulsive, no one would drink from it — it becomes pure
Rabbi ḤaninaSmearing oil on the body during a warStays impure even if dirty — one still uses a grimy vessel to smear oil
Rabbi YoḥananStrapping it to his own foot while fleeing battle, to run over thornsIf the shoe is too heavy to wear, it becomes pure, though it would still hold oil
The point: Only Rabbi Yoḥanan's answer keeps the SAME kind of use — footwear. That is the proof that he alone requires the new use to resemble the original one.

👑 City of Gold — Ornament or Burden?

A gold tiara engraved with Jerusalem, 'like the one Rabbi Akiva made for his wife'

Rabbi Meir

  • It is a burden, not an ornament
  • Going out with it makes one liable to a sin-offering

The Rabbis

  • It IS an ornament — no sin-offering if she goes out
  • Still forbidden ab initio: she might remove it to show a friend and come to carry it

Rabbi Eliezer

  • Permitted even ab initio
  • Only an important woman wears a city of gold, and an important woman does not remove her ornaments to show them off

🎀 Kelila and Kamra — One Ruling, Two Towns Respond

A tiara and a belt, argued twice over and then put into practice
1️⃣ Rav forbids going out with a kelila (a tiara-like ornament); Shmuel permits it
↓
2️⃣ First version: all agree a fully metal kelila is forbidden; they disagree only about one woven with metal — Rav treats the metal as primary (forbidden), Shmuel the fabric (permitted)
↓
3️⃣ Rav Ashi reverses it: all agree a purely woven kelila is permitted; they disagree only about a metal one — fear she'll remove and carry it (Rav) vs. only an 'important woman' wears one and won't (Shmuel)
↓
4️⃣ When Levi taught 'the kelila is permitted' in Neharde'a, 24 women went out wearing one; when Rabba bar Avuh taught the same in Meḥoza, 18 went out from a single alleyway
↓
5️⃣ Related: a gilded kamra (belt) is permitted, like a king's belt; but two belts worn together are not — one becomes a mere burden; a short cloak needs straps or it is forbidden

💍 A Ring's Status — Sealed vs. Plain

The same mishna's ring-rule seems to contradict a mishna in Kelim
ItemOur mishna (Shabbat)Mishna in Kelim
Ring without a sealAn ornament — forbidden to go out with it, but exempt if she doesImpure — counted as a woman's ornament
Ring with an engraved sealA burden — liable to a sin-offering if she goes out with itImpure — no distinction made from a plain ring
Rabbi Zeira resolves it: Our mishna follows Rabbi Neḥemya, who says 'for a ring, follow its seal' (and for a yoke, 'follow its rods') — a sealed ring is a man's tool, so for a woman it is a burden. The Kelim mishna follows the Rabbis, who look only at the ring's own primary material.
