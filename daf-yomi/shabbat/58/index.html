📖 Overview: Slaves' Seals, Animals' Bells, and What Makes a Vessel Impure

Shabbat 58a–58b
יוֹצֵא הָעֶבֶד בְּחוֹתָם שֶׁבְּצַוָּארוֹ אֲבָל לֹא בְּחוֹתָם שֶׁבִּכְסוּתוֹ
The scene: The daf continues the mishna's list of items one may not wear into the public domain on Shabbat, focusing on a slave's identifying seal (chotam). The Gemara reconciles Shmuel's ruling — permitted around the neck, forbidden on the clothes — with contradictory baraitot, first via who made the seal and then via whether it is metal or clay. It contrasts this with an animal, for which any such item is a burden rather than an ornament and is always forbidden. The discussion then turns to ritual impurity: a bell (zog) is a 'vessel' only when it has a clapper (inbal) that lets it sound, a principle traced to a verse about metal implements. The daf closes with Abaye and Rava disputing why a bell whose clapper was removed keeps its earlier impurity, drawing in the laws of jointed tools like scissors and a carpenter's plane.

🔏 Shmuel's Seal — Neck Yes, Clothes No

Two contradictions, two resolutions
Gemara
Shmuel: a slave may go out with a seal שֶׁבְּצַוָּארוֹ (around his neck) but not one שֶׁבִּכְסוּתוֹ (on his clothes). Yet a baraita forbids both!
Answer 1
Not difficult: the baraita permitting the neck-seal means his master made it — the slave won't dare remove it. The baraita forbidding it means he made it himself, so he might remove and carry it.
Gemara
Then why forbid a master-made seal on the clothes? He wouldn't remove that one either!
Answer 1
It might snap off; in fear of his master he'd fold his cloak onto his shoulders to hide the gap — and a folded cloak carried on the shoulders makes one liable for a sin-offering (Rabbi Yoḥanan).
Alt. view
Rav Naḥman (Rabba bar Avuh): both baraitot speak of a master-made seal. The real difference is metal (forbidden — the master is particular about it) versus clay (permitted — he is not).
Proof
The baraita adds 'both cannot become impure' — that only teaches something novel for metal seals, since stone, dung, and unfired earthenware never contract impurity to begin with.
Conclusion: The metal/clay reading wins: it alone explains why the baraita bothers to note that these seals cannot become ritually impure.

🐴 What May Go Out on Shabbat — Person vs. Animal

The same items, different rules
ItemOn a slave's neckOn a slave's clothesOn an animal
Seal (chotam)Permitted — fear of his master keeps it onForbidden — might snap and be carriedForbidden — always a burden, never an ornament
Bell (zog)Forbidden — might snap and be picked upPermitted — 'anything woven into the garment, the Sages did not decree against' (Rav Huna b. Rav Yehoshua)Forbidden — same reasoning as the animal's seal
Why animals differ: For a person a seal or bell can be an ornament; for an animal, the Gemara says, these items are considered burdens it carries, not adornments — so they are barred outright, regardless of the neck/clothes distinction that applies to people.

🔔 A Bell Is Only a 'Vessel' If It Can Sound

The clapper (inbal) decides ritual impurity

Has a Clapper

  • Bells for a mortar, a cradle, Torah-scroll mantles, children's coverings — impure
  • A person's bell — impure
  • Derived from Numbers 31:23: 'every davar' includes dibbur (something that 'speaks') — a metal sound-making vessel is impure (R' Shmuel bar Naḥmani, R' Yonatan)

No Clapper

  • Child's bell — pure; it exists only to make sound, so silent = not a vessel
  • Mortar/cradle/mantle bells — pure

Exception

  • An adult's bell — impure even with no clapper, since for an adult it is an ornament in itself
  • Once impure, removing the clapper does NOT purify it (see next slide)

🚪 A Door's Bell, an Animal's Bell

Vessels fall into impurity by thought, but rise out only through an actual change
❓ Baraita: a door's bell is pure (it is like the house — attached to the ground); an animal's bell is impure. But another source calls an animal's bell pure!
↓
🔑 Resolved: that second source means a bell with no clapper — silent bells are not vessels at all
↓
Door's bell → made into an animal's bell
Becomes impure — it now has a new, sound-producing use
Animal's bell → made into a door's bell
STAYS impure, even nailed permanently into the door
↓
📜 Principle: כׇּל הַכֵּלִים יוֹרְדִין לִידֵי טוּמְאָתָן בְּמַחְשָׁבָה, וְאֵין עוֹלִין מִידֵּי טוּמְאָתָן אֶלָּא בְּשִׁנּוּי מַעֲשֶׂה — vessels FALL into impurity through mere intent (designation); they RISE out of it only through an actual physical change

🛠️ A Clapperless Bell — Still Impure?

Abaye and Rava debate a detached part
1️⃣ Tosefta: bells for a mortar, cradle, Torah-scroll mantle, or children's covering — if the clapper is later removed, the bell's earlier impurity remains
↓
2️⃣ Question: without a clapper it seems useless — why isn't it simply 'broken' and purified?
↓
3️⃣ Abaye: an ordinary person, not only a craftsman, can pop the clapper back in — so it is never truly 'broken'
↓
4️⃣ Rava objects, from a baraita: jointed scissors and a detachable plane-blade are a 'connection' לַטּוּמְאָה (for impurity) but NOT a connection לַהַזָּאָה (for sprinkling the purification waters) — whichever way that word is read, Abaye's bell case does not match
↓
5️⃣ Rabba: by Torah law, parts are 'connected' only while assembled and in use; the Sages then fenced in both directions — always connected for impurity, never connected (even in use) for sprinkling. But this leaves Abaye's bare, unused bell unexplained — so Rava sets out to answer differently, continuing onto the next amud
