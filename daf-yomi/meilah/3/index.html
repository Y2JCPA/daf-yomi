📖 Overview: Refining the Rules of Me'ilah

Meilah 3a–3b
זְרִיקַת פִּיגּוּל אֵינוֹ מוֹצִיא מִידֵי מְעִילָה בְּקׇדְשֵׁי קָדָשִׁים, וְאֵינוֹ מֵבִיא לִידֵי מְעִילָה בְּקָדָשִׁים קַלִּים
The scene: The daf first explains why Ulla's rule about sacrificial animals that died was necessary even though a similar case (sin offerings left to die) was already known — the difference is whether people distance themselves from the animal during its lifetime or only afterward. Rav Yosef then marshals three mishnayot about disqualified bird offerings to conclusively refute Rabba's view that disqualified offerings must descend from the altar. The Gemara turns to Rabbi Elazar's teaching that a personal-altar offering brought into the Temple courtyard is 'absorbed' by its partitions, and his unresolved dilemma about whether such an offering, if later disqualified, must descend from the altar. The daf closes with Rav Giddel's rule about piggul intent during blood-sprinkling and its effect on me'ilah, tested against a mishna about the thanks offering's loaves.

💀 Two Kinds of 'Distancing' From a Dead Offering

Why the Sages needed Ulla's rule even for ordinary sacrificial animals that died
CaseDo People Distance Themselves?Need for Rabbinic Me'ilah
Sin offering disqualified while alive (e.g. its owner atoned differently)Yes, already in its lifetime — it looks unfitNo decree needed — no one benefits from it anyway
Sin offering fit in life, then diesNo — it looked fine until it diedDecree needed to stop casual misuse
Other offerings of the most sacred order that dieNo — not brought for atonement, so people assume no reason to avoid themUlla's decree: still needed, since they look like valid offerings
The logic: Rabbinic me'ilah is instituted precisely where people would NOT naturally avoid a disqualified offering — since it still looks legitimate, a decree is needed to prevent casual misuse.

🐦 Rav Yosef's Three-Mishna Proof Against Rabba

Chaining sources to show a disqualified offering that ascended the altar does not descend
1️⃣ Mishna (Zevaḥim 66b): bird offerings disqualified by wrong-place rites don't impurify the throat when swallowed, and ARE subject to me'ilah
↓
2️⃣ Mishna (Zevaḥim 68b): 'whose disqualification occurred in the sacred area' likewise doesn't impurify the throat — so the first mishna's birds are cases of disqualification 'in the sacred area'
↓
3️⃣ Mishna (Zevaḥim 84a): anything disqualified 'in the sacred area' — if it ascended the altar, it does NOT descend
↓
4️⃣ Combine all three: these birds, disqualified in the sacred area, are subject to me'ilah AND do not descend if they ascended
↓
5️⃣ תְּיוּבְתָּא — conclusive refutation of Rabba, who held disqualified offerings must always descend
Result: Rav Yosef wins this exchange: an offering disqualified inside the Temple courtyard, once it ascends the altar, stays there — supporting his view that the mishna's me'ilah could be Torah-law, not merely rabbinic.

🏛️ Rabbi Elazar's Dilemma: A Personal Altar's Offering in the Temple

What happens when an offering meant for a private bamah enters the Temple courtyard?
R' Elazar
קְלָטוּהָ מְחִיצוֹת לְכׇל דָּבָר — a burnt offering consecrated for a personal altar that one brought into the Temple courtyard is 'absorbed' by the courtyard's partitions for every purpose, taking on full Temple-offering status.
R' Elazar asks
If that offering is then disqualified — say, slaughtered in the south — and its limbs ascend the altar, what is the halakha: do they descend?
Gemara
Perhaps even Rabba would agree here they descend only if the owner intended to sacrifice it properly within the courtyard; had he intended a personal altar, slaughtering in the south wouldn't fully disqualify it, so it stays.
Gemara
Or perhaps even Rav Yosef would agree it descends here — since it was never properly admitted by the courtyard's partitions in the first place, unlike an offering consecrated for the Temple.
Conclusion
תֵּיקוּ — the question stands unresolved.
Note: Rabbi Elazar's second question is asked 'one dilemma from another' — it doesn't reveal which side of the Rabba/Rav Yosef debate he holds for the standard case.

🚫 Rav Giddel's Rule: Piggul Intent at Sprinkling

The blood-sprinkling stage governs whether piggul removes or creates me'ilah

Offerings of the Most Sacred Order

  • Piggul intent at sprinkling does NOT remove the meat from me'ilah
  • Reason: no valid sprinkling ever permitted the priests to eat it
  • It remains 'the sacred items of the Lord' (Leviticus 5:15)

Offerings of Lesser Sanctity

  • Piggul intent at sprinkling does NOT bring the sacrificial portions into me'ilah
  • Only a VALID sprinkling confers me'ilah status on lesser offerings
  • Without proper sprinkling, no me'ilah applies to them at all
Rav Giddel said Rav said: In both directions, an invalid (piggul) sprinkling changes nothing about me'ilah — sacred-order offerings stay subject to it, lesser offerings stay exempt from it.

📜 Rav Pappa's Objection From the Thanks-Offering Loaves

Does piggul at slaughter alone create me'ilah for offerings of lesser sanctity?
❓ Mishna (Menaḥot 78b): thanks offering slaughtered chutz lizmano/limkomo — the loaves ARE sanctified and subject to me'ilah
↓
⚡ Rav Pappa to Abaye: this contradicts Rav Giddel — piggul intent DOES bring lesser-sanctity offerings into me'ilah! Abaye is silent.
↓
💡 Rabbi Abba's resolution: the mishna's piggul intent was at slaughter only, not at sprinkling — sprinkling was proper, so me'ilah applies normally
↓
🔄 Rav Ashi challenges Rava: but Ulla says a piggul handful (komeitz) burned on the altar loses its piggul status — and komeitz = slaughter!
↓
✅ Rava: Ulla meant only a prohibited act occurred, not full piggul — full piggul status still needs improper intent at the offering-up stage too
Bottom line: Piggul intent limited to the slaughter stage, without matching intent at sprinkling, does not remove an offering from ordinary me'ilah rules.
