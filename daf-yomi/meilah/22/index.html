📖 Overview: One Last Challenge, and the Tractate's Close

Meilah 22a
הַדְרָן עֲלָךְ הַשָּׁלִיחַ שֶׁעָשָׂה שְׁלִיחוּתוֹ וּסְלִיקָא לַהּ מַסֶּכֶת מְעִילָה
The scene: Rav Pappa offers a third version of Reish Lakish's challenge to Rabbi Yoḥanan, this time from a mishna in Demai about wine purchased from Samaritans before Shabbat: Rabbi Meir permits designating future portions as teruma and tithes and drinking immediately, relying on that future separation, while Rabbi Yehuda, Rabbi Yosei, and Rabbi Shimon forbid it. If Rabbi Meir's retroactive designation is valid, shouldn't the mishna about a consecrated peruta lost somewhere in a purse likewise allow drinking down to the last coin without concern, the way the earlier version about bulls assumed the finer of two animals was meant? Rabbi Yoḥanan again resolves the difficulty the same way as before, showing the mishna's language about 'this purse will not be exempt from consecration' picks out the specific, final coin. With that, the daf closes with the formula ending tractate Meilah.

🍷 Rabbi Meir's Method for Samaritan Wine

Designating future separations to drink immediately, before Shabbat

🚫 Does Retroactive Designation Really Work?

Rav Pappa's version of Reish Lakish's challenge to Rabbi Yoḥanan
Rav Pappa (for Reish Lakish)
Rabbi Meir permits drinking now and separating teruma/tithes LATER, relying on a future designation — doesn't that prove retroactive designation works generally? If so, why does the mishna about a lost consecrated peruta require spending down the WHOLE purse, rather than simply relying on a future separation?
Implicit reply
Even Rabbi Meir's own method only works when he specifies 'that which I will separate in the FUTURE' — a concrete, upcoming act. In the purse case, by contrast, the consecrated coin is already unidentifiably mixed in NOW — there's no future act of separation to rely on.
R' Yoḥanan's resolution
The mishna's latter clause is understood through its specific wording: 'this purse will not be exempt from consecration' — meaning the person intended the very LAST coin remaining to be the consecrated one, not an as-yet-undetermined future portion.

🔑 The Wording That Resolves Everything

'This purse will not be exempt from consecration'
סֵיפָא דְּמַתְנִיתִין בְּאוֹמֵר ״לֹא יִפָּטֵר כִּיס זֶה מִן הַהֶקְדֵּשׁ״
The key phrase: Rabbi Yoḥanan resolves each version of Reish Lakish's challenge — whether comparing the purse mishna to bulls, to log-measures of wine, or now to the Demai teaching on Samaritan wine — the same way. When the mishna's latter clause says one is not liable for me'ilah until the ENTIRE purse is spent, it is describing someone who explicitly framed his consecration as 'this purse will not be exempt' — a phrase that identifies the last, final coin as the consecrated one, regardless of when it turns up. That is fundamentally different from an open-ended 'the consecrated coin is somewhere in here.'

⚖️ Two Cases, One Real Difference

Why Rabbi Meir's wine ruling doesn't actually contradict the purse mishna

Samaritan Wine (R' Meir)

  • Declares a SPECIFIC future act: 'the two log I will separate LATER'
  • The designation names a concrete quantity, to be actualized going forward
  • Relies on a real future separation — a defined, upcoming event

The Purse (Rabbis, first clause)

  • 'A peruta IN this purse is consecrated' — no specific coin identified
  • Any coin spent might be THE coin — no future act pending
  • Liability begins with the first coin spent, per the Rabbis
Where they converge: Only when the purse-case wording shifts to 'this purse will not be exempt' does it functionally resemble Rabbi Meir's case — designating the LAST remaining item, much like designating a specific future portion.

🏁 Tractate Meilah Concludes

From the Temple altar to a coin in an agent's pocket
1️⃣ Opened with offerings disqualified by place or time, and Rabbi Yehoshua's principle: a 'period of fitness for the priests' removes an item from me'ilah
↓
2️⃣ Traced me'ilah through blood, libations, ashes, milk, eggs, and produce — precisely defining where consecration's reach begins and ends
↓
3️⃣ Established which items combine to reach the liability threshold, and clarified when damage, benefit, or both are required
↓
4️⃣ Closed with the law of agency — who bears liability when a messenger acts faithfully, partially, or not at all
The closing formula: הַדְרָן עֲלָךְ הַשָּׁלִיחַ שֶׁעָשָׂה שְׁלִיחוּתוֹ וּסְלִיקָא לַהּ מַסֶּכֶת מְעִילָה — 'We shall return to you, [the chapter of] the agent who performed his agency, and thus concludes tractate Meilah.'
