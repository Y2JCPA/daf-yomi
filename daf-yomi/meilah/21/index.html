📖 Overview: The Fine Grain of Agency Law

Meilah 21a–21b
דְּבָרִים שֶׁבַּלֵּב אֵינָם דְּבָרִים
The scene: The daf works through an escalating series of agency scenarios to pin down exactly when the homeowner or the agent bears liability for me'ilah. A literal instruction, obeyed literally, binds the homeowner even if his unspoken intent differed — 'matters of the heart are not matters.' Sending consecrated funds through someone legally incapable of agency (a deaf-mute, imbecile, or minor) still works, through a creative comparison to olives releasing beneficial moisture. The daf then walks through cases of partial fulfillment — half the instructions followed, half not — testing when both parties, one party, or neither becomes liable, and closes with a mishna on money deposited with a money changer versus a homeowner, and the classic dispute between Rabbi Akiva and the Rabbis over a stray consecrated coin lost in one's purse.

🐒 Can People Who Can't Be Agents Still Act as Agents?

Explaining how a deaf-mute, imbecile, or minor can still trigger the homeowner's liability
❓ A deaf-mute, imbecile, or minor legally CANNOT be formally appointed as an agent — so how can sending consecrated money through them make the HOMEOWNER liable when they succeed?
↓
🫒 Rabbi Elazar: like the olive-press vat, where beneficial moisture is treated as occurring with the owner's 'intent' simply because it serves his purpose — so too here, since the outcome suits the homeowner, it's treated as his own act
↓
🐘 Rabbi Yoḥanan's parallel: like placing an eiruv on a monkey or trained elephant that carries it to the right spot — the animal 'performs' the agency though it cannot legally be an agent
↓
✅ Either way: successful, purpose-serving completion of the task is enough to attribute the result to the homeowner — legal agency isn't strictly required

🍋 Partial Fulfillment: Who's Liable for What?

Half-instructions, split purchases, and Rabbi Yehuda's dissent
InstructionWhat the Agent BroughtOutcome
Half a peruta for lamps, half for wicksAll lamps, or all wicksNEITHER liable — instructions followed for only half a peruta each way
All of a peruta for lamps (or wicks) from ONE place eachLamps from the wick-place, wicks from the lamp-placeThe AGENT is liable — deviated on the full peruta
Two perutot: 'bring me an etrog'An etrog with one peruta, a pomegranate with the otherBOTH liable — homeowner for the etrog peruta, agent for the pomegranate peruta. Rabbi Yehuda dissents: homeowner isn't liable, since he can say 'I wanted a BIGGER etrog'
A gold dinar: 'bring me a robe'A robe (3 sela) plus a cloak (3 sela)BOTH liable — same logic. Rabbi Yehuda again exempts the homeowner: 'I wanted a finer robe for the full price'
Gemara's clarification: Rabbi Yehuda's defense works because the agent found a genuine bargain — had he spent the FULL amount as instructed, the homeowner would have gotten an even better item, so the homeowner can plausibly claim dissatisfaction rather than acceptance.

🏦 New Mishna: Money Changer vs. Homeowner

Who may use deposited consecrated money — and where does a storekeeper fit?

Money Changer (Bound coins)

  • May NOT use them
  • If he spends them: liable for me'ilah

Money Changer (Loose/unbound coins)

  • MAY use them freely
  • If he spends them: NOT liable

Ordinary Homeowner (either way)

  • May NEVER use deposited funds, bound or not
  • If he spends them: always liable
Where does the storekeeper fit? Rabbi Meir: a storekeeper is treated like an ordinary HOMEOWNER — never permitted to use deposits. Rabbi Yehuda: a storekeeper is treated like a MONEY CHANGER — permitted to use loose coins, since storekeepers commonly handle cash the way money changers do.

🪙 A Stray Consecrated Peruta in the Purse

Rabbi Akiva vs. the Rabbis, and a subtle wording difference
Rabbi Akiva
If a consecrated peruta fell into an ordinary purse — or someone said 'a peruta IN this purse is consecrated' — once the FIRST peruta is spent, he's liable, since any coin might be the consecrated one.
Rabbis
Not liable until he spends the ENTIRE purse — only then is it certain the consecrated peruta was actually spent.
But Rabbi Akiva concedes...
If someone said 'a peruta FROM this purse is consecrated' — everyone agrees: not liable until the WHOLE purse is spent.
Rabbi Yoḥanan's resolution
The wording shift matters: 'from this purse' implies he meant 'let this purse not be exempt from consecration' — i.e., his intent was for the VERY LAST coin to be the consecrated one, so only spending everything triggers liability.
