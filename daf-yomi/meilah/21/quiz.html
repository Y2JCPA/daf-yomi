QUESTION 1 OF 5
According to the mishna, if a homeowner tells an agent 'bring it from the window or the chest' (either is acceptable) and the agent brings it from the chest despite the homeowner's silent preference for the window, who is liable for me'ilah?
A. The agent, since he didn't guess the homeowner's true wish
B. The homeowner, since 'matters of the heart are not matters' — only the spoken instruction counts
C. Neither party is liable
D. Both parties share liability equally
Correct: B — The Gemara explains that unspoken intentions carry no legal weight; since the agent fulfilled the literal spoken instruction, the homeowner bears the liability.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
How does Rabbi Elazar explain that sending consecrated money through a deaf-mute, imbecile, or minor can still create liability for the homeowner, despite these individuals being legally incapable of formal agency?
A. He says the mishna is incorrect
B. He compares it to olives in a press-vat, where beneficial moisture is treated as occurring with the owner's intent simply because it serves his purpose — similarly, a successful outcome is attributed to the homeowner
C. He says only adults can ever be considered agents, so the ruling doesn't really apply
D. He says these individuals are actually legally competent for this purpose
Correct: B — Rabbi Elazar's analogy teaches that when an outcome serves the owner's purpose, it can be treated as though done with his intent, even without formal legal agency.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
In the case of a homeowner giving an agent two perutot for an etrog, but the agent buying an etrog with one and a pomegranate with the other, what is Rabbi Yehuda's dissenting view?
A. That neither party is liable at all
B. That the homeowner is NOT liable, since he can claim he wanted a larger, better etrog worth the full two perutot, and thus didn't get what he wanted
C. That only the pomegranate seller is liable
D. That the agent is exempt in this case
Correct: B — Rabbi Yehuda argues the homeowner can plausibly say he wanted a superior etrog for the full amount, meaning his instructions weren't truly fulfilled, so he shouldn't be liable.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
According to the new mishna, what is the difference between a money changer and an ordinary homeowner regarding deposited consecrated money?
A. There is no difference between them
B. A money changer may use LOOSE (unbound) deposited coins, while an ordinary homeowner may never use deposited money, bound or unbound
C. A homeowner may always use deposited money freely
D. A money changer may never use any deposited money
Correct: B — The mishna distinguishes a money changer, who may use unbound coins (since that's the nature of his business), from a homeowner, who is never permitted to use deposited funds under any circumstances.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
What is the key difference between saying 'a peruta IN this purse is consecrated' versus 'a peruta FROM this purse is consecrated,' according to Rabbi Yoḥanan's resolution?
A. There is no difference; both phrasings yield identical rulings
B. 'In this purse' triggers liability upon spending the FIRST coin (Rabbi Akiva); 'from this purse' means the LAST coin was intended as consecrated, so liability begins only once the entire purse is spent
C. 'From this purse' means no coins are ever consecrated
D. Both phrasings exempt the person from all liability
Correct: B — Rabbi Yoḥanan explains that 'from this purse' implies the person meant the purse should not be exempted from consecration until the very end, meaning the final remaining coin was intended as the consecrated one.
