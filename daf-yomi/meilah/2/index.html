📖 Overview: When Does Misuse (Me'ilah) Still Apply?

Meilah 2a–2b
קׇדְשֵׁי קָדָשִׁים שֶׁשְּׁחָטָן בַּדָּרוֹם – מוֹעֲלִין בָּהֶן
The scene: Masechet Meilah opens mid-topic, assuming the reader knows that misusing consecrated property (me'ilah) requires bringing a guilt offering and repaying the value plus one-fifth. The mishna teaches that offerings of the most sacred order disqualified by an improper location or time of slaughter — south instead of north, night instead of day — remain subject to me'ilah if one derives benefit from them. The Gemara explains why every listed case is necessary, then states Rabbi Yehoshua's guiding principle: an offering that once had 'a period of fitness for the priests' to eat is no longer subject to me'ilah once disqualified, but one that never had such a window remains fully consecrated. The daf closes with a dispute between Rabba and Rav Yosef about offerings that ascended the altar despite disqualification, and whether the mishna's me'ilah is by Torah law or only rabbinic law.

Location and time errors that still leave an offering subject to me'ilah
CaseWhat Went WrongStatus
Slaughtered in the southShould be slaughtered in the northSubject to me'ilah
Slaughtered south, blood collected northThe more significant rite (collection) was correctSubject to me'ilah
Slaughtered north, blood collected southThe more significant rite was wrongSubject to me'ilah
Slaughtered by day, blood sprinkled by night (or reverse)One of the two rites at the wrong timeSubject to me'ilah
Slaughtered chutz lizmano or chutz limkomo (piggul intent)Intent to eat/sprinkle beyond its time or placeSubject to me'ilah
Why list them all? The Gemara shows each case is needed: without it, one might think that only the exact combination taught is included, and that a different mix of correct/incorrect rites would be 'as though strangled' and exempt from me'ilah.

📜 Rabbi Yehoshua's Principle

Did the offering ever have a moment when priests could eat it?
❓ A sacrificial animal becomes disqualified. Is one liable for me'ilah if he benefits from it?
↓
📏 Rabbi Yehoshua's rule: כׇּל שֶׁהָיָה לָהּ שְׁעַת הֶיתֵּר לַכֹּהֲנִים — did it ever have a period of fitness for the priests?
↓
Had a period of fitness
It stayed overnight (notar), became impure, or left the courtyard — after the priests could already eat it. No me'ilah.
Never had fitness
Slaughtered/sprinkled outside its time or place, or by disqualified people — the priests could never eat it. Me'ilah applies.
↓
🎯 Once permitted to priests, an offering exits the category of items forbidden to everyone; if never permitted, it stays fully consecrated to God
Key line: Me'ilah applies only to items that are entirely forbidden for human use — once priests could have eaten it, that status ends even if it is later disqualified for another reason.

⚖️ Rabba vs. Rav Yosef: If They Ascended, Must They Descend?

A disqualified offering that reached the altar — sacrifice it or remove it?
Rabba
אִם עָלוּ – יֵרְדוּ. If a disqualified offering ascended the altar, it must still descend and not be sacrificed.
Rav Yosef
אִם עָלוּ – לֹא יֵרְדוּ. Once it has ascended, it stays and may be sacrificed.
Gemara
This only applies according to Rabbi Shimon's view (Zevaḥim 84a) — Rabbi Yehuda agrees such offerings always descend, no dispute there.
Rav Yosef
I follow Rabbi Shimon plainly: once inside the courtyard and on the altar, it stays.
Rabba
Not so — Rabbi Shimon meant only cases like blood placed above vs. below the red line, where slaughter and collection were done properly. Here, slaughtering in the south is like strangling the animal — it never counts as valid slaughter, so it must descend.
The stakes: If Rabba is right that it must descend, the mishna's ruling that one is liable for me'ilah becomes puzzling — how can something removed from the altar still carry Torah-level me'ilah?

🚫 Why Piggul Offerings Still Carry Me'ilah

Chutz lizmano and chutz limkomo are unfit for consumption anywhere — so why not exempt from me'ilah?

The Question

  • Slaughtered/sprinkled with intent to eat or sprinkle beyond its time or place
  • Unfit even for offerings of lesser sanctity
  • For what are they now 'fit'? Why treat them as still consecrated?

The Answer

  • הוֹאִיל וּמְרַצִּין לְפִיגּוּלִין — the blood's sprinkling still 'accepts' the offering into piggul status
  • Piggul only takes effect if all permitting factors (like blood sprinkling) were done properly
  • That significance shows the offering has not fully lost its consecrated status
Takeaway: An offering's ability to trigger piggul — a stringency — is itself proof that it retains enough sanctity to remain subject to me'ilah.

💰 Torah-Law Me'ilah vs. Rabbinic-Law Me'ilah

Resolving the difficulty with Rabba's opinion
מַאי אִיכָּא בֵּין דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא לְרַבָּנַן? דְּאוֹרָיְיתָא מְשַׁלְּמִין חוֹמֶשׁ, דְּרַבָּנַן – לָא
The resolution: According to Rabba, an offering slaughtered in the south must descend from the altar — so the mishna's me'ilah must be only by rabbinic law, not Torah law. The practical difference: Torah-law me'ilah requires repaying the principal plus an extra one-fifth; rabbinic-law me'ilah does not. The Gemara supports the idea of rabbinic me'ilah from Ulla citing Rabbi Yoḥanan: sacrificial animals that died are excluded from Torah-law me'ilah, yet are still subject to it by rabbinic decree — precisely so people will not casually derive benefit from animals that still look like ordinary offerings.
