📖 Overview: Two Chapters End, a New One Begins

Meilah 10a–10b
כׇּל שֶׁיֵּשׁ לוֹ מַתִּירִין – אֵין חַיָּיבִין עָלָיו מִשּׁוּם פִּיגּוּל וְנוֹתָר וְטָמֵא עַד שֶׁיַּקְרִיבוּ מַתִּירִין
The scene: The daf closes out the second chapter with two rulings from Rava — one exempting benefit derived from meat or portions that can no longer be brought to the altar (since nothing is lost from a Temple perspective), and one refining exactly when misused sin/guilt-offering money goes to the Dead Sea versus to communal gifts. A new mishna then opens the third chapter, listing items that are entirely for the altar — the handful, frankincense, incense, and special meal offerings — and introduces the crucial concept of matirin, 'permitting factors': some items need another item sacrificed first before piggul, notar, or tamei liability can apply to them. The daf ends with the opening of yet another chapter, listing the five sin offerings that must be left to die.

🚫 Rava's Two Refinements

Closing out the debates from recent dafim
הַכֹּל מוֹדִים שֶׁאִם נֶהֱנָה מִבְּשַׂר קׇדְשֵׁי קָדָשִׁים שֶׁנִּטְמָא קוֹדֶם זְרִיקָה... דְּפָטוּר
Two rulings: First: everyone agrees that deriving benefit from sacred-order meat that became impure before sprinkling, or from lesser-sanctity portions already placed on the altar, incurs NO me'ilah — even though a mitzva (burning, or turning with a fork) technically remains, that mitzva is not part of the sacrificial service itself, so nothing of value is lost. Second: the earlier rule that misused sin-offering money goes to the Dead Sea applies only when the misuse became known before atonement was achieved; if it became known only after, the money goes to communal gifts instead, since 'one does not set aside money from the start intending it to be lost.'

🔑 Deriving the Rule for Tamei From Piggul

A kal vaḥomer, then a verse, then a homiletical reading
1️⃣ Known: for PIGGUL, one is liable only for an item with matirin (permitting factors) — even though piggul is a strict prohibition (one awareness suffices, fixed offering, never permitted communally)
↓
2️⃣ Kal vaḥomer: TUMAH is more lenient in structure (needs two awarenesses, sliding-scale offering, sometimes permitted communally) — so it should ALSO require matirin, if anything more so
↓
3️⃣ But the Torah states broadly: 'anyone who approaches the sacred items... while his impurity is on him' (Leviticus 22:3) — applying to ALL sacred items, matirin or not
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Elazar's resolution: the odd word 'approaches' (יקרב) is read as 'fit to be offered' (יִקָּרֵב) — items WITH matirin: liable once matirin are sacrificed; items WITHOUT matirin: liable once sanctified in a vessel
Why the verse seemed puzzling: A plain reading would make one liable merely for TOUCHING consecrated food — an impossible halakha, since Rabbi Elazar notes there is no such liability for touching alone.

🔑 With Matirin vs. Without Matirin

Two categories, two different triggers for piggul/notar/tamei liability

Has Matirin (e.g. meat with blood sprinkling)

  • Needs another item (like blood) sacrificed to permit it
  • No piggul/notar/tamei liability until the matirin are actually sacrificed
  • The 'permission' comes from outside itself

Has No Matirin (e.g. komeitz, levonah)

  • The item itself IS what permits others (e.g. the handful permits the remainder of a meal offering)
  • Notar and tamei liability begin as soon as it is sanctified in a vessel
  • But NO piggul liability — since piggul requires a valid act that itself creates the disqualification, and here there's no separate matir-act to misapply intent to
