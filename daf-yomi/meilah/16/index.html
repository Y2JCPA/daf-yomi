📖 Overview: How Much of What Counts?

Meilah 16a–16b
אֲכִילַת שְׁרָצִים – לוֹקֶה עָלָיו בִּכְזַיִת
The scene: Building on yesterday's mishna that all carcasses combine for their measure, the Gemara asks whether that combination extends to the prohibition of EATING carcasses, not merely to ritual impurity. Rav, Levi, and Rav Asi stake out different positions, tested against baraitot about a dead cow and a live camel in every possible combination — resolved through the principle of whether a prohibition can 'take effect' on an item already forbidden by another prohibition. The daf then turns to creeping animals: Rav says one is flogged for eating an olive-bulk, but a baraita praised by Rabbi Yoḥanan suggests a smaller lentil-bulk suffices — reconciled first by distinguishing live from dead creatures, and then, after further challenges, by limiting the lentil-bulk rule to the eight specific creeping animals the Torah singles out.

🐄 Do Kosher and Non-Kosher Carcasses Combine for Eating?

Three amoraic positions on the mishna's scope
Rav
The mishna's rule that all carcasses combine applies only to ritual IMPURITY. For the prohibition of EATING, kosher carcasses combine only with other kosher carcasses, and non-kosher only with non-kosher.
Levi
Even for EATING, kosher and non-kosher carcasses combine together to reach the liability measure.
Rav Asi
Kosher carcasses combine only among themselves; non-kosher only among themselves — though it's unclear whether he means this for impurity too, or agrees with Rav there and disagrees only about eating.
Why it matters: The dispute turns on whether 'carcass' is one unified category for every purpose, or whether the EATING prohibition specifically singles out kosher animals (already forbidden once dead) differently from non-kosher animals (forbidden even alive).

⚖️ Rav Asi's Resolution: Issur Chal Al Issur

Does the carcass prohibition 'add on' to an already-forbidden non-kosher animal?
1️⃣ Non-kosher meat is already forbidden to eat, whether alive or dead — so does the ADDITIONAL 'carcass' prohibition (which applies once an animal dies) take effect on top of that pre-existing prohibition?
↓
2️⃣ The tanna of the contradictory baraita holds YES — a prohibition CAN take effect on an already-prohibited item — so the carcass prohibition applies equally to cow and camel, letting their dead flesh combine
↓
3️⃣ Rav Asi himself holds NO — a prohibition does NOT take effect where another already exists — so a non-kosher camel's flesh is governed only by its ORIGINAL non-kosher-animal prohibition, never by the separate carcass prohibition
↓
4️⃣ Result: since cow and camel are then governed by two DIFFERENT prohibitions, their flesh never combines — Rav Asi's view survives, unthreatened by this baraita

🦎 Olive-Bulk or Lentil-Bulk for Eating Creeping Animals?

Rav's rule tested, defended, and eventually narrowed
📏 Rav: one is flogged for eating creeping animals only at an OLIVE-BULK — since 'eating' language always implies that measure
↓
⚡ Challenge: a baraita (praised by R' Yoḥanan!) derives from Lev. 20:25 that eating shares its measure with IMPURITY — a LENTIL-bulk, smaller than an olive-bulk
↓
🔄 First resolution: Rav's olive-bulk applies to LIVE creeping animals; the lentil-bulk baraita speaks of DEAD ones (which also transmit impurity)
↓
⚡ Abaye's counter: but our very mishna (combining sheratzim) implies even DEAD ones need an olive-bulk for lashes — Rav Yosef dismisses this as Abaye's own inference, not Rav's actual intent
↓
✅ Final resolution (Rava): R' Yoḥanan's lentil-bulk rule applies ONLY to the EIGHT specific creeping animals the Torah singles out (מוּבְדָּלִין) — all OTHER creeping animals still need a full olive-bulk

🔟 Rav Adda bar Ahava Pushes the Logic Further

If the eight special creeping animals get their own measure, why not kosher vs. non-kosher too?
Rav Adda bar Ahava
If Rabbi Yoḥanan's lentil-bulk rule is limited to the eight singled-out creeping animals, shouldn't the same logic apply to ANIMALS? Leviticus 20:25 juxtaposes kosher animals with creeping animals too — so kosher carcasses (a distinct, Torah-recognized category) should have one measure, and non-kosher carcasses (not distinguished by the Torah as edible) a DIFFERENT, larger measure — perhaps an egg-bulk.
Setup for Rava
The challenge presses Rava to explain whether his 'eight special creatures' principle is a narrow textual detail, or reflects a broader pattern that should also reshape the rules for kosher and non-kosher animal carcasses.
Open thread: This challenge sets up the continuing analysis of exactly how far the Torah's distinctions among forbidden foods reach into their measured liability.
