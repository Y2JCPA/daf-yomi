QUESTION 1 OF 5
According to Shmuel, why is a Temple treasurer not liable for me'ilah merely for holding a consecrated stone or beam?
A. Because treasurers are exempt from all Temple laws
B. Because consecrated property is legitimately entrusted to his custody as part of his role; liability begins only once he transfers it to someone with no rightful claim to it
C. Because stones and beams are never subject to me'ilah
D. Because only priests can commit me'ilah
Correct: B — Shmuel explains that the mishna's case of 'no liability' describes a treasurer to whom the property is properly assigned; giving it away, beyond his legitimate custody, is what triggers me'ilah.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
According to Rav, why does the mishna say one is not liable for a beam built into a house until he actually resides beneath it?
A. Because beams are never subject to me'ilah
B. Because the case involves a beam merely placed on a windowsill without being shaped or changed — so no 'change' has occurred yet to trigger liability
C. Because houses can never be consecrated
D. Because the mishna is discussing a different structure entirely
Correct: B — Rav explains that since the beam was placed, not altered or built into a wall, there's no 'change' (as idol worship comparisons require for damage-prone items) until actual residential benefit occurs.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
What alternative principle does Rav Aḥa, son of Rav Ika, offer to explain liability for the beam case, regardless of the attached/detached debate?
A. That attachment to the ground makes no difference at all under normal circumstances
B. That the Torah forbids any benefit that is 'visible to the eye,' which applies here independent of whether the item counts as attached or detached
C. That only the treasurer can ever be liable
D. That beams are treated differently from stones
Correct: B — Rav Aḥa proposes that visible, apparent benefit is inherently forbidden by the Torah's me'ilah prohibition, sidestepping the entire question of whether reattached items revert to 'attached' status.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
According to the new mishna, who is liable for me'ilah if a homeowner instructs an agent to give guests meat, but the agent gives them liver instead?
A. The homeowner, since he gave the original instruction
B. The agent, since substituting liver for meat is a deviation from his agency, making him act independently rather than as an agent
C. The guests, since they consumed the item
D. No one is liable in this case
Correct: B — The mishna teaches that deviating from clear instructions (like swapping meat for liver) means the agent is no longer acting as an agent, so he personally bears the liability for misuse.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
How does Rav Pappa ultimately explain why an agent could reasonably substitute bird meat for animal meat, but not fish?
A. Fish are never eaten by Jews
B. The scenario involves a day when the person's eyes hurt, and fish were considered harmful to the eyes, so an agent would not consider buying fish as a reasonable substitute, unlike birds
C. Birds are always more expensive than fish
D. There is no meaningful difference; the mishna is simply imprecise
Correct: B — After an initial answer about bloodletting was challenged (since bloodletting also restricts bird meat), Rav Pappa revises his explanation to a day when eating fish was considered harmful to the eyes specifically.
