📖 Overview: A New Chapter on the Nature of Misuse

Meilah 18a–18b
הַנֶּהֱנֶה מִן הַהֶקְדֵּשׁ שָׁוֶה פְּרוּטָה, אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁלֹּא פָּגַם – מָעַל, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא
The scene: The daf closes out the fourth chapter's discussion of combining measures — orlah and kilayim, and garments of different materials — before opening a new chapter with its central mishna: does me'ilah require actually damaging consecrated property, or does mere benefit suffice? Rabbi Akiva says any peruta's worth of benefit is enough; the Rabbis distinguish items that CAN be damaged (requiring peruta-worth of damage) from those that cannot (where benefit alone suffices). Rava narrows down exactly where they truly disagree. The daf then presents an extensive baraita deriving, through a verbal analogy to teruma, the essential structure of me'ilah: who is liable, what 'ma'al' means, and a whole set of technical requirements — and their surprising exceptions.

💍 New Mishna: Does Me'ilah Require Damage?

Rabbi Akiva vs. the Rabbis
R' Akiva
Deriving a peruta's worth of benefit from consecrated property is ALWAYS me'ilah — even if nothing was damaged.
Rabbis
It depends on the item: if it's the KIND of thing that gets damaged by use, one isn't liable until he causes a peruta's worth of damage. If it's the kind that does NOT get damaged by use, benefit alone triggers me'ilah.
Examples (damage-free)
Wearing a consecrated necklace or ring, or drinking from a gold cup — these aren't worn down by use, so ALL agree: benefit alone is enough.
Examples (damage-prone)
Wearing a consecrated robe, covering oneself with a garment, or chopping wood with a consecrated ax — these ARE worn down by use, so no me'ilah until a peruta's worth of actual damage.
Parallel rule: A LIVE sin offering (which could still be redeemed, hence 'damaged' in value) needs a peruta of damage; a DEAD one (no redemption possible) triggers me'ilah from any benefit at all.

👕 Rava Locates the Real Disagreement

Both sides actually agree more than the mishna first suggests
מוֹדֶה רַבִּי עֲקִיבָא לַחֲכָמִים בְּדָבָר שֶׁיֵּשׁ בּוֹ פְּגָם
Rava's clarification: A baraita reveals that Rabbi Akiva actually CONCEDES to the Rabbis for items that can be damaged. So what's left to disagree about? Rava explains: for OUTER garments (exposed to weather) and INNER garments (touching skin, worn by sweat), everyone agrees damage is required, since wear is obvious and cumulative. The real dispute is about a MIDDLE garment (neither fully exposed nor against the skin) and fine, expensive fabric (malmala) — items where wear happens so slowly or carefully that no measurable depreciation accrues. There, Rabbi Akiva says benefit alone still triggers me'ilah; the Rabbis insist on actual peruta-level damage regardless.

📜 The Gezeirah Shava: Me'ilah Modeled on Teruma

A single shared word — 'sin' (חֵטְא) — unlocks the technical structure of misuse
Shared Requirement (from Teruma's 'sin')Applied to Me'ilah
Damage AND benefit must both occurOne is liable only when both damaging and deriving benefit happen (not damage alone, not benefit alone)
The same person who damaged must be the one who benefitedNo liability if one person damages and a different person benefits
Benefit must come from the very item damagedNo 'mixing' benefit from one item with damage to another
Damage and benefit must occur simultaneouslyThey cannot be separated in time as distinct events
Applies only to something detached from the groundLand or attached property is excluded from me'ilah under this analogy
An agent who properly performs his mission acts for the senderThe one who APPOINTED the agent, not the agent himself, bears liability for the me'ilah
Also derived independently: 'Anyone' (נֶפֶשׁ) in the verse includes the ordinary individual, the king, AND the anointed High Priest equally — all share the same me'ilah liability despite differing in other obligations.

📜 Then the Analogy Breaks — On Purpose

'If anyone commits a misuse' (תִּמְעֹל מַעַל) overrides several of teruma's own limits
1️⃣ Teruma's model would require damage-and-benefit to be the SAME item — but 'תמעל מעל' teaches misuse liability applies even without a damageable item at all
↓
2️⃣ Teruma's model wouldn't combine two SEPARATE acts of consumption — but me'ilah DOES: eating half an olive-bulk today and half tomorrow (even much later) still combines to create liability
↓
3️⃣ Teruma's model requires ONE person's damage to match their OWN benefit — but me'ilah combines: one person's consumption WITH another's, even years apart, still adding up to a peruta
↓
4️⃣ Each expansion is anchored in the same inclusive phrase — 'תִּמְעֹל מַעַל' teaches liability 'in any case,' overriding what the teruma-analogy alone would have restricted
The method: The Gemara builds structure through one verbal analogy, then immediately widens it through the verse's own inclusive language — showing me'ilah is both technically precise and deliberately expansive.
