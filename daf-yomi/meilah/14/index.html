📖 Overview: Chapter Three Closes on Construction and Idolatry

Meilah 14a–14b
בּוֹנִין בַּחוֹל וְאַחַר כָּךְ מַקְדִּישִׁין
The scene: The daf opens with a tangential dispute — sparked by the previous daf's bird's-nest mishna — between Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish over whether an idol that broke on its own remains forbidden. Reish Lakish tries to prove his lenient view from the mishna's ruling about nests atop a worshipped tree, prompting several rounds of reinterpretation about where the nest's branches came from. A new mishna then shifts to Temple construction: treasurers who buy wood are liable for misusing the logs but not the sawdust or fallen leaves, since those were never actually purchased. This leads into Shmuel's rule that Temple structures are built with non-sacred materials and only consecrated upon completion — and Rav Pappa's memorably human explanation for why: workers need to rest, and the Torah was written for people, not angels.

🗿 Does a Self-Broken Idol Remain Forbidden?

Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish disagree
R' Yoḥanan
It remains forbidden. Nullification of an idol requires a gentile to actively break it WITH INTENT to nullify — the idol simply breaking on its own accomplishes nothing.
Reish Lakish
It becomes permitted. A gentile witnessing the break would reason: 'If it couldn't even save itself, how could it save me?' — that realization itself constitutes effective nullification.
Reish Lakish's proof attempt
The mishna says a nest atop a worshipped tree may be dislodged with a pole — doesn't this show branches that broke off (even without a gentile's intent) become permitted?
R' Yoḥanan's defense
No proof — the bird brought its nesting branches from elsewhere, not from the worshipped tree itself, so nothing broke off the idol at all.

🐦 Chasing Down the Nest's Branches

Several rounds of reinterpreting a single mishna
1️⃣ R' Yoḥanan's first defense: the bird brought branches from elsewhere — nothing broke off the tree
↓
2️⃣ Reish Lakish objects: then why does the mishna ALSO restrict the parallel case of a consecrated tree's nest? Outside branches should be fully permitted there too!
↓
3️⃣ New answer: the nest IS built from the tree's own branches — but ones that grew AFTER consecration. Growths of consecrated property carry no me'ilah, but are still forbidden ab initio
↓
4️⃣ Support: if branches came from elsewhere, why insist on a pole for the idol-tree case? One could simply take the nest by hand!
↓
5️⃣ Rabbi Abbahu (citing R' Yoḥanan) offers yet another read: the branches WERE from elsewhere — but 'dislodge' means dislodge the CHICKS (which requires climbing, risking real benefit from the tree), not the nest itself
Final clarification: Chicks that can already fly are permitted either way; eggs (and chicks still dependent on their mother) remain forbidden, since they cannot be separated from the tree without genuine benefit.

🏗️ Shmuel's Rule: Build First, Consecrate Later

How Temple construction avoids constant me'ilah violations
❓ Donated money for Temple maintenance is instantly consecrated — so how can it ever be used to pay builders or buy materials without them committing me'ilah?
↓
💡 Shmuel: build with NON-sacred materials first; the donor's sanctity is directed to land on the COMPLETED STRUCTURE, not the raw materials or labor
↓
✅ Once construction finishes, sanctity transfers to the structure — the money becomes non-sacred and can now pay the artisans
↓
⚡ Objection from leftover incense (Shekalim): why not just transfer ITS sanctity onto a completed structure to pay artisans, instead of a roundabout buy-back scheme?
↓
✅ Resolved: this applies only when there's no construction currently large enough in value to absorb that sanctity

😴 Rav Pappa's Very Human Reason

Why not just consecrate the materials immediately, and be careful?
1️⃣ Rav Pappa: 'The Torah was not given to ministering angels' — real workers need rest and will inevitably sit on the stones or wood they're building with
↓
2️⃣ If those materials were already consecrated, sitting on them would itself be an act of me'ilah — an near-certain, foreseeable violation
↓
3️⃣ Challenge: but our mishna says the treasurers' purchased WOOD IS subject to me'ilah right away — shouldn't the same 'sitting' concern apply there too?
↓
4️⃣ Rav Pappa's resolution: the mishna's wood is bought for use THAT SAME DAY — immediate use means no real risk of a worker sitting around on it
The distinction: Long-term construction materials stay non-sacred until the building is done; wood meant for same-day use is consecrated immediately, since there's no meaningful delay for anyone to rest upon it.
