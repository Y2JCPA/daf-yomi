📖 Overview: Money, Blood, and Wine — When Does Sanctity End?

Meilah 11a–11b
הַדָּם – קַל בַּתְּחִלָּה וְחָמוּר בְּסוֹפוֹ. נְסָכִין – חוֹמֶר בִּתְחִלָּתָן וְקַל בְּסוֹפָן
The scene: After closing the mishna on sin offerings left to die, the daf turns to a nazirite who set aside undesignated money for his three completion offerings — no me'ilah applies, since any of it could become the peace offering, which is only subject to me'ilah after its blood is sprinkled. Reish Lakish's attempt to extend this leniency to bird-offering money is rejected by Rava. A new mishna then presents Rabbi Shimon's elegant symmetry: blood begins lenient and ends strict, while libation wine begins strict and ends lenient. The Gemara traces this to a dispute about whether blood is Temple property at all, and explores the principle of 'two verses that come as one' — a hint that some rules (like ashes remaining subject to misuse even after their mitzva is done) are narrow exceptions, not models for other cases.

💰 The Nazirite's Money: Designated vs. Undesignated

What happens to funds set aside for a nazirite's three completion offerings
ScenarioHalakha
Undesignated money, nazirite aliveNo me'ilah — all of it is fit to become the peace offering (which incurs me'ilah only after sprinkling)
Undesignated money, nazirite diesAll funds go to communal gift offerings (nedava)
Designated sin-offering money, nazirite diesGoes to the Dead Sea for disposal — not usable
Designated burnt-offering money, nazirite diesUsed to bring a gift burnt offering — subject to me'ilah
Designated peace-offering money, nazirite diesUsed to bring a gift peace offering — eaten only one day, without the usual accompanying loaves
Reish Lakish's challenge: Why not teach the same leniency for undesignated bird-offering money (for a leper or new mother)? Rava answers: the Torah directs undesignated nazirite money toward a valid peace offering, but undesignated bird money would need to buy birds unfit for the altar (too young or too old) — not a comparable case.

🩸 Rabbi Shimon's Mishna: A Mirror-Image Pattern

Blood and libation wine trade places on the leniency/stringency scale
R' Shimon
Blood is lenient at the start, strict at the end: before sprinkling, no me'ilah applies; but once its remainder flows out through the canal to the Kidron Valley — now you ARE liable for me'ilah.
R' Shimon
Libation wine is the reverse — strict at the start, lenient at the end: from the moment it's consecrated, me'ilah applies; but once it descends into the altar's drainpipes (shitin), its mitzva is done and me'ilah no longer applies.
Rabbis (baraita)
We disagree about the blood at the end: Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Shimon say me'ilah applies once it reaches the Kidron; we say it does NOT — blood was never Temple property to begin with.
Ulla / Beit R' Yishmael
The verse 'I have given it to you upon the altar' (Lev. 17:11) means the blood belongs to YOU (the people), given for atonement, not for misuse liability — so it's never subject to me'ilah at all.
R' Yoḥanan's proof: 'For it is the blood that atones' (Lev. 17:11) — its status before atonement equals its status after: since there's no me'ilah after atonement (the mitzva is done), there was none before either.

📜 Why Doesn't 'Trumat HaDeshen' Prove Sanctity Can Outlast Its Mitzva?

Testing R' Yoḥanan's symmetry argument against a known exception
❓ Challenge: couldn't the SAME logic run backward — before atonement equals AFTER, so blood should carry me'ilah even after atonement?
↓
🔄 Rejected: is there any precedent for an item whose mitzva is done yet still carries me'ilah? — Yes! Trumat hadeshen (ash removal), even once performed, remains subject to me'ilah
↓
🚫 Answer: trumat hadeshen is paired with the scapegoat's limbs (or the High Priest's Yom Kippur garments, or the broken-necked heifer) as 'two verses that come as one' — a rule shared by only two special cases NEVER generalizes further
↓
✅ So trumat hadeshen cannot serve as a precedent for blood — the symmetry argument for 'no me'ilah, before or after atonement' survives intact
Multiple pairings tested: The Gemara runs through several possible partner-verses for trumat hadeshen (the scapegoat, priestly garments, the broken-necked heifer), each blocked by its own textual exclusion, showing the exception truly stays contained.

🍇 The Once-in-Seventy-Years Ritual

Rabbi Elazar bar Rabbi Tzadok's account of congealed libation wine
כְּשֵׁם שֶׁנִּיסּוּכָהּ בִּקְדוּשָּׁה, כָּךְ שְׂרֵיפָתָהּ בִּקְדוּשָּׁה
The account: A small gap between the ramp and the altar led down to the drainpipes. Once every seventy years, young priests descended to remove congealed wine — resembling pressed fig-cakes — accumulated from decades of libations, and burned it in sanctity within the courtyard, derived by verbal analogy ('holy'/'holy') from the law of burning leftover offerings. This seems to contradict the mishna's claim that libation wine loses its sanctity once it reaches the drainpipes — resolved by distinguishing wine that merely collected on the way down (still subject to misuse, needing sanctified burning) from wine that actually reached the very bottom of the pipes (which the mishna says is no longer subject to misuse); alternatively, the requirement to burn it in sanctity may be only a rabbinic stringency, supported but not required by the verse.
