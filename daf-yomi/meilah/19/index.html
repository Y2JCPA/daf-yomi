📖 Overview: The Fine Print of Misuse

Meilah 19a–19b
אֵין מוֹעֵל אַחַר מוֹעֵל בְּמוּקְדָּשִׁין אֶלָּא בְּהֵמָה וּכְלִי שָׁרֵת בִּלְבַד
The scene: The baraita's gezeirah shava with teruma continues: moving money from one sacred purpose to another — like buying bird nests with Temple funds meant for something else — is still me'ilah. Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Yehuda then dispute exactly when spending consecrated money for one's own sin or guilt offering becomes me'ilah. The Gemara draws out three separate lessons from misuse's comparison to sotah, idol worship, and teruma, then tests whether gold jewelry can really be considered damage-proof. A new mishna limits 'misuse after misuse' — where a second person can commit an independent act of me'ilah on the same item — to animals and service vessels alone, with Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi expanding the category to include Temple firewood.

🪺 When Spending Consecrated Money Becomes Me'ilah

Sacred-to-sacred transfers, and a timing dispute
CaseHalakha
Using Temple treasury funds to buy bird nests for a zav/zavah/new motherStill me'ilah — even though the birds serve a sacred purpose (obligatory offerings), they should be bought with PRIVATE funds
Spending consecrated money on one's own shekel, sin offering, or guilt offering — Rabbi Shimon's viewMe'ilah occurs the MOMENT the money is spent
Same case — Rabbi Yehuda's viewMe'ilah occurs only once the blood is SPRINKLED — that's the true moment of atonement/benefit
Underlying question: When exactly does a person 'benefit' from consecrated funds used for their own religious obligation — at the transaction, or at the completion of the mitzvah?

🔀 Three Comparisons, Three Distinct Lessons

The verse links me'ilah to sotah, idol worship, and teruma — each teaching something different
⚖️ Compared to SOTAH: liability applies even WITHOUT damage — like a woman placing a consecrated ring on her hand, deriving benefit alone is enough
↓
🗿 Compared to IDOL WORSHIP: for damage-prone items, liability requires an actual CHANGE — from fit-for-sacred-use to fit-only-for-non-sacred-use (e.g., chopping wood with a consecrated ax)
↓
🌾 Compared to TERUMA: one who damages WITHOUT deriving benefit (e.g., spilling it) is EXEMPT — pure damage alone, without benefit, creates no liability
The synthesis: Three different verbal analogies each isolate one distinct rule — together they define exactly what combination of benefit and damage triggers me'ilah.

💍 Is Gold Truly Immune to Damage?

Rav Kahana challenges the mishna's classic example
Rav Kahana
The mishna treats gold jewelry as damage-free — but isn't that wrong? Look what happened to the gold jewelry of the wealthy man Nun's daughter-in-law — it deteriorated over time!
Rav Zevid
Perhaps her gold suffered the same fate as gold in careless hands generally — poor handling, not inherent wear. And besides, even granting gold isn't damaged IMMEDIATELY by use, does that mean it NEVER sustains damage, even gradually over a very long time?
Related resolution: For the mishna's sin offering case (removing wool while it's alive), Rav Pappa clarifies it must involve a BLEMISHED animal, since a healthy sacrificial animal remains fully fit for the altar regardless of missing wool — only a blemished one (destined for sale) loses monetary value from the loss.

🪨 New Mishna: A Stone or Beam of Hekdesh

Opening the next topic
נָטַל אֶבֶן אוֹ קוֹרָה שֶׁל הֶקְדֵּשׁ – הֲרֵי זֶה לֹא מָעַל
The new case: One who takes a consecrated stone or beam for his own use is NOT liable for me'ilah — setting up the next stage of discussion about why land, or items still attached to or built into structures, occupy a different category from the portable consecrated items (animals, vessels, jewelry) discussed until now.
