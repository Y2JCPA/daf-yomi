📖 Overview: Where Consecration Does and Doesn't Reach

Meilah 13a–13b
וְלַד הַמְעוּשֶּׂרֶת – לֹא יִנַק מִן הַמְעוּשֶּׂרֶת
The scene: The daf works through cases where consecration's reach is ambiguous: a cistern, dovecote, or tree consecrated together with its contents is fully subject to me'ilah, but the Rabbis hold that content added AFTER consecration is not — only Rabbi Yosei disagrees for natural growth. The Gemara then derives, through verbal analogies to the firstborn offering, why an unconsecrated calf may not nurse from its now-consecrated or now-tithed mother, and why laborers and animals working with Temple produce may not eat from it. A new mishna maps consecration across physical boundaries — tree roots, spring water, and the golden jug used for the Sukkot water libation — setting up a dispute between Reish Lakish and Rabbi Yoḥanan about whether that libation has a fixed measure.

🕳️ Fit for the Altar? Fit for Temple Maintenance? Neither?

The mishna's categories for consecrated property, and what happened AFTER consecration
CaseFit ForRule
Cistern full of water (at consecration)Temple maintenance only (kneading clay)Me'ilah applies to both the cistern and the water inside
Dovecote full of pigeons / tree full of fruit (at consecration)The altar (pigeons, fruit-wine), not maintenanceMe'ilah applies to both
Garbage dump full of manure (at consecration)Neither altar nor maintenanceSold; proceeds go to the Temple; me'ilah still applies to both
Empty cistern/dovecote/tree/field, filled AFTERWARDSame categories as aboveMe'ilah applies only to the vessel/land itself — NOT to what filled it later
Rabbi Yosei's dissent: For a field or tree, Rabbi Yosei holds that even growth appearing AFTER consecration (grass, new fruit) IS subject to me'ilah, since it is still 'growth of consecrated property' — the Rabbis disagree only for growth, not for animals born into a pen or objects deposited afterward.

🐄 Why the Milk Is Off-Limits Too

Two verbal analogies (gezeirah shava) extend me'ilah to a mother's milk
1️⃣ Mishna: an unconsecrated calf born to a tithed mother, or to a not-yet-consecrated sacrificial mother, may not nurse from her once she becomes tithed/consecrated
↓
2️⃣ Source for the tithed animal's milk: the word 'passing' (הַעֲבָרָה) links tithing to the firstborn offering — just as the firstborn is subject to me'ilah in its entirety (it's male, no milk), so is the tithed animal's milk
↓
3️⃣ Source for the sacrificial animal's milk: the word 'its mother' (אִמּוֹ) links a newborn sacrificial animal's seven days with its mother to the same phrase for a firstborn — again extending full me'ilah, including the milk
↓
4️⃣ Result: since the milk itself is under me'ilah, allowing the unconsecrated offspring to nurse would mean it derives forbidden benefit — so it may not
Same logic protects the Temple's produce: A laborer may not eat consecrated dried figs while working (derived from 'your neighbor's vineyard' — not the Temple's), and a threshing cow may not eat consecrated vetch (from 'do not muzzle an ox... in its treading' — 'its' excludes a consecrated field).

🐄 Two Sources, One Principle: The Temple's Produce Isn't Yours to Eat

Why the Gemara needs a separate verse for the cow but not the laborer

The Laborer

  • Verse: 'when you come into your neighbor's vineyard' (Deut. 23:25)
  • Excludes Temple property automatically — no separate derivation needed
  • Simple, obvious exclusion

The Threshing Cow

  • Verse: 'do not muzzle an ox during ITS treading' (Deut. 25:4)
  • 'Its' (your own) treading excludes a consecrated field — you must muzzle the ox there
  • A separate, less obvious derivation was needed
A further case: Threshing ordinary legumes (kalilin) atop a consecrated field is itself me'ilah — Ravina infers that the field's loosened dust benefits the threshing, so one derives benefit from something 'detached,' satisfying the usual requirement for misuse.

💧 Is There a Fixed Measure for the Water Libation?

Reish Lakish and Rabbi Yoḥanan disagree about the golden jug's flask
Reish Lakish
Once the water is placed in the service-vessel flask, me'ilah applies ONLY to the required three log (like the wine libation) — any water beyond that measure is not consecrated at all.
R' Yoḥanan
Me'ilah applies to ALL the water in the flask, however much it holds — there is no fixed maximum measure for the water libation.
Challenge to Reish Lakish
But Rabbi Elazar teaches that pouring the water libation outside the Temple courtyard carries karet, just like an ordinary offering sacrificed outside — following Rabbi Akiva's reading that 'their libations' (Num. 29:31) includes BOTH water and wine libations equally.
Resolution
Reish Lakish himself agrees there IS a fixed measure for the water (matching wine's three log) — his earlier statement about 'no measure' was framed only within Rabbi Menaḥem Yodafa's explanation of Rabbi Elazar's position, not his own view.
