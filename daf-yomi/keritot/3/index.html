📖 Overview: Counting and Recounting the Thirty-Six

Keritot 3a–3b
כׇּל מָקוֹם שֶׁאַתָּה מוֹצֵא שְׁנֵי לָאוִין וְכָרֵת אֶחָד – חַלֵּק חַטָּאת בֵּינֵיהֶן
The scene: The Gemara continues probing the mishna's list of thirty-six karet violations from every angle. It first denies that punishment can ever be derived by a fortiori logic alone, then lays down Rabbi Elazar's rule that wherever two distinct prohibitions share a single karet verse, one who transgresses both unwittingly must bring two separate sin offerings, illustrated by the anointing oil. The Gemara then asks a very practical question: since the list mixes prohibitions that only a man could violate with one only a woman could violate, who could ever transgress all thirty-six in one lapse? This sends the Gemara through several proposed edits to the list, and finally to Rav's own accounting of the number: thirty-three genuine unwitting-sin-offering cases, plus three added only to round out the karet count. The daf closes with a dispute between Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish over why the mishna's list includes the necromancer but omits the sorcerer, turning on whether a sin offering requires a physical action.

🚫 No Punishment by Inference

Can karet for a full sister be deduced from the karet stated for a half sister?
Gemara
The Torah states karet separately for intercourse with a father's-side half sister and a mother's-side half sister. Why not simply infer, a fortiori, that a full sister — both father's and mother's daughter — is equally liable?
Answer
One does not administer punishment based on an a fortiori inference (עוֹנְשִׁין מִן הַדִּין). Even an airtight logical inference cannot generate a new punishment; the Torah must state it explicitly.
Objection
But Rabbi Yitzḥak disagrees — he holds one does administer punishment by a fortiori inference!
Alt. answer
Or, if you prefer: Rabbi Yitzḥak instead derives the karet for a full sister from the parallel prohibition (warning) stated for her — since she is explicitly forbidden, the same karet that applies to other forbidden relatives applies to her too.
Underlying principle: Halakhic punishments, unlike prohibitions, cannot rest on logical inference alone — they require an explicit verse.

🎗️ One Karet, Two Prohibitions

Rabbi Elazar in the name of Rabbi Hoshaya's general rule
ElementDetail
The ruleWherever two separate prohibitions share a single stated karet, an unwitting transgressor of both owes two sin offerings — split the sin offering between them
Case: anointing oilTwo prohibitions in Exodus 30:32 — blending it (מְפַטֵּם) and applying it to skin (סָךְ) — but only one karet in 30:33
Proof from the mishna's orderThe mishna lists blending oil, then blending incense, then applying oil — placing incense between the two oil clauses
What that order teachesJust as blending incense is its own independent karet violation, so blending oil and applying oil, though sharing one verse's karet, are independent — two sin offerings
Question raised: If prohibitions can split this way without a separate karet verse for each, why did the Torah bother writing karet for one's sister separately at all (Lev. 20:17)? Answer: for the Rabbis, to teach 'no punishment by inference'; for Rabbi Yitzḥak, to render one liable to three offerings for a woman who is sister, father's sister, and mother's sister at once.

🚻 Who Could Transgress All Thirty-Six?

The list mixes cases only a man can do with one only a woman can do
❓ Rabbi Yoḥanan says the tally teaches liability for each of 36 if done in one lapse — but who could do all 36? A man cannot bring an animal upon himself (a woman's act); a woman cannot lie with a male or an animal (two men's acts)
↓
💡 Rabbi Yoḥanan: the list is really about men. Drop 'a woman who brings an animal upon her' and add instead 'a male who brings another male upon him' — per Rabbi Yishmael's view that this is a second, independent prohibition
↓
Rabbi Yishmael
Derives active and passive male intercourse from two separate verses (Lev. 18:22 and Deut. 23:18) — two sin offerings possible
Rabbi Akiva
Derives both from one verse (reading תִּשְׁכַּב as both active and passive) — only one sin offering
↓
✅ Abaye: bestiality can't be split this way even for Rabbi Yishmael, since 'sodomite' (Deut. 23:18) applies only to men — so only the homosexual case gets the extra clause
Net result: The mishna's list, properly read, describes prohibitions a single man could violate in one unwitting lapse.

3️⃣3️⃣+3️⃣ Rav's Structure of the Mishna

Rabbi Elazar in the name of Rav: not all 36 are the same kind of case
1️⃣ The mishna really teaches 33 transgressions for which a man is liable to a sin offering when done unwittingly
↓
2️⃣ Then it adds 3 more just to round out the full count of 36 karet violations: a woman who brings an animal upon her, failing to bring the Paschal offering, and failing to circumcise
↓
3️⃣ Proof: the last two are positive mitzvot — but a baraita teaches that a sin offering for unwitting sin applies only where the transgression is a 'sit and do nothing' violation, like idolatry (from Numbers 15:29–30) — not failure to perform a positive act
↓
4️⃣ So Pesach and circumcision carry karet for willful non-performance, yet no sin offering for unwitting non-performance — they belong to the 'completing the count' group of three, not the 33
Takeaway: The mishna's single list actually blends two different kinds of items: 33 true sin-offering cases and 3 karet-only cases added purely to reach the number thirty-six.

🔮 Necromancer In, Sorcerer Out

Why does the mishna's karet list mention only the necromancer (בַּעַל אוֹב), not the sorcerer (יִדְּעוֹנִי)?

Rabbi Yoḥanan

  • Both are stated together in one prohibition (Lev. 19:31) — 'do not turn to the necromancers and the sorcerers'
  • One prohibition, one offering — the mishna need only list one of the pair
  • Necromancer is listed because the verse opens with him

Reish Lakish

  • The sorcerer's act involves no physical action — the bone speaks on its own, so no sin offering applies at all
  • The necromancer's arm-striking counts as an action (per Rabbi Akiva's minimal-action standard)
  • Rav Pappa: for stoning the two are listed separately since the death penalty divides differently than the prohibition does
Underlying dispute: Rabbi Yoḥanan holds the mishna follows Rabbi Akiva, who needs no action at all for a sin offering (hence blasphemy, mere speech, is also excluded only by its own explicit verse); Reish Lakish holds even Rabbi Akiva requires a minor action, which sorcery lacks entirely.
