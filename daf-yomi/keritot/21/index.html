📖 Overview: Mapping the Edges of the Blood Prohibition

Keritot 21a–21b
לְאֵתוֹיֵי דָּמוֹ שֶׁל כּוֹי
The scene: The daf continues excluding categories from the Torah's blood prohibition — creeping animals, eggs, fish, grasshoppers — before turning to include one unusual case: the koy, an animal of uncertain domesticated/undomesticated status, whose blood, fat, carcass, and sciatic nerve are all shown to be forbidden through a chain of derivations. The Gemara then works through the generalization-detail-generalization exegesis that defines exactly which creatures' blood the Torah forbids, and confronts an apparent contradiction about whether human flesh, while a person is alive, can become susceptible to the 'light' impurity that applies to ordinary food. The daf closes with several marginal rulings — when collected fish blood becomes suspect, when creeping-animal blood earns lashes, and whether human blood carries even a preference to abstain — plus the technical rules for combining a sliver of animal carcass with ordinary food to reach the minimum measure for impurity.

🦌 The Koy's Extended Prohibitions

A creature of uncertain category (domesticated? undomesticated?) still ends up fully regulated
1️⃣ Blood: included via 'so too every item' in the generalization-detail-generalization reading — Rav Adda bar Avin identifies this as covering the koy specifically, since it's a genuinely distinct category, not mere uncertainty
↓
2️⃣ Fat: derived from the extra word 'kol' ('all fat') in Leviticus 7:23
↓
3️⃣ Carcass: derived from the extra word 'kol' ('all carcasses') in Deuteronomy 14:21
↓
4️⃣ Sciatic nerve: since the Torah ties this prohibition to having a 'spoon of the thigh' (Gen. 32:33), and the koy has one, the prohibition applies automatically
↓
5️⃣ Impurity and the need for ritual slaughter: no specific verse — derived by sheer logic, since the Torah treats the koy like a domesticated animal in every other regard
Method shift: Four of these five prohibitions rest on close textual readings; the last relies purely on consistency — once treated like a domesticated animal everywhere else, it must be one here too.

🐓 Why 'Bird AND Animal'? The Generalization-Detail Exegesis

Testing why the verse needs both terms, not just one
❓ Why does the verse specify BOTH 'bird' and 'animal' rather than just one?
↓
🐄 If only 'animal' were written: one might think the prohibition applies only to creatures like an animal — NOT subject to diverse-kinds crossbreeding (kilayim) restrictions on WOOL specifically — excluding birds' feathers from that comparison
↓
🐦 If only 'bird' were written: one might exclude non-kosher birds' blood (since the 'mother bird' mitzva doesn't apply to them) — so 'animal' is added to correct this too
↓
📖 Overall structure: 'no manner of blood' (generalization) → 'bird or animal' (detail) → 'that soul shall be cut off' (generalization again) — a classic generalization-detail-generalization reading, limiting the prohibition to creatures sharing the detail's defining traits
↓
✅ Objection resolved: though the two generalizations carry different penalties (lashes vs. karet), the school of Rabbi Yishmael still applies this exegetical method even across mismatched severity levels
Result: Only creatures combining light+severe impurity, forbidden/permitted status, and 'meat' classification fall under the blood prohibition — precisely birds and animals (and, per the extension, the koy).

🧑 Does Human Flesh Ever Carry 'Light' Impurity?

A mishna about cutting flesh from a living person seems to contradict the baraita's claim
Baraita
Human blood is excluded from the Torah's prohibition because people lack 'light' (food) impurity — only the severe impurity of a corpse applies to them.
Challenge
But a mishna (Oktzin) says flesh cut from a LIVING person becomes impure as food, given intent to eat it plus contact with liquid — that's exactly 'light' impurity!
Resolution
The baraita's claim applies only after death — a corpse carries only severe impurity, no longer susceptible to food-impurity at all. While alive, flesh CAN become impure as food.
Further challenge
But animal carcasses carry severe impurity too (both flesh and blood, per Beit Hillel) — so why does the baraita treat animals as having 'light' impurity but not humans?
Final resolution
The distinction matters for a specific mishna: non-kosher carcasses (or kosher birds in villages) require explicit INTENT to be eaten before becoming impure as food — but a human corpse, even wrapped to preserve it, NEVER counts as food, since 'his intention is overridden by everyone else's,' as people simply don't eat corpses.
Underlying idea: 'Light' impurity for food always requires the item to be a realistic candidate for eating — something categorically true of animal meat but never true of a human body.

🩸 Marginal Cases: Fish Blood, Human Blood, Creeping Things

Precise rulings that hinge on collection, warning, and identifying marks

Rav: collected fish blood

  • Fish/grasshopper blood is normally permitted outright
  • But once COLLECTED in a container without visible scales, it's prohibited — it now LOOKS like forbidden animal/bird blood
  • If scales are visibly present, it remains permitted even collected

Rabbi Zeira: creeping-animal blood

  • If warned specifically about the 'creeping animal' prohibition (which includes its blood), lashes apply
  • If warned specifically about the separate 'blood' prohibition, NO lashes — that prohibition doesn't cover this blood at all

Rav Sheshet: human blood

  • Human blood carries not even a preference to abstain from it ab initio
  • Contrasted with blood of the spleen/heart/kidneys, which — though not punished by karet — still carry a real prohibition
Common thread: Each ruling depends on a precise legal technicality — what the item looks like, what prohibition was specified in the warning, or whether any prohibition applies at all.

🍞 Combining Less Than an Olive-Bulk

A sliver of animal carcass, too small alone to transmit impurity, still counts when combined with food
PositionRuling
Rabbi Ḥiyya's caseLess than an olive-bulk of carcass, combined with less than an egg-bulk of food, together reaching a full egg-bulk — becomes susceptible to food impurity, requiring the added intent (machshava)
Rav's challengeShouldn't this combination also require contact with LIQUID (hekhsher) to become susceptible, per the general rule for items lacking severe impurity?
Rabbi Ḥiyya's answerNo — since this carcass sliver COULD eventually combine to a full olive-bulk (triggering severe impurity), it's treated differently from ordinary food and needs no liquid contact
Rav Ḥananya's alternativeEven a full olive-bulk carcass, if wrapped in less-than-egg-bulk dough, still needs the combination logic — since wrapping blocks contact-impurity but not carrying-impurity, distinguishing it from a wrapped human corpse (whose severe impurity 'breaks through' any wrapping)
The corpse contrast: A human corpse's severe impurity 'breaks through and ascends, breaks through and descends' even when wrapped — a stringency with no parallel in animal carcass law, reinforcing why humans and animals are treated so differently throughout this sugya.
