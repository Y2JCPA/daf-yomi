📖 Overview: Measuring Time, Impurity, and Intoxication

Keritot 13a–13b
יֵשׁ אוֹכֵל אֲכִילָה אַחַת וְחַיָּיבִין עָלֶיהָ אַרְבָּעָה חַטָּאוֹת וְאָשָׁם אֶחָד
The scene: The daf resolves the previous daf's open question about Rabbi Meir's time-window for eating a forbidden olive-bulk, confirming it as a stringency, and then tests Shmuel's parallel theory about impure foods against several baraitot — a test it fails. The Gemara works out exactly how food, liquid, and wine combine across multiple bites or sips to cross an impurity or Temple-entry threshold, including a puzzling ruling about a nursing infant whose mother contracted corpse-impurity. It then turns to the prohibition on a priest drinking wine before Temple service, parsing three different readings of 'wine and strong drink' that determine how much, and which beverages, actually disqualify him — with a real anecdote about Rav Ashi declining to rule after a festival meal. The daf closes with a striking new mishna: a single act of eating that generates liability for four separate sin offerings and one guilt offering simultaneously.

🕐 Settling the Time-Window Dispute

Confirming Rabbi Meir's standard, then testing — and refuting — Shmuel's parallel theory
1️⃣ The Gemara confirms: Rabbi Meir's 'as though eating roasted grain' standard is a stringency — liable even across a long stretch of time, so long as there's no gap between bites
↓
2️⃣ Shmuel (via Ravnai) proposes a parallel rule for ritual impurity: eating a disqualifying quarter-loaf combines across an entire day, provided each individual olive-bulk was eaten within the shorter 'half-loaf' time-window
↓
3️⃣ A baraita directly contradicts this: the entire quarter-loaf, start to finish, must fall within the half-loaf time-window to combine — not just each individual bite
↓
4️⃣ The Gemara concludes bluntly: 'the refutation of Ravnai's view is a conclusive refutation' (תְּיוּבְתָּא)
Why it matters: Both disputes hinge on the same underlying question: does a prohibition's minimum measure require continuous eating, or does each sub-measure only need its own brief window?

📏 Measuring Impurity Thresholds

A baraita's detailed rules for combining bites, sips, and Temple entry
RuleDetail
Food thresholdA quarter-loaf of impure food disqualifies eating teruma — but only if entirely consumed within the 'half-loaf' time-window, start to finish
Liquid thresholdA quarter-log of impure liquid works the same way — same time-window applies
No 'partial' immersionOne who ate less than the threshold may not immerse yet — doing so risks him wrongly believing a later completing bite needs no new immersion
Pregnant woman's exceptionMay eat even large amounts of less-than-threshold impure food due to the danger of miscarriage
Wine before Temple entryA quarter-log of wine, drunk within the half-loaf window, then entering and remaining in the Temple for that same duration, creates liability
Common thread: Every one of these thresholds is measured not just by quantity, but by the pace at which it was consumed.

🍼 A Nursing Infant's Uncertain Status

If a mother contracted corpse-impurity, is the milk she nurses to her child impure?
Baraita
The Sages permitted a woman impure through contact with a corpse to nurse her child, and the child remains pure.
Challenge
But doesn't the child become impure by drinking impure milk, once that milk is rendered 'susceptible' by the wet drop on the nipple?
Rava's first theory
The place milk emerges from is like a 'spring' — inherently impure once the woman is, requiring no susceptibility at all (citing a mishna: milk transmits impurity even when not 'desired,' unlike ordinary liquids).
Objection
But a baraita on the nine liquids of a zav treats milk like ordinary liquids (quarter-log threshold), not like saliva or urine (impure in any amount) — which a 'spring' status would require!
Rava's real answer
The true reason the child stays pure: it is doubtful whether he nursed the full threshold amount at all, and even if he did, doubtful whether it was within the required time-window — both uncertainties favor purity.
Result: A halakhic leniency built entirely on layered uncertainty (safek), not on any special exemption for nursing.

🍷 Wine and Temple Service — Three Readings of 'Wine and Strong Drink'

Leviticus 10:9 forbids a priest from drinking before entering the Tent of Meeting — but how much, and of what?

The first tanna

  • 'Strong drink' (שֵׁכָר) means specifically wine, learned by verbal analogy from the nazirite verse
  • Only a quarter-log of forty-day-old (fermented) wine triggers death at the hand of Heaven
  • Any lesser amount of wine is still forbidden by ordinary prohibition, just not the death penalty

Rabbi Yehuda

  • 'Strong drink' includes any intoxicating beverage, not just wine
  • Wine specifically carries the death penalty; other intoxicants carry only an ordinary prohibition

Rabbi Elazar

  • 'Wine and strong drink' is one phrase, not two items — meaning wine consumed 'in its intoxicating manner'
  • Interrupting the drinking, or adding any water, exempts him entirely — the halakha follows this view
Real-world footnote: Rav Ashi once postponed a ruling for Rav Aḥa of Huzal because he had just drunk wine at a festival meal — even diluted wine impairs judgment once more than a quarter-log was drunk.

🍽️ One Bite, Five Offerings

The new mishna's extreme case: a single act of eating triggers overlapping liability
Offering owedWhy
Sin offering #1For eating forbidden fat (chelev)
Sin offering #2For eating notar (leftover consecrated meat past its time)
Sin offering #3For eating consecrated food while ritually impure
Sin offering #4For eating on Yom Kippur
Guilt offeringFor misuse of consecrated property (me'ila)
Rabbi Meir's rejected addition: He argued that if this bite also involved carrying the food from a private to a public domain on Shabbat, a sixth offering (for Shabbat desecration) should apply. The Rabbis disagreed: that liability stems from the act of carrying, not from the act of eating, so it does not belong on this list.
