📖 Overview: Opening the Gate to Karet

Keritot 2a–2b
שְׁלֹשִׁים וָשֵׁשׁ כָּרֵיתוֹת בְּתוֹרָה
The scene: Masechet Keritot opens with its own mishna listing thirty-six transgressions for which the Torah prescribes karet (excision) for intentional violation and a sin offering for unwitting violation. The list spans forbidden sexual relations, blasphemy, idolatry, Molekh, sorcery, Shabbat desecration, impurity in sacred contexts, forbidden fats and blood, notar and piggul, slaughtering offerings outside the Temple, chametz on Pesach, transgressions of Yom Kippur, compounding the anointing oil or incense, and the positive mitzvot of the Paschal offering and circumcision. The Gemara immediately asks why the mishna bothers to state the number thirty-six at all, and Rabbi Yoḥanan answers that the tally teaches a halakha: one who unwittingly violates all thirty-six in a single lapse of awareness is liable to bring a separate sin offering for each. The Gemara then surveys a whole series of other numbered mishnayot to show that tallies always teach something, before turning to why karet for intercourse with one's sister is singled out in its own verse.

✂️ The Mishna's Thirty-Six

Every case in the Torah punishable by karet for intentional violation
CategoryExamples
Forbidden relationsMother, father's wife, daughter-in-law, male intercourse, bestiality, woman and her daughter, married woman, sister, father's/mother's/wife's sister, brother's wife, niddah
Against God/TempleBlasphemy, idol worship, giving offspring to Molekh, necromancy, desecrating Shabbat
Sacrificial impurityImpure person eating sacrificial food, impure person entering the Temple, eating forbidden fat, blood, notar, piggul, slaughtering and offering outside the Temple
Calendar/anointingEating chametz on Pesach, eating or working on Yom Kippur, blending the anointing oil or incense, applying the anointing oil
Positive mitzvotFailing to bring the Paschal offering; failing to circumcise
The pattern: For each of these: intentional violation brings karet; unwitting violation brings a sin offering; and uncertainty brings a provisional guilt offering (asham talui) — except for one who defiles the Temple, says Rabbi Meir, since that case already carries a sliding-scale offering.

🔢 Why State the Number at All?

The Gemara's opening question on the mishna
❓ Why does the mishna say 'thirty-six'? One could simply count the list!
↓
💡 Rabbi Yoḥanan: the tally teaches that if one performed all thirty-six in one lapse of awareness, he is liable for each separately
↓
📜 Proof text: 'For whoever shall do any of these abominations...shall be cut off' (Leviticus 18:29) — teaches liability, not exemption
↓
Possible reading
One who transgresses all thirty-six together is liable for only one sin offering, like transgressing once
Actual halakha
He brings thirty-six separate sin offerings — one for each prohibition
Result: The number in the mishna is not decorative — it fixes how many separate offerings a person owes.

📋 Tallies Elsewhere in the Mishna

The Gemara shows every numbered list in the Mishna excludes a dissenting opinion

Excluding a larger count

  • 39 primary labors (Shabbat 73a) — teaches liability for each if done in one lapse
  • 4 mechusrei kappara — excludes Rabbi Eliezer ben Ya'akov's 'five' (adding a convert)
  • 4 primary categories of damage (Bava Kamma 2a) — excludes Rabbi Oshaya's 13, Rabbi Ḥiyya's 24

Excluding a smaller count

  • 4 who bring an offering for intentional as for unwitting sin — excludes Rabbi Shimon, who removes the false oath on a deposit
  • 5 who bring one offering for many transgressions — needed for the nazirite clause

Setting up a later clause

  • 5 who bring one offering for many transgressions — sets up the case of a nazirite impure on the seventh day twice, per Rabbi Yosei bei Rabbi Yehuda
  • 5 who bring a sliding-scale offering — excludes Rabbi Eliezer, who has the king bring a goat instead
Method: Every time the Mishna states a number, the Gemara assumes it comes to exclude some other tanna's count — never mere description.

👭 Why Is Karet for One's Sister Singled Out?

Leviticus 20:17 states karet for a sister separately from the general verse — why?
Gemara
Granted one is not entirely exempt for violating all forbidden relations together — Leviticus 18:29 says 'whoever does any of these abominations...shall be cut off'. But why not say: one transgression, one offering; all of them together in one lapse, still only one offering?
R' Yoḥanan
That is why karet for one's sister was singled out (Leviticus 20:17) — to separate the prohibitions, teaching a distinct liability for each forbidden relation.
Rav Beivai
Objects: say instead that only intercourse with a sister — explicitly detailed — earns its own offering, while all the other relations done together in one lapse still yield only one!
Challenge
But doesn't Rav Beivai accept the hermeneutical rule that something singled out from a generalization teaches its halakha for the whole generalization — as with peace offerings among consecrated foods?
Rav Beivai
That rule applies only to cases similar to what was singled out. A sister is unique: forbidden forever, with no possible permission in her brother's lifetime — unlike a married woman, whom divorce can permit.
Resolution: Rabbi Yona (or Rav Huna b. Rav Yehoshua) answers from the juxtaposition in Leviticus 18:29 itself: all forbidden relations are equated to the sister for separate liability, even without full similarity.

⚖️ Rabbi Yitzḥak's Different Reading

A dissenting tanna reassigns the purpose of the sister-verse entirely
1️⃣ Rabbi Yitzḥak: all cases of forbidden relations were already included in the general karet verse (Lev. 18:29) — nothing new there
↓
2️⃣ So why single out karet for a sister (Lev. 20:17)? Not to separate liability, but to establish that she is judged by karet, not lashes
↓
3️⃣ Separation of liability, for Rabbi Yitzḥak, instead comes from a different verse: 'a woman...as long as she is impure by her menstruation' (Lev. 18:19) — its extra words teach a separate penalty for each woman
↓
4️⃣ Then what does 'his sister' at the verse's end (Lev. 20:17) add? It teaches separate liability for a woman who is simultaneously one's sister, father's sister, and mother's sister — three offerings for one act
Takeaway: Two tannaim reach the same practical halakha of separate liability for each forbidden relation, but derive it from two different verses with two different roles for the sister-verse.
