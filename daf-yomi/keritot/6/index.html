📖 Overview: The Incense — Composition, Liability, and Precision

Keritot 6a–6b
הַמְפַטֵּם אֶת הַקְּטֹרֶת לְלַמֵּד בָּהּ אוֹ לְמוֹסְרָהּ לַצִּיבּוּר – פָּטוּר, לְהָרִיחַ בָּהּ – חַיָּיב
The scene: After a brief aggadic note on good omens in anointing (David and Solomon anointed from a horn, whose kingships endured, versus Saul and Jehu anointed from a jug, whose kingships did not), the Gemara turns fully to the incense. A baraita defines who is liable for blending it — for personal use versus for teaching or communal service — and distinguishes smelling incense as it burns (liable for misuse of consecrated property) from smelling it once its smoke has risen (exempt). The daf then details the incense's eleven spices and their exact weights, the extra three maneh set aside for Yom Kippur, why blending it in half-batches is nonetheless liable (unlike the anointing oil), and closes with a dispute over whether applying anointing oil to a king or priest a second time, after they are already anointed, still creates liability.

🕯️ Smelling the Incense — Liable or Not?

A baraita maps out exactly which uses of incense trigger which liability
ActionLiability
Blending incense to learn its formula, or to supply the communityExempt
Blending incense in order to smell it personallyLiable to karet (Exodus 30:38)
Smelling incense while it burns on the altar (its 'mitzva moment')Liable to a guilt offering for misuse of consecrated property (me'ila)
Smelling incense after its smoke has already risenExempt — its mitzva has already been performed
Exception noted: The Gemara flags that removing the Temple's altar ashes (terumat hadeshen) carries misuse liability even after its mitzva is complete — but this is one of a paired 'two verses that come as one' exception (with the broken-necked heifer), not a general rule.

🌿 Eleven Spices, Precisely Weighed

The incense recipe and its strict rules
1️⃣ Balm, onycha, galbanum, frankincense — 70 maneh each; myrrh, cassia, spikenard, saffron — 16 maneh each; costus 12, aromatic bark 3, cinnamon 9
↓
2️⃣ Plus lye for polishing the onycha, Cyprus wine for soaking it, a quarter-kav of Sodomite salt, and a trace of 'smoke-raiser' (Rabbi Natan adds Jordan amber)
↓
3️⃣ Adding honey disqualifies the incense entirely (Lev. 2:11); omitting even one spice makes the preparer liable to death at the hand of Heaven
↓
4️⃣ Total yearly weight: 368 maneh — 365 for the days of the solar year, plus 3 extra maneh, ground extra fine, for the High Priest's handful on Yom Kippur
Leftovers: Unused incense from the year's 3 extra maneh was desacralized by transfer onto Temple artisans' wages, then repurchased with the new year's shekels.

🔢 Deriving 'Eleven' from the Verse

Rabbi Yoḥanan: God told Moses eleven spice-ingredients at Sinai — where is this hinted?
📜 Exodus 30:34: 'Take for you spices [סַמִּים], stacte, and onycha, and galbanum; spices [סַמִּים] with pure frankincense'
↓
🔢 Rav Huna's count: 'spices' (plural) = 2; stacte/onycha/galbanum = 3, totaling 5; second 'spices' doubles that total to 10; 'pure frankincense' = 1 more → 11
↓
Objection
Maybe read as generalization–detail–generalization: only items like stacte/onycha/galbanum (smoke rises, scent diffuses) are included — no fixed number 11
Reish Lakish's answer
The very word ketoret (incense) means 'that which produces smoke and rises' — this itself is the defining criterion, independent of a count
↓
✅ Both readings converge: eleven distinct spices are required, each necessary since no two could be derived from a shared trait
Aggadic note: Rav Ḥana bar Bizna: any communal fast lacking the participation of some 'sinners' is incomplete — just as the foul-smelling galbanum is still counted among the incense's fragrant spices.

⚖️ Oil vs. Incense on 'Halves'

Why does blending only half the specified batch differ between the two?

Anointing Oil — half-batch exempt

  • Verse: 'according to its composition...like it' (Ex. 30:32) — only the full, exact composition is forbidden
  • A partial blend simply isn't 'like it'

Incense — half-batch liable

  • Verse: 'the incense that you shall prepare' (Ex. 30:37) — covers any act of its preparation
  • Real precedent: every 60–70 years, leftover incense piled up enough that the new year's blend needed only a half-batch
  • Since half-batches were routinely burned (morning and evening), a private half-blend is equally liable — Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, in the name of the Deputy High Priest
Limit noted: Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel had heard this ruling only for halves — not for thirds or quarters.

👑 Anointing an Already-Anointed King or Priest

Does applying more anointing oil to someone already validly anointed create new liability?
Baraita
Applying anointing oil to animals, vessels, gentiles, or corpses is exempt. But what about anointing a king or priest a second time, after he is already anointed?
R' Meir
Liable — for any amount. The verse 'upon a stranger' (Ex. 30:33) now applies, since once already anointed, there is no more mitzva to anoint him — he has become a 'stranger' to the act.
R' Yehuda
Exempt. Liability requires someone who was a 'stranger' to the anointing from beginning to end — but a king or priest was never a stranger to it; they were the intended recipients from the start.
Ordinary person
For an ordinary person (never eligible for anointing at all), Rabbi Yehuda agrees: liable, with a minimum of an olive-bulk — derived from the general legal measure for 'giving' (netina).
Underlying dispute: Rabbi Meir measures liability by any amount, deriving 'placing' oil directly from the act of anointing itself; Rabbi Yehuda instead analogizes 'placing upon a stranger' to ordinary 'giving,' which requires a minimum of an olive-bulk.
