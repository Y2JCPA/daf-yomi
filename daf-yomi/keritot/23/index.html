📖 Overview: Closing the Chapter on Doubtful Sins

Keritot 23a–23b
יֵצֵא וְיִרְעֶה בָּעֵדֶר
The scene: The daf works through the mishna's full grid of two-piece scenarios — forbidden fat paired with non-sacred meat, consecrated food, sacrificial forbidden fat, or notar — tracking exactly which offerings one or two people owe in each combination, and testing whether Rabbi Shimon's rulings are internally consistent regarding whether one prohibition can take effect atop another. The Gemara concludes that sacrificial food is a unique case where the Torah does allow layered prohibitions, unlike ordinary non-sacred items. The chapter then closes, and a new mishna opens: what happens to an animal already designated as a provisional guilt offering when it becomes known that no sin was ever committed — compared point by point with a definite guilt offering, an ox sentenced to stoning, and the decapitated heifer of an unsolved murder, each handled differently depending on exactly which stage of its process had been reached.

🐖 Does Notar's Prohibition 'Land' on Already-Forbidden Fat?

Testing whether Rabbi Shimon consistently rejects layered prohibitions
Rava
Rabbi Shimon rules THREE sin offerings for forbidden fat that is also notar — meaning the notar prohibition takes effect even though the fat prohibition already applied. But doesn't Rabbi Shimon elsewhere reject this? He exempts one who eats neveila on Yom Kippur, since the neveila prohibition already covers the meat!
First attempt
Perhaps notar (a SEVERE prohibition) can land on the fat's prohibition, even though Yom Kippur's SEVERE prohibition can't land on neveila's LENIENT one — severity determines direction.
Rejected
That can't be right — neveila is lenient (lashes only) and Yom Kippur is severe (karet), yet the severe one still doesn't land on the lenient one. The pattern doesn't hold.
Resolution
Rather: specifically for SACRIFICIAL food, the Torah reveals that prohibitions DO stack — proven from the impurity prohibition landing on sacrificial portions already forbidden by both the 'ascend to the altar' rule AND the forbidden-fat prohibition (Lev. 7:20, 'which belong to the Lord').
A parallel proof: Rebbi similarly holds that misuse of consecrated property (a lenient, death-at-Heaven's-hand prohibition) lands atop the SEVERE forbidden-fat prohibition specifically for offerings — confirming sacrificial food is genuinely exceptional.

💰 Why Doesn't Spoiled Notar Need a Guilt Offering Too?

If notar is sacrificial food, shouldn't eating it also trigger misuse liability?
1️⃣ Rava's question: notar is sacrificial food — so eating it should ALSO require a definite guilt offering for misuse, alongside the three sin offerings
↓
2️⃣ Rav Naḥman's answer: this particular case involves notar worth LESS than a peruta — no misuse liability applies below that threshold
↓
3️⃣ Challenge: but the mishna's OWN earlier clause (fat + sacrificial fat) explicitly requires a peruta's worth to generate a definite guilt offering — so why treat notar differently?
↓
4️⃣ Resolved: notar specifically has lost value through spoilage — by the time it's notar, most people wouldn't pay for it. Two further explanations offered: a SMALL piece of notar vs. a LARGE one, or notar in SUMMER (spoils fast) vs. WINTER (stays edible longer)
Why it matters: This resolves an apparent inconsistency between different clauses of the same mishna by locating a genuine factual difference in the value of the food involved.

🐑 What Happens to a Discovered-Innocent Guilt Offering?

A new mishna compares four cases where a designated offering turns out to be unnecessary

Provisional guilt offering

  • Before slaughter: R' Meir — released to graze (never truly consecrated); Rabbis — stays consecrated (must become blemished, then sold); R' Eliezer — sacrifice it anyway, it may cover an UNKNOWN sin
  • After slaughter: blood poured out, meat burned
  • After blood sprinkled: meat may be eaten (R' Yosei extends this even to blood still in the cup)

Definite guilt offering, stoned ox, decapitated heifer

  • Definite guilt offering: before slaughter, released to graze; after slaughter, buried (never eaten, unlike provisional); after blood sprinkled, still burned, not eaten
  • Stoned ox: once actually stoned, benefit becomes PERMITTED regardless of later exoneration
  • Decapitated heifer: once its neck is broken, it's buried in place — its purpose (atoning for the uncertainty) is already fulfilled and cannot be undone
The pattern across all four: Each case is judged by whether its defining ritual act (slaughter, stoning, decapitation) has already occurred — before that point, discovery of the truth cancels everything; after it, the act's consequences are generally locked in, though exactly how differs case by case.

❤️ Why Do Rabbi Meir and the Rabbis Disagree?

The philosophical root of the provisional-guilt-offering dispute
Rabbi Meir
Since it turns out he never needed this animal, its consecration is retroactively nullified — as though it was never sanctified at all.
The Rabbis
No — at the moment he consecrated it, his conscience was genuinely troubled by uncertainty. That sincere resolve to consecrate stands, regardless of what's later discovered.
A Sage's clarification
This dispute applies in BOTH directions: even when it turns out he DID sin (needing an ordinary sin offering instead), Rabbi Meir still releases the provisional animal to graze — since at the moment of consecration, he didn't yet KNOW he had sinned.
Symmetric case
And even when it turns out he did NOT sin, the Rabbis still hold the animal consecrated — since his pangs of conscience at the time were real, independent of the eventual outcome.
Underlying question: Does consecration depend on the objective facts turning out to justify it, or on the subjective sincerity of the person's state of mind at the moment he acted?
