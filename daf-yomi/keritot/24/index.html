📖 Overview: Consecration, Conscience, and Ownerless Property

Keritot 24a–24b
מִתּוֹךְ שֶׁלִּבּוֹ נוֹקְפוֹ גּוֹמֵר וּמַקְדִּישׁוֹ
The scene: The daf refines exactly when the Rabbis' and Rabbi Meir's dispute about a mistakenly-consecrated provisional guilt offering applies, identifying two cases where even Rabbi Meir concedes the animal stays consecrated: when a backup offering was designated as insurance, and — per Rav Yehuda in the name of Rav — when the original designation rested on witness testimony that was later discredited. This leads into a related dispute between Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Yoḥanan, and a broader survey of cases where relinquished ownership makes property available to anyone who claims it — a stoned ox, an idolatrous city, a refused gift. The daf then resolves several apparent contradictions in the mishna about disqualified offerings — burial versus burning, eating versus not — before closing with a dispute over exactly when the prohibition on benefiting from a decapitated heifer (eglah arufah) takes effect.

🐏 When Even Rabbi Meir Concedes

Two cases where the animal stays consecrated despite Rabbi Meir's general view
Case 1: Backup offering
Someone designates TWO provisional guilt offerings as insurance, in case one is lost, and achieves atonement with one. Rabbi Meir agrees the second stays consecrated — why? Because designating a spare in the first place reveals genuine, deep anxiety about sin, unlike the ordinary case.
Case 2: Witness-based designation
Rav Yehuda (Rav): if witnesses testify someone may have sinned, prompting him to designate an offering, and the witnesses are later proven conspiring — even the RABBIS concede it's released to graze! He never truly relied on them, always suspecting a rebuttal might come.
Rava's challenge
But the mishna treats a stoned ox with conspiring witnesses differently — doesn't THAT count as the same category, yet the Rabbis don't release the guilt offering there?
Abaye's resolution
No — the ox case may involve the 'victim' walking in ALIVE (undeniable proof), not conspiring witnesses at all. The two cases aren't actually parallel.
The pattern: Both concessions trace back to the same root question — did the person's INITIAL act of consecration reflect genuine, settled conviction, or provisional hedging?

⚖️ Meal Offering of Jealousy or Genuine Atonement?

Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Yoḥanan dispute what happens when the witnesses are discredited

Rabbi Elazar: compare to sotah's meal offering

  • A sotah's jealousy-offering, once her accusing witnesses are discredited, becomes non-sacred entirely
  • The provisional guilt offering should work the same way — released to non-sacred status

Rabbi Yoḥanan: still consecrated, disqualified

  • The sotah's offering never sought ATONEMENT — only clarification of guilt or innocence; once clarified, its purpose is simply gone
  • A provisional guilt offering seeks genuine ATONEMENT — and genuine pangs of conscience at the moment of consecration keep it sacred regardless of the later outcome
Why the analogy fails for Rabbi Yoḥanan: Two offerings can look procedurally similar (both depend on witness testimony) while serving fundamentally different halakhic purposes — investigation versus atonement — which is enough to break the comparison.

🏚️ Anyone Who Takes Possession Acquires It

A chain of cases where relinquished ownership opens property to any claimant
CaseWhy ownership is relinquished
Stoned ox, witnesses later discredited (Rabbi Yoḥanan)The owner assumed the testimony was true and gave up on the animal — UNLESS the accusation was that he himself committed bestiality, since he knows that's false and would actively fight the charge
Idolatrous city, witnesses later discreditedWith many residents, each individually assumes 'others sinned, not me' and gives up his own property rather than fight — a crowd psychology of resignation
Refused gift ('I don't want it')Reish Lakish: the item becomes ownerless the moment refusal is stated — challenged by several baraitot, ultimately reconciled by distinguishing precise wording ('I don't want it' vs. 'I have no legal dealings with it')
The unifying test: In each case, the Gemara asks: does this specific person have a real, ongoing reason to contest the situation, or has he genuinely given up? That answer alone determines whether the property becomes free for anyone to claim.

🔥 Buried or Burned? Eaten or Not?

Resolving apparent contradictions in the disqualified-offering mishna
1️⃣ Apparent contradiction: a provisional guilt offering discovered innocent AFTER slaughter is BURNED, but a definite guilt offering in the same position is BURIED — Rabbi Elazar initially calls the mishna 'disjointed' (different authors)
↓
2️⃣ Rabba's resolution: no contradiction at all — a definite guilt offering was consecrated on the ASSUMPTION of need, so once unneeded, it's simply never been sacred (treated like ordinary meat, buried); a provisional offering was consecrated from real anxiety, so it stays sacred, but disqualified (hence burned, its normal fate)
↓
3️⃣ Rav Ashi refines further: burning applies specifically because the animal now LOOKS like a disqualified offering — this explains even the definite guilt offering case, once blood was already sprinkled before discovery
↓
4️⃣ Separately: why can the MEAT still be eaten if blood was sprinkled before the discovery of innocence, despite now having full knowledge? Rava: 'he did not know, and shall be forgiven' (Lev. 5:18) — the critical moment is knowledge AT THE TIME of atonement (the sprinkling), not afterward
Rabbi Yosei's extension: Even blood still sitting in the cup, not yet sprinkled, is treated as 'as good as sprinkled' (Rabbi Shimon's principle) — so the offering remains valid and edible even in that intermediate state.

🐄 When Does the Heifer's Prohibition Begin?

A closing dispute: from what moment is deriving benefit from an eglah arufah forbidden?
Rav Hamnuna
The prohibition takes effect from when the animal is still ALIVE — even before its neck is broken, it is already consecrated to this purpose and off-limits.
Rava
The prohibition only takes effect AFTER the decapitation itself — the physical act of breaking its neck is what triggers the forbidden status.
The challenge
Rava's view makes intuitive sense — the prohibition activates from the moment 'an action was performed on it.' But how can Rav Hamnuna explain a prohibition beginning even before any action occurs at all?
Left open: The Gemara flags this as a genuine difficulty for Rav Hamnuna's position, setting up further analysis in the sugya that follows.
