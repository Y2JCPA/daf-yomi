📖 Overview: Boundaries of Liability, Atonement, and a New Mishna on Miscarriage

Keritot 7a–7b
יֵשׁ מְבִיאוֹת קׇרְבָּן וְנֶאֱכָל, וְיֵשׁ מְבִיאוֹת קׇרְבָּן וְאֵינוֹ נֶאֱכָל, וְיֵשׁ שֶׁאֵינָם מְבִיאוֹת
The scene: The daf opens by finishing the anointing-oil discussion with the case of a priest's daughter who eats teruma after marrying out of the priesthood, then returns to the original mishna's list of exceptions: why the one who defiles the Temple does not bring a provisional guilt offering for uncertainty, and whether the same exclusion should apply to a sin whose Yom Kippur has already passed. This opens into a broader debate about whether Yom Kippur atones even without repentance, and a related dispute between Rabbi Akiva and the Rabbis over whether the unwitting blasphemer must bring a sin offering at all. The daf then turns a corner entirely: a new mishna opens tractate Keritot's discussion of the sin offering a woman brings after childbirit or miscarriage, classifying which fetal forms obligate an offering that may be eaten, which obligate one that may not, and which exempt her entirely.

Her liability shifts with her marital status — a case tied to the earlier dispute over anointing 'strangers'

Married an Israelite (still eligible to return to teruma if widowed/divorced childless)Principal only, no added fifthBurning (as a priest's daughter)
Married someone permanently disqualifying (Rabbi Meir)Principal and added fifthStrangulation (as an Israelite)
Married someone permanently disqualifying (the Rabbis)Principal only, no added fifthBurning — she retains her priestly status conceptually
Connected dispute: Rav Yosef links this to Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehuda's disagreement over anointing a 'stranger': Rabbi Yehuda requires the person to be a stranger from beginning to end, which is why the Rabbis (aligned with his view) still treat her, in some sense, as a priest's daughter.

🚫 Two Exceptions to the Provisional Guilt Offering

The mishna excludes one case explicitly — should Yom Kippur create another?
1️⃣ Mishna's stated exception: one who defiles the Temple or its sacred food does not bring a provisional guilt offering — he already owes a sliding-scale offering for the definite case
↓
2️⃣ Proposed addition: shouldn't the mishna also exclude one whose Yom Kippur has passed, since Yom Kippur already atoned for any possible sin?
↓
3️⃣ Reish Lakish: correct — but the mishna's exceptions cover cases where a sin still exists and the Torah exempts it anyway; after Yom Kippur, the sin no longer exists at all, so it needs no separate mention
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Yoḥanan disagrees: the true case needing exclusion is one who rejects Yom Kippur's atonement outright — for him, atonement does not take effect, so he would still owe an offering later
Underlying question: Can Yom Kippur atone for a sin the sinner insists it should not atone for? This launches the next major debate.

🕊️ Does Yom Kippur Atone Without Repentance?

Two baraitot in the Sifra appear to contradict each other
Baraita 1
By logical inference from sin and guilt offerings (which atone only for those who repent), Yom Kippur too should atone only for the penitent.
Baraita 2
'It is Yom Kippur' (Lev. 23:27) — atones in any case, regardless of fasting, labor, or repentance.
Abaye's resolution
No contradiction: the first baraita reflects Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's report of Rabbi Yehuda's opinion; the second reflects Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's own view — that Yom Kippur atones even without repentance, except for three severe sins (denying God, twisting Torah meaning, breaking the covenant of circumcision).
Rava's resolution
Both baraitot state Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's own view; he concedes only that Yom Kippur cannot atone for the very transgression of desecrating Yom Kippur itself — since a person eating or working on Yom Kippur may die before daytime atonement can apply.
Related retraction: Rava initially held that a sin offering atones even if its owner insists 'let it not atone for me' — but retracted this after finding a baraita showing such a stipulation blocks atonement.

🗣️ Does the Blasphemer Bring an Offering?

Rabbi Akiva and the Rabbis dispute whether an unwitting blasphemer needs a sin offering

Rabbi Akiva — liable

  • Karet for blasphemy (Numbers 15:30) is written in a passage that discusses an offering — that placement itself teaches liability
  • The Rabbis' challenge (why then not Pesach/milah, which also carry karet?) is answered: it's specifically karet near an offering-context that counts

The Rabbis — exempt

  • Blasphemy involves no physical action — 'one law for him who acts unwittingly' (Numbers 15:29) excludes pure speech
  • The karet clause instead teaches, by verbal analogy ('shall bear his sin'), that one who misses the second Paschal offering is liable to karet
A second dispute: Isi ben Yehuda reads 'blasphemes the Lord' as comparing the sinner to one who damages God's own 'bowl' (a severe image); Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya reads it more mildly, and elsewhere even identifies the verse's blasphemer with an idol worshipper — against the Rabbis, who insist it means one who curses God's Name.

🤰 Which Miscarriages Require a Sin Offering?

The new mishna opens with three categories, based on the form of what was miscarried
CategoryExamples
Offering brought, eatenFetus shaped like a domesticated/undomesticated animal or bird (R' Meir) — the Rabbis require human form; a 'sandal' fetus, placenta, developed amniotic sac, or fetus emerging in pieces; also a Canaanite maidservant, derived from the extra word 'woman'
Offering brought, not eatenMiscarrying an unidentified mass; or two women miscarrying together where only one type obligates an offering and it's unknown which — unless (R' Yosei) both stood together, in which case it is eaten
No offering at allAmniotic sac full of water, blood, or discolored matter; fetus shaped like fish, locusts, or vermin; miscarriage on day forty; a caesarean birth (though R' Shimon obligates this last case, deriving it from a seemingly superfluous 'she bears')
Practical footnote: Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel go on to dispute a related case: one who miscarries on the night before her eighty-first day (when offerings normally cannot be brought) — Beit Hillel obligate her in a second offering, Beit Shammai exempt her, arguing the night was never fit for sacrifice at all.
