📖 Overview: Settling a Doctrine, Then Pricing an Offering

Keritot 10a–10b
יֵשׁ מֵבִיא בְּדַלּוּת וַעֲשִׁירוּת, וְיֵשׁ מֵבִיא בְּדַלּוּת וְיֵשׁ מֵבִיא בְּדַלֵּי דַלּוּת
The scene: The daf resolves the previous daf's open question: does Rabbi Yehuda treat a woman's 'second' overlapping birth or miscarriage as though it truly happened, or as though it didn't? Through a series of proofs from baraitot about the Paschal offering and about counting purification days, the Gemara concludes that Rabbi Yehuda applies this fiction only when it creates a stringency for the woman, never when it would grant her a leniency. The Gemara then returns to the mishna's sliding-scale offerings, cataloging exactly what each of the five relevant transgressors brings at each level of wealth, working out through careful arithmetic just how little an offering can be worth. The daf closes with a new mishna cataloging five ways the case of an espoused maidservant diverges from every other forbidden relation.

🤱 Rabbi Yehuda's Second Offspring: Stringency Only

A chain of proofs settles how far his legal fiction extends
❓ Does Rabbi Yehuda's 'second offspring is as though nonexistent' rule (for offerings) also apply to ritual purity — and if so, does it ever work in the woman's favor?
↓
🐑 Rav Huna's proof attempt from a Paschal-offering baraita is redirected: Rav Ḥisda reads it as two sons born on consecutive days, so 'day 40' is really day 41 of the first — testing whether the second birth delays her purity
↓
📜 Multiple baraitot on 'sixty days,' 'thirty days,' and 'six days' each independently teach that her count must be entirely continuous — never interrupted or dispersed by an intervening birth
↓
🔍 Each of these continuity-verses only makes sense according to Rabbi Yehuda, since the Rabbis already count purely from the second birth without dispersal
↓
✅ Conclusion: Rabbi Yehuda's fiction that the second offspring 'doesn't exist' applies only for stringency (delaying her offering) — for purity, where nonexistence would be a leniency, he treats the second birth as fully real and interrupting
Takeaway: A legal fiction in halakha is never applied uniformly for its own sake — its scope is fixed by which direction, stringent or lenient, the underlying law actually points.

🧮 Calculating the Value of a Peruta Offering

How cheap can the extreme-poverty meal offering really be?
1️⃣ A guilt offering ram must be worth 2 sela (Lev. 5:15); therefore a lamb (used in poverty tiers) is worth 1 sela
↓
2️⃣ The historical nest-price crisis (daf 8) shows a poverty-tier bird-pair cost a quarter-dinar — 1/16th of a sela's value
↓
3️⃣ Rava: the whole calculation is better anchored to a woman after childbirth, whose single bird is worth 1/32 of a lamb (rather than the 1/16 ratio used for the other sliding-scale cases)
↓
4️⃣ Applying that same 1/32 ratio one level further down (extreme poverty relative to poverty) yields a value just under one peruta — rounded up, since the Torah does not sanction an offering worth less than a peruta
Why it matters: This math establishes just how minimal the Torah's leniency toward poverty can go — down to the smallest coin in circulation, but never lower.

👥 Maidservant vs. Every Other Forbidden Relation

A new mishna: five ways intercourse with an espoused maidservant diverges from all other forbidden relations

All other forbidden relations

  • Unwitting violation brings a sin offering (a female animal)
  • Man and woman are equally liable for lashes and for an offering
  • Even the initial stage of intercourse (ha'meareh) is treated like full completion — liable for each act separately

The espoused maidservant

  • Unwitting violation brings a guilt offering (a male animal)
  • Only the woman receives lashes; only the man brings an offering — the two are never equal
  • Liability requires a completed act; multiple acts with her collapse into a single offering (as taught on daf 9)
The mishna's framing: The Torah imposed a stringency on the maidservant case regarding repeated acts (only full completion counts, and even so, one offering covers many), while treating the punishment and offering structure far more leniently than for other forbidden relations.

🐑 Whose Case Explains the Paschal-Offering Baraita?

A challenging baraita about a woman after childbirth eating the Paschal offering while still technically impure
Rav Huna
A baraita states: one slaughters and sprinkles the Paschal offering's blood on behalf of a woman after childbirth on day 40 (for a male) or day 80 (for a female) — proof that Rabbi Yehuda treats her as pure enough by then!
Challenge
But she's still impure that night and cannot eat sacrificial food until day 41/81 — how could she eat the Paschal offering?
Rav Ḥisda
The baraita describes two sons born on consecutive days — 'day 40' is really day 41 relative to the first son, so she qualifies through the first birth's timeline.
Further test
If Rabbi Yehuda held the second birth interrupts purity even for leniency, this reading collapses — so the baraita only works if his fiction is stringency-only, confirming the daf's earlier conclusion.
Rava's alternative
Proposes instead that this is a Paschal offering brought while the community is impure — permitted even for those normally excluded, once they've immersed, though a 'lacking-atonement' status still limits some until nightfall of the qualifying day.
Bottom line: Multiple competing readings of one baraita all converge on confirming how narrowly Rabbi Yehuda's legal fiction actually operates.
