📖 Overview: Value, Substitution, and Whose Offering Is It?

Keritot 27a–27b
אָדָם מִתְכַּפֵּר בִּשְׁבַח הֶקְדֵּשׁ, אוֹ לֹא
The scene: The daf resolves the previous daf's terminology puzzle about 'his misuse' before turning to a foundational question: if a consecrated animal's market value rises while in the owner's possession, can he use that appreciation toward his offering, or must the offering's value match the amount originally designated? Several proof attempts are tested and rejected before a baraita settles the matter. Rabbi Elazar then raises this same dilemma independently, prompting Rabbi Yoḥanan's surprise that his own student hadn't already absorbed the answer — a pattern repeated with two more of Rabbi Elazar's dilemmas, about permanently disqualified sacred animals and about offerings when market prices shift. The daf's second half turns to two new mishnayot: one establishing that a son can never bring his deceased father's designated sin offering, nor may anyone redirect an offering from one sin to another, and another detailing exactly how a person may substitute between sliding-scale offering tiers as his financial circumstances change.

📈 Can a Person Achieve Atonement with Appreciated Value?

Rav Menashya bar Gadda's dilemma about accumulated one-fifth payments
The dilemma
If someone accumulates enough one-fifth misuse payments to total two sela, can he use that sum to purchase a guilt offering and achieve atonement — the same way an animal that grows more valuable while in his possession might count?
Attempted proofs
Several mishnayot and baraitot are tested: a ram bought cheap that appreciated on its own; a ram deliberately FATTENED (where the owner invested effort and expense); each time, the Gemara distinguishes whether the added value came from the owner's own exertion or merely happened passively
Conclusion
A baraita settles it: 'worth one sela at designation, two sela at the time of atonement — he has NOT fulfilled his obligation.' Passive appreciation does not count.
Why it matters: The distinction between effort-driven value increase (fattening) and passive market appreciation determines whether many real-world scenarios of accumulating small sums toward an offering are valid.

❓ Rabbi Elazar's Puzzling Dilemmas

Rabbi Yoḥanan's astonishment — and where these questions actually lead
כַּמָּה שָׁנִים גָּדַל זֶה בֵּינֵינוּ, וְלֹא שָׁמַע הֲלָכָה זוֹ מִמֶּנִּי
The pattern: Three times, Rabbi Elazar raises a dilemma to Rabbi Yoḥanan — the same 'appreciated value' question independently; whether permanently disqualified living consecrated animals (ba'alei ḥayim nidḥin) can ever become fit again; and what happens if lamb prices crash so low that a fixed two-sela guilt offering becomes unobtainable. Each time, Rabbi Yoḥanan expresses genuine surprise that his own student hadn't already absorbed these answers from years of study together. The Gemara traces each answer to teachings Rabbi Yoḥanan HAD given, just not in direct response to these exact dilemmas — for instance, a partnership-consecration case yields three halakhot at once, including that disqualification can indeed be permanent. But the third dilemma, about depreciated lamb prices, ends unresolved: Abaye and Rava each raise counter-cases (an ordinary sin offering; a nazirite's 'offering that comes for naught') that seem to contradict the proposed reasoning, and the Gemara simply concludes: 'this is difficult' (קַשְׁיָא).

👨‍👦 A Son Cannot Bring His Father's Sin Offering

A new mishna's rule, unpacked through a chain of verse-based exclusions
Excluded scenarioSource phrase
Son brings father's already-designated ANIMAL for a different-severity sin'His offering' (Lev. 4:23) — must be his own
Son brings father's designated animal even for an EQUALLY severe sin'His offering' repeated (Lev. 4:28) — no exception for matching severity
Son uses father's designated MONEY (not yet an animal) for his own offeringContrast with nazirite law, where unallocated money CAN pass to an heir — sin offering money cannot
A person redirects his OWN designated offering from one sin to a different one'His offering for HIS sin' — must match the specific sin, even if equally severe
The animal/money distinction: Redirecting a designated ANIMAL to a different sin fails entirely (no misuse, no atonement — it simply can't be reassigned); but redirecting designated MONEY does constitute misuse of consecrated property, since money, unlike an already-designated animal, can still be legally redirected via that specific mechanism.

🔄 Deriving 'From His Sin' and 'Concerning His Sin'

Why the Torah uses two different phrases for different tiers of the sliding-scale offering
1️⃣ 'From his sin' (מֵחַטָּאתוֹ) appears for both the lamb/goat tier AND the bird tier — teaching that money designated for one can be REDIRECTED, even partially, to the other as wealth changes
↓
2️⃣ Without this phrase appearing for BIRDS specifically, one might think money designated for a bird pair couldn't convert to flour when the person becomes even poorer — since flour isn't a 'blood offering' like lamb/bird are
↓
3️⃣ 'Concerning his sin' (עַל חַטָּאתוֹ), used specifically for the flour tier, teaches the OPPOSITE movement — that money designated for flour must be ADDED TO (not simply reassigned) when the person becomes wealthier, since flour is a different category of offering entirely
↓
4️⃣ Together, the two phrases map the full bidirectional flow: redirect freely between lamb/goat and bird (both blood offerings), but ADD to move up from flour, and simply switch categories to move down to flour
Precision matters: Every phrase's specific wording — not just its general meaning — is mined to establish the exact mechanics of converting between offering tiers.

A lamb/goat itself develops a blemishN/ARedeem it, use proceeds toward a bird — but a BIRD that develops a blemish has no redemption at all, since birds cannot be redeemed
Bottom line: The system allows fluid downward substitution (freely) and upward substitution (by adding funds), but a disqualified bird offering has no path to conversion — its money is simply lost to that specific purpose.
