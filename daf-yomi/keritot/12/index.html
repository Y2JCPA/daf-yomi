📖 Overview: Testimony, Repetition, and the Pace of Eating

Keritot 12a–12b
אָדָם נֶאֱמָן עַל עַצְמוֹ יוֹתֵר מִמֵּאָה אִישׁ
The scene: The daf works through the practical edges of the previous mishna on witnesses and sin offerings: why the Rabbis exempt someone who denies two witnesses' testimony, whether their reasoning is a broad credibility principle or a narrower 'better claim' (miggo) logic, and how that choice plays out differently for old versus new ritual impurity. Several Sages then identify additional cases where even Rabbi Meir concedes to the Rabbis, each turning on a hidden alternative claim the accused could have made. The Gemara then returns to the mishna's rules about eating forbidden substances — when two olive-bulks in one lapse of awareness combine into one liability, when eating from separate dishes creates two liabilities instead, and finally, in a new mishna, exactly how much time a person may take to eat a forbidden olive-bulk and still be liable.

🤔 A Person's Word Against Witnesses

Why do the Rabbis exempt someone who denies two witnesses' testimony?
R' Meir
A fortiori: if two witnesses can convict someone of a capital crime, surely they can obligate him in a mere offering!
The Rabbis
Not necessarily — but why exactly? Two theories: either 'a person is more credible about himself than a hundred witnesses,' or simply that he has a better claim available (miggo) — he could have said 'I did it intentionally' and been exempt, so his weaker denial is believed too.
Testing the theories
Applied to old ritual impurity (witnesses: 'you were impure yesterday'): under the miggo theory he's exempt, since he could claim 'I immersed since then.' Applied to new impurity (witnesses: 'you just became impure'): the miggo theory would make him liable, since no such claim is available.
Resolution
A baraita confirms the broader credibility principle: even for new impurity, the Rabbis hold he is simply more believable than the witnesses. Rav Naḥman rules the halakha follows Rabbi Yehuda (=the Rabbis' view) on this.
Caveat: Rav Yosef limits this: he is believed only for his own purposes (to avoid an offering), not to declare himself pure in matters affecting others.

🙅 Where Even Rabbi Meir Concedes

In each case, a hidden 'better claim' persuades even Rabbi Meir that a denial is credible
CaseThe available 'better claim'
Relations with an espoused maidservant'I did not complete the act' (Reish Lakish) — since only completed intercourse creates liability
An impure nazirite'I had my vow dissolved by a halakhic authority' (Rav Sheshet) — permitting contact otherwise forbidden
False oath of testimony'I did not intend to testify' (Abaye) — since only an intentional false oath of testimony creates liability
General patternWhenever a milder, believable alternative claim exists, even Rabbi Meir agrees the person's denial is credible against two witnesses
The exception that proves the rule: Ravina shows why plain ritual impurity from direct contact is different — there, no alternative claim can explain away 'I did not become impure' once witnesses saw the contact itself.

🍽️ One Lapse, One Offering — Unless the Dishes Divide It

The mishna's rule on eating forbidden fat twice, and how 'separate dishes' complicates it
🧀 Ate an olive-bulk of forbidden fat, then another olive-bulk, in one lapse of awareness → only one sin offering (same lapse = same liability)
↓
❓ Rabbi Zeira: but he ate two full olive-bulks — why just one offering? Abaye: separate lapses of awareness divide liability, not separate bites within one lapse
↓
🍲 Two half-olive-bulks eaten from two different dishes, same food type: Reish Lakish (name of bar Tutini) — per Rabbi Yehoshua, this creates two separate transgressions, hence liability
↓
⚖️ But could the same rule work as a leniency (two dishes of two DIFFERENT foods, still exempting him from combining to a full measure)? The mishna's phrasing shows Rabbi Yehoshua's 'dishes divide' rule applies to both stringency and leniency
↓
✅ Ravina's alternative for the 'one dish' case: it involves intervening awareness between two half-measures — valid under Rabban Gamliel's view that awareness doesn't interrupt sub-measure eating
Underlying theme: Whether two partial acts combine into liability depends on precise technical dividers — the lapse of awareness, and even the dish the food was served on.

⏱️ How Long Counts As 'Eating'?

A new mishna: the maximum time allowed to consume a forbidden olive-bulk
1️⃣ Rabbi Meir: the time allowed is as long as it takes to eat roasted grain — kernel by kernel
↓
2️⃣ The Rabbis: liability requires eating the whole olive-bulk within the longer time it takes to eat a half-loaf of bread (kdei achilat pras)
↓
3️⃣ Gemara's dilemma: is Rabbi Meir's standard a stringency (liable even if it takes all day, so long as there's no gap) or a leniency (liable only if eaten with zero interruption, even within the shorter pras-window)?
↓
4️⃣ Resolved from the Rabbis' own phrasing — 'from beginning to end' — showing the debate is about total elapsed time, not about interruptions
Why it matters: This time-window determines whether scattered small bites of a forbidden food ever combine into a liable olive-bulk.

📝 No Awareness for a Half-Measure

Rabban Gamliel's rule lets two half-measures combine even across an interruption of awareness
אֵין יְדִיעָה לַחֲצִי שִׁיעוּר
The principle: Ravina explains a puzzling case in the mishna — eating two half-olive-bulks of the same forbidden food from the same dish, which seems obviously liable — by positing that the person actually became briefly aware of his sin between the two half-bites. Whether that awareness breaks the chain depends on a known dispute, drawn from the parallel case of writing two letters on Shabbat in two lapses: Rabban Gamliel holds there is no legal 'awareness' for a mere half-measure, so the two half-bulks still combine into one liability; the Rabbis hold awareness of even a half-measure does divide the acts, so no liability results.
