📖 Overview: From the Decapitated Heifer to the Guilt Offering of the Pious

Keritot 25a–25b
אָשָׁם חֲסִידִים
The scene: The daf continues the dispute over when the prohibition on benefiting from a decapitated heifer (eglah arufah) begins, with Rav Hamnuna and Rava trading proofs from parallel mishnayot and adjusting the text to defend their positions, before the Gemara settles the matter as a genuine tannaitic dispute. A new mishna then introduces one of the more striking figures in the tractate: Bava ben Buta, who — following Rabbi Eliezer's view that a provisional guilt offering may be brought voluntarily — sacrificed one every single day except the day after Yom Kippur. This launches a debate over whether such an offering can ever be a true gift, or whether, as the Rabbis hold, it is always fundamentally obligatory. The daf closes by tracing, through several rounds of proposed verses and objections, exactly why Yom Kippur atones for uncertain sins (exempting one from ever bringing a provisional guilt offering after the day passes) while it does not atone for confirmed sins requiring a sin or guilt offering.

🐄 When Does the Heifer Actually Become Forbidden?

Rav Hamnuna and Rava trade proofs and text-adjustments
Rav Hamnuna
Proof from Ḥullin: Rabbi Shimon exempts one who slaughters the heifer (rather than decapitating it) from the 'mother and offspring' prohibition, because he holds 'slaughter that doesn't render fit for consumption isn't real slaughter' — meaning the heifer was ALREADY forbidden from benefit while alive!
Rava
Proof from OUR mishna: it says discovering innocence BEFORE decapitation lets the heifer simply 'go graze with the flock' — implying no prohibition existed yet while alive!
Counter-adjustment
Reread the mishna as 'before it became FIT for decapitation' (i.e., before reaching the valley) — consistent with my view that once fit, even while alive, it's forbidden.
Further proof
But the mishna's own reasoning says the heifer 'atones for its uncertainty' only once decapitated — while alive, no atonement has occurred, so no prohibition should apply yet either!
Resolution
This is a genuine dispute between tanna'im — a baraita comparing the heifer to other 'enabling' offerings outside the Temple confirms Rav Hamnuna's position, that prohibition begins from life itself.
Rava's silence: When first challenged, Rava had no answer and stayed silent — only later realizing he could have argued Rabbi Shimon holds the heifer IS validly killed by slaughter, contradicting a different mishna's assumption.

🙏 Bava ben Buta's Daily Offering

A new mishna: Rabbi Eliezer's view that anyone may bring a provisional guilt offering purely voluntarily
ElementDetail
Rabbi Eliezer's rulingA person may volunteer a provisional guilt offering any day, at any time — called 'the guilt offering of the pious' (אֲשַׁם חֲסִידִים)
Bava ben Buta's practiceBrought one every single day — except the day immediately after Yom Kippur
His stated wishHe swore he would have brought one even that day too, if permitted
Why he was stoppedTold to 'wait until you enter a situation of potential uncertainty' — some real doubt must exist first, even if minor
The Rabbis' opposing viewA provisional guilt offering may be brought ONLY for uncertainty about a sin that would carry karet if intentional and a sin offering if unwitting
Practical framing: Even according to those who permitted Bava ben Buta's near-daily practice, some minimal genuine uncertainty was still required — pure invention was never sanctioned.

🎁 Gift or Obligation?

Is a provisional guilt offering ever truly voluntary — and does it fully atone?
Gemara's proof for Rabbi Eliezer
If it were purely obligatory, why would someone who brought one still need a SIN offering once his sin becomes certain? He'd already have fulfilled his obligation! Since he doesn't, the guilt offering must be voluntary.
The Rabbis
Burnt and peace offerings can be voluntary, but sin and guilt offerings — including the provisional one — are always obligatory. One brings it beforehand purely to spare the person needless suffering while awaiting clarity, not as a true 'gift.'
A challenging baraita
'Five guilt offerings achieve complete atonement; the provisional guilt offering does not achieve complete atonement' — meaning if the sin later proves definite, ANOTHER offering is still required.
Ravina's reading
Alternative: the five REQUIRE nothing else once brought; the provisional guilt offering's uncertainty is resolved by something ELSE entirely — namely, Yom Kippur itself, since if the year ends before certainty arrives, no sin offering is ever needed at all.
Underlying stakes: Whether the provisional guilt offering is a gift or an obligation shapes whether Rabbi Eliezer's daily practice reflects genuine piety or a technical loophole.

🗓️ Does Yom Kippur Atone for Known Sins?

Tracing why sin/guilt-offering-level sins still require their offering even after Yom Kippur
❓ Why does the mishna say those liable to sin/guilt offerings must STILL bring them after Yom Kippur passes, while provisional-guilt-offering cases are exempt?
↓
📜 Rav Dimi (R' Ami/R' Ḥanina): Lev. 16:16 equates 'sins' with 'transgressions' — just as the scapegoat atones for INTENTIONAL transgressions (which carry no offering), it atones for unwitting sins only when THEY too carry no offering
↓
🛑 Abaye's objection: that verse discusses the INSIDE goat, which never atones for known violations at all — maybe the SCAPEGOAT (which does atone for known violations) atones for everything, offering-eligible or not!
↓
🔄 Abaye redirects to Lev. 16:21 (the scapegoat's own confession verse) for the same 'sins like transgressions' comparison — but Rav Dimi challenges: how do we know 'transgressions' here even excludes offering-eligible sins? (four cases bring offerings even for INTENTIONAL violation!)
↓
✅ Final resolution: the verse's own structure — 'iniquities' (intentional) THEN 'transgressions and ALL their sins' — teaches by extra emphasis that sins parallel to non-offering transgressions are what's atoned, excluding sins that DO carry an offering
Net result: Yom Kippur's atonement is broad but not unlimited — it reaches purely 'between a person and God' sins, not sins the Torah has already assigned a specific offering to resolve.

🤫 A Sin Only God Knows

Why the provisional guilt offering specifically becomes unnecessary once Yom Kippur passes
מִכֹּל חַטֹּאתֵיכֶם לִפְנֵי ה׳ תִּטְהָרוּ
The derivation: Rabbi Elazar locates the source in Leviticus 16:30: 'For on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you; from all your sins you shall be pure before the Lord.' The phrase 'before the Lord' signals a sin known only to God — precisely the character of an uncertain transgression that would otherwise require a provisional guilt offering. Since no human being (including the sinner himself) can confirm whether it occurred, Yom Kippur's atonement reaches it completely, closing the matter without further sacrifice. Rav Taḥlifa (in Rava's name) notes this reasoning does not equally prove the mishna's parallel claim about definite sin and guilt offerings, which are governed by the separate derivation explored earlier in the daf.
