📖 Overview: The Limits of 'A Sin Only God Knows'

Keritot 26a–26b
לְכׇל חַטֹּאתָם – וְלֹא לְכׇל טוּמְאֹתָם
The scene: The daf tests the principle that Yom Kippur atones only for 'a sin known only to God' against five cases that seem to fit that description just as well — a woman uncertain if her miscarriage requires an offering, an uncertain leper, an uncertain nazirite, a suspected sotah, and the decapitated heifer — showing each time why the case is really different. It then works through a rigorous three-way elimination proving that all three Torah mentions of 'when it becomes known to him' are needed, ultimately teaching that a sin offering is still owed even after Yom Kippur passes. After exploring what a provisional guilt offering actually accomplishes if a sin offering is needed anyway, the daf turns to a dispute between Rav and Rabbi Yoḥanan over whether an uncertain bird sin offering may be eaten once certainty arrives after its neck is already pinched, and closes with a new mishna tracking value shifts across paired rams purchased for a guilt offering and its associated misuse payment.

🔤 Five Cases That Look Like 'God Alone Knows' — But Aren't

Each case seems to fit Yom Kippur's atonement for hidden sin — until examined closely
CaseWhy Yom Kippur does NOT exempt it
Uncertain miscarriage (yoledet)Her offering isn't for atonement at all — it's for PURIFICATION, to permit eating sacrificial food ('for all their sins,' not 'all their impurity')
Uncertain leper (metzora)Same principle — his offering enables eating sacrificial food, not atonement, even though leprosy stems from sin
Uncertain nazirite impuritySame principle again — purification-focused, not atonement-focused
Suspected sotahAbaye: the paramour himself knows the truth, so it's not 'known only to God'; Rava: her offering exists to CLARIFY guilt, not to atone
Decapitated heifer (eglah arufah)Abaye: the murderer himself knows; Rava/Rav Pappa: a specific verse ties its atonement to bloodshed uniquely, unreachable by Yom Kippur
The recurring move: Every apparent counterexample is defused by showing the case either isn't truly 'unknown to everyone,' or the offering in question was never about atonement to begin with.

📜 Three Verses of 'Knowing' — The Elimination Process

Why does the Torah repeat 'when it becomes known to him' three separate times?
1️⃣ The phrase appears for an individual's sin offering, a Nasi's, and the community's (Lev. 4:14, 23, 28) — could any one be derived from the others?
↓
2️⃣ Individual from Nasi? No — a Nasi is uniquely exempt from the 'hearing a voice' oath category; individuals aren't
↓
3️⃣ Community from Nasi? No — a Nasi's offerings sometimes include a female animal; the community's never does
↓
4️⃣ Every combination of 'derive two from one' fails the same way — each category has some unique feature blocking the derivation
↓
5️⃣ Conclusion: since none is truly extra for its own halakha, the 'leftover' teaching applies elsewhere — that a sin offering is required even when the sin becomes known only AFTER Yom Kippur has passed
Abaye's version: Abaye reaches the same conclusion through a different verbal analogy ('mitzvot'/'mitzvot' linking all three sin-offering categories), showing multiple paths lead to the same halakha.

🛡️ What Does a Provisional Guilt Offering Actually Accomplish?

If a sin offering is still needed once certainty arrives, what was the point of the earlier offering?
Challenge
Rava derives from 'if his sin becomes known to him' (Lev. 4:28) that a sin offering is owed whenever certainty eventually arrives — even after a provisional guilt offering was already brought. So what did that earlier offering actually achieve?
Rabbi Zeira
It matters in case he dies before learning the truth — then at least he dies without that iniquity counted against him.
Rava's objection
That can't be the reason — death itself already absolves a person of sin! No special offering is needed for that.
Rava's real answer
Its true purpose is to protect the person from the anguish and suffering of living under unresolved guilt while awaiting clarity — the Torah spares the person's peace of mind, not his ultimate legal record.
Reframing the offering: A provisional guilt offering isn't really about final atonement — it is about immediate psychological and spiritual relief during genuine uncertainty.

🐦 Can the Uncertain Bird Offering Be Eaten?

Rav and Rabbi Yoḥanan dispute what happens when certainty arrives just after pinching

Rav: yes, it may be eaten

  • Once the blood is sprinkled/squeezed after pinching, atonement is achieved regardless of what's later confirmed
  • A supporting baraita (Levi) explicitly permits eating in this case

Rabbi Yoḥanan: no, it's still forbidden

  • A rabbinic decree — permitting it risks people mistakenly thinking ANY uncertain bird offering may be eaten, even in ordinary unresolved cases
  • A supporting baraita explicitly forbids eating, even forbidding BENEFIT once blood is fully processed
Underlying principle either way: Both agree the sprinkling accomplishes genuine atonement (fulfilling the woman's purification) — they disagree only on whether a protective rabbinic fence should still bar consumption.

🐏 Two Rams for Two Sela

A new mishna: tracking value shifts when two rams are purchased with guilt-offering funds
The scenario
Someone sets aside two sela for a guilt offering and buys TWO rams instead of one — misusing consecrated funds. One ram is now worth two sela; the other, ten dinars (2.5 sela). Which offering does which ram serve?
Gemara's question
What does 'the second ram covers his misuse' (מְעִילָתוֹ) actually mean — the misuse GUILT OFFERING itself, or merely the REPAYMENT (principal plus a fifth) for the stolen value?
First reading challenged
If 'misuse' meant the guilt-offering ram itself, that would mean the added one-fifth payment comes bundled WITH the ram — but the Torah (Lev. 5:16) clearly requires the one-fifth to accompany the STOLEN VALUE repayment, not the offering animal!
Resolution
So 'his misuse' in the first clause must mean the REPAYMENT for what he stole — yet the mishna's LATER clause seems to use 'misuse' to mean the guilt-offering ram itself, creating an internal inconsistency the Gemara must still resolve.
Ongoing puzzle: The mishna's own two clauses appear to use the identical term 'me'ilato' in two different senses — a tension the Gemara continues untangling into the next daf.
