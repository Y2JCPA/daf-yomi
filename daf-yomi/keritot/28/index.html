📖 Overview: Closing Tractate Keritot

Keritot 28a–28b
כְּבָשִׂים קוֹדְמִין אֶת הָעִזִּים בְּכׇל מָקוֹם... מְלַמֵּד שֶׁשְּׁנֵיהֶם שְׁקוּלִין
The scene: The final daf of Keritot completes the discussion of permanently disqualified consecrated animals, drawing three halakhot from a single ruling and testing them against Rabbi Shimon's view that a Paschal offering's disqualified mother can still yield a valid offspring. It then examines exactly when a pair of birds becomes designated as a sin offering and a burnt offering — only at the moment of purchase or slaughter, never in between — testing this rule against the Yom Kippur goats and a case of a poor person's changing fortune. The tractate's final mishna teaches that the Torah treats several paired options — lambs and goats, doves and pigeons, even father and mother — as fundamentally equal, despite their usual order of mention. This launches the Gemara into its closing aggada: four dramatic cries once rang out from the Temple courtyard, climaxing in the story of a High Priest whose ignorance of this very mishna cost him both his hands. This daf concludes tractate Keritot.

🕊️ Three Halakhot from One Ruling

A partnership-consecration case teaches more than expected — then meets a counter-example
1️⃣ Rav Huna (son of Rav Yehoshua): from the case of a disqualified bird-pair whose owner later became poor again, derive that consecrated LIVING animals can be permanently disqualified — even without blemish
↓
2️⃣ Second lesson: even sanctity that inheres only in an item's MONETARY VALUE (not fit for the altar itself) can carry this permanent disqualification
↓
3️⃣ Third lesson: disqualification 'from the very outset' (never having been fit) counts the same as disqualification arising later
↓
4️⃣ Rav Ukva bar Ḥama objects: a baraita about a Paschal offering has Rabbi Shimon ruling that even though a designated FEMALE animal is unfit, her MALE OFFSPRING may still be sacrificed as the Paschal offering — proving Rabbi Shimon holds disqualified living animals are NOT permanently disqualified after all
Resolution: The school of Rabbi Oshaya clarifies: the three-halakhot ruling reflects the Rabbis' view; Rabbi Shimon consistently holds the opposite — also reflected in his separate view that lot-casting for the Yom Kippur goats isn't strictly indispensable.

📛 When Does a Name Take Hold?

Rav Ḥisda's rule on designating one bird as a sin offering, one as a burnt offering
Rav Ḥisda
A bird-pair's designation (one for sin offering, one for burnt offering) can happen ONLY at two moments: when the owner purchases them, or when the priest slaughters them — never in between.
Objection from Yom Kippur goats
A baraita teaches that only the drawing of LOTS makes one goat a sin offering — not verbally naming it. If that's the model, shouldn't verbal naming ALSO only work at 'purchase or slaughter,' just like lots (which happen at neither moment)? That actually FITS Rav Ḥisda's timing restriction after all!
Second objection
But a baraita about a formerly-poor person who became WEALTHY has him saying 'this is for my sin offering, this for my burnt offering' — seemingly NEITHER at purchase NOR slaughter!
Resolution
Rav Sheshet: reinterpret the baraita as describing a declaration made BACK when he was still poor (i.e., truly at the time of designation/purchase) — preserving Rav Ḥisda's rule intact.
Underlying stakes: Precisely when a consecration-declaration 'sticks' determines whether many everyday scenarios of changing designations are valid or not.

⚖️ Lambs and Goats, Doves and Pigeons

The tractate's closing mishna: paired options the Torah treats as fundamentally equal
PairWhy NOT one over the other
Lambs and goatsThough lambs are usually named first, Leviticus 4:28 and 4:32 reverse the order between them — teaching they are equally valid, not that lambs are superior
Doves and pigeonsSame pattern: the order reverses across different verses (Lev. 5:7 vs. 12:6), teaching equal standing
Father and mother (in the mitzva of honor/reverence)Exodus 20:12 names father first, Leviticus 19:3 names mother first — teaching the Torah's OWN valuation treats them equally
Practical exception: father before motherThe Sages nonetheless rule father's honor takes precedence in practice — since both the son AND the mother share an obligation to honor the father
Practical exception: teacher before fatherIf the son's Torah primarily came from his teacher, the teacher's honor takes precedence — since both son and father alike owe honor to that teacher
Method: Wherever the Torah lists two items in reversed order across different verses, that reversal itself is read as a deliberate teaching of equal status.

📢 Four Cries of the Temple Courtyard

A baraita's account of dramatic moments in Temple history
1️⃣ 'Remove Ḥofni and Pineḥas!' — the sons of Eli, who defiled the Sanctuary at Shiloh
↓
2️⃣ 'Open the gates for Yoḥanan ben Nedavai!' — a glutton who ate 40 se'a of doves as dessert, ensuring nothing sacrificial ever went to waste
↓
3️⃣ 'Lift your heads, O gates, for Elishama ben Pikai!' — worthy student of a priestly lineage, welcomed to serve as High Priest
↓
4️⃣ 'Open the gates and expel Yissakhar of Kfar Barkai!' — he wrapped silk over his hands during the Temple service, unwilling to let sacrificial blood touch him directly
Setting up the story: The fourth cry's subject, Yissakhar of Kfar Barkai, becomes the focus of the Gemara's closing narrative about the price of not knowing this daf's own mishna.

✋ The High Priest Who Didn't Know His Mishna

A royal dinner debate exposes a costly gap in Yissakhar's learning
King Yannai
Goat meat is the better food.
The Queen
No — lamb meat is better. Let's ask Yissakhar of Kfar Barkai, the High Priest — he'll know.
Yissakhar
If goat were better, it would be the daily offering — but the daily offering is lamb. (Gestures dismissively with his hand while answering.)
The King
Furious at the disrespectful gesture: sever his right hand! (A bribed official cuts the left instead — discovered, the king orders the right hand cut too.)
Rav Ashi's verdict
Yissakhar never properly learned THIS mishna — that lambs and goats are actually taught as EQUAL, not that lamb is superior. His confident but flawed answer, and his contemptuous manner, cost him everything.
Ravina adds
He hadn't even read the plain verses correctly — both animals are offered interchangeably by choice, with no inherent hierarchy at all.
The tractate's closing words: Rabbi Elazar (Rabbi Ḥanina): 'Torah scholars increase peace in the world' — reading 'your children' (banayikh) as 'your builders' (bonayikh), since it is through faithful, careful study — the very opposite of Yissakhar's careless error — that the world is built up in peace.
