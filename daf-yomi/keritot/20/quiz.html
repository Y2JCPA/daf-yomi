QUESTION 1 OF 5
According to the candle-lighting baraita, what happens if someone intends to extinguish one candle but accidentally extinguishes a different one instead?
A. He is liable, since a candle was extinguished either way
B. He is exempt, since his specific intended act was not accomplished
C. He must extinguish a third candle to atone
D. The ruling depends on the candle's size
Correct: B — The baraita rules he is exempt in this case, since his intent was for a specific candle, and that specific outcome was not achieved — unlike the case where his overall intention (one lit, one extinguished) was fulfilled simultaneously.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
According to Rav Ashi's explanation of the two-sin-offerings dispute over stoking coals, what is the key underlying disagreement?
A. Whether coals can ever be considered fuel
B. Whether an unintended byproduct of an action (davar she'eino mitkavein) creates liability, disputed between Rabbi Yehuda (liable) and Rabbi Shimon (exempt)
C. Whether Shabbat labor requires two witnesses
D. Whether a blacksmith is exempt from all Shabbat laws
Correct: B — Rav Ashi locates the dispute in the debate between Rabbi Yehuda (who holds an unintended result of an action is still liable) and Rabbi Shimon (who holds it is exempt), applied to coals that ignite on their own without intent.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
In the parallel baraita about stoking coals purely for warmth, where the lower coals ignite entirely on their own, what does the dispute between the two versions hinge on?
A. Whether coals can be moved on Shabbat at all
B. Whether one is liable for 'labor not needed for its own sake' (melakha she'eina tzericha legufah)
C. Whether warming oneself is ever permitted on Shabbat
D. Whether the coals were kosher
Correct: B — The two versions of the baraita reflect a dispute over whether performing a labor whose result serves no purpose of the actor's own (melakha she'eina tzericha legufah) creates liability.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
According to the new mishna, which type of blood is exempt from the Torah's karet penalty even though it comes from a properly slaughtered kosher animal?
A. Blood that spurts during the act of slaughter itself
B. Blood of the spleen or heart
C. Blood from bloodletting through which the soul departs
D. Blood from stabbing or maiming
Correct: B — The mishna exempts blood of the spleen, heart, eggs, and grasshoppers, since this blood is not bound up with the departure of the life-force, unlike slaughter blood, stabbing/maiming blood, or blood from lethal bloodletting.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
According to the Gemara's derivation, why is human blood excluded from the Torah's prohibition against consuming blood, despite the broad phrase 'you shall consume no manner of blood'?
A. Because humans cannot become ritually impure at all
B. Because the verse's comparison to 'bird or animal' requires the capacity for BOTH light and severe ritual impurity, and a human body lacks the light 'food' impurity that applies to birds and animals
C. Because human blood was never actually blood
D. Because the prohibition applies only to Temple offerings
Correct: B — The Gemara derives that the prohibition applies only to creatures sharing birds' and animals' defining traits — including the capacity for both light (food-related) and severe (corpse) impurity — and since a human body never carries the light food-impurity, human blood is excluded.
