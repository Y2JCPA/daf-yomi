📖 Overview: Closing the Chapter on Intent, Opening the Laws of Blood

Keritot 20a–20b
כׇּל דָּם לֹא תֹאכְלוּ
The scene: The daf completes its exploration of intent and sequence in unwitting transgressions, testing whether performing two intended acts in reverse order still counts as fulfilling one's intent, using a memorable case of candles lit and extinguished in confusion. It then examines a famous dispute over one who stokes coals on Shabbat — extinguishing the upper coals while inadvertently igniting the lower ones — offering four different Amoraic explanations for why this might generate one or two sin offerings, each rooted in a different underlying tannaitic debate about intent and side-effects. With that, the chapter on uncertain and unintentional transgressions closes, and a new mishna opens: which types of blood, from which sources, carry the Torah's severe penalty of karet, and which are entirely exempt.

🕯️ Reversed Order — Does Intent Still Count?

A baraita about two burning candles tests Rava's theory of reversed sequence
Rava's theory
Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua may disagree specifically about someone who intended to do two things in one order, but unwittingly did them in the REVERSE order.
Baraita
Two candles are burning. He meant to extinguish THIS one but extinguished THAT one instead (or meant to light this one but lit that one) — exempt, since neither intended act was truly accomplished.
Contrast case
But if he meant to light one candle and then extinguish the other, and instead blew a single breath that did both simultaneously — liable, since his overall intention (one lit, one extinguished) was accomplished, even out of strict sequence.
Why state the obvious?
One might think: since he intended lighting FIRST and extinguishing SECOND, but they happened together, his intent wasn't truly fulfilled either way. The baraita teaches: since it didn't happen in reverse order either, the outcome still counts as fulfilling his intent.
Underlying test: The precise sequencing of two intended results — not just their eventual accomplishment — can determine liability.

🔥 Four Ways to Explain 'Liable for Two'

Why does stoking coals (extinguishing the top, igniting the bottom) generate two sin offerings for one sage but not another?

R' Elazar & R' Ḥanina / R' Yoḥanan

  • A blacksmith wants only the top coals removed; igniting the bottom is an unwanted side-effect
  • Hinges on: is an undesired/destructive result of kindling still liable? (מְקַלְקֵל בְּהַבְעָרָה)

Ami bar Avin & R' Ḥananya bar Avin

  • He intended BOTH extinguishing and kindling
  • Hinges on: was kindling singled out in the Torah merely as an ordinary prohibition (Rabbi Yosei), or to divide labors and make each independently liable (Rabbi Natan)?

Rava

  • He intended to kindle FIRST, extinguish SECOND — but did them in reverse
  • Hinges on the same 'reversed order' principle as the candle case

Rav Ashi

  • He intended only to extinguish; the bottom coals ignited entirely on their own, unintended
  • Hinges on: is an unintended byproduct (דָּבָר שֶׁאֵין מִתְכַּוֵּין) liable (Rabbi Yehuda) or exempt (Rabbi Shimon)?
One case, four frameworks: Each Amora locates the SAME two-offering ruling inside a completely different, independently attested tannaitic dispute — showing how many distinct legal principles govern intent on Shabbat.

🪵 Stoking for Warmth — When Self-Igniting Coals Divide the Sages

A parallel baraita on stoking coals purely to get warm, with the bottom coals catching fire unintentionally
🔥 One stokes coals only to warm himself; the coals below ignite entirely on their own, with no intent at all
↓
📜 One version of the baraita: LIABLE
↓
💡 Reasoning: this tanna holds one IS liable for 'labor not needed for its own sake' (מְלָאכָה שֶׁאֵינָהּ צְרִיכָה לְגוּפָהּ) — even an incidental, unwanted result of a permitted action still counts
↓
📜 Another version: EXEMPT
↓
💡 Reasoning: this tanna holds one is NOT liable for labor not needed for its own sake — since the kindling served no purpose of his own, it doesn't count as prohibited labor
Chapter closes here: With this dispute over intent and incidental labor, the chapter on uncertain and unintentional transgressions ("Safek Akhal Ḥelev") concludes.

🩸 Which Blood Carries Karet?

A new mishna opens: the Torah's blood prohibition, precisely sourced
Blood sourceLiability
Blood that spurts during proper slaughter (domesticated, undomesticated, bird, kosher or not)Karet (intentional) / sin offering (unwitting)
Blood from stabbing (neḥira) or maiming (ikur), or from bloodletting that removes the soulKaret / sin offering — the soul departed through it
Blood of the spleen, heart, eggs, or grasshoppersExempt — not the blood that carries the life-force
Blood of exudate (oozing after the initial spurt of slaughter)Exempt, per the first tanna; Rabbi Yehuda holds it IS liable
Underlying principle: Only blood genuinely bound up with the departure of the life-force ('the soul departs through it') carries the Torah's severe penalty — blood that merely accompanies death without being its vehicle does not.

🐦 Excluding Human Blood — the Gemara's Comparison

How the Torah's phrase 'of bird or animal' narrows the scope of the blood prohibition
1️⃣ Leviticus 7:26: 'you shall consume no manner of blood' — read broadly, this could include human blood, egg blood, grasshopper blood, fish blood
↓
2️⃣ The verse narrows it: 'whether it is of bird or of animal' — meaning only creatures sharing birds' and animals' defining features
↓
3️⃣ Those shared features: capacity for BOTH light impurity (as food, if it becomes impure) AND severe impurity (as a corpse); capacity to be forbidden or permitted; and being a type of meat
↓
4️⃣ Human beings are EXCLUDED: though a human corpse carries severe impurity, a living or dead human body never carries the 'light' food-impurity that applies to birds and animals — so human blood falls outside this prohibition
Method: The Gemara isolates a precise set of shared halakhic traits from the verse's own comparative language, rather than reading the prohibition at its broadest possible scope.
