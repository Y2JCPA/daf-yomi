📖 Overview: When the Sin Itself Is Unknown

Keritot 19a–19b
אֲשֶׁר חָטָא בָּהּ – עַד שֶׁיִּוָּדַע לוֹ בַּמֶּה חָטָא
The scene: The daf resolves lingering contradictions from the two-paths impurity case, distinguishing 'partial knowledge' from full knowledge and reconciling apparently conflicting statements from Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish by tracing them to different verses. A new mishna then raises the sharpest form of this problem: someone who certainly sinned, but cannot identify which of two forbidden acts he committed (forbidden fat or notar, his menstruating wife or his sister, Shabbat or Yom Kippur labor). Rabbi Eliezer holds he is liable regardless — either way he sinned — while Rabbi Yehoshua exempts him, since the Torah requires him to know specifically what he did. Rabbi Yosei, and separately Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Shimon Shezuri, each narrow exactly which version of this scenario the two disagree about. The daf closes with the special category of mit'asek — acting without any intent to sin at all — where the Gemara distinguishes cases of forbidden pleasure (still liable) from Shabbat labor (exempt, since it requires purposeful action).

🛤️ Partial Knowledge — Is It Knowledge?

Untangling self-contradictions in Rabbi Yoḥanan's and Reish Lakish's own statements
❓ Someone walked path 1 (forgetting he'd walked it by the time he walked path 2), then entered the Temple — is that 'knowledge' of possible impurity?
↓
⚖️ Dispute: the first tanna holds partial/forgotten knowledge still counts as knowledge; Rabbi Shimon holds it does not
↓
🔄 Separately: why is one liable after purifying and re-entering with NO knowledge at all connecting the two entries? Reish Lakish: this baraita follows Rabbi Yishmael, who never requires 'knowledge at the outset.' Rabbi Yoḥanan: even the Rabbis agree HERE — the impurity verse's doubled language uniquely treats uncertain knowledge as full knowledge
↓
🧩 Reconciling both Sages' apparent self-contradictions: Rabbi Yoḥanan limits his 'uncertain=full knowledge' rule to this specific impurity verse, not the rest of the Torah (which requires the ordinary phrase 'known to him'); Reish Lakish clarifies Rabbi Yishmael himself needs no such requirement at all
Textual key: The impurity verse (Lev. 5:2) uniquely says 'it was concealed FROM him' — even uncertain awareness triggers liability there, unlike the general formula 'known TO him' used elsewhere in the Torah.

🌆 Twilight and the Limits of the Dispute

Rabbi Yosei: the twilight (Shabbat/Yom Kippur) case isn't even disputed
R' Yosei
Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua never disagreed about the twilight case — both agree he's exempt, since I say: part of the labor happened today, part happened tomorrow, so neither day's threshold was actually crossed.
Colleagues
Test this: what if he lifted an object right at twilight and carried it between adjacent domains — fast enough that it couldn't span two days?
R' Yosei
'You've been diligent examining me' — but I still hold firm: even that lifting can be conceptually split between the two days.
Further challenge
But doesn't Rabbi Eliezer elsewhere hold one liable for even a SINGLE added thread completing a weave — suggesting he'd count even a split act as complete?
Rav Yosef's resolution
Rabbi Yosei's version of Rabbi Eliezer's opinion must then read as requiring TWO added threads, not one — preserving the idea that a truly split act still needs its own full measure on each side.
Key move: Rabbi Yosei doesn't deny the mishna's dispute — he relocates it to a different, more genuine case of true doubt.

🍇 One Category or Two?

Rabbi Shimon and Rabbi Shimon Shezuri redraw where the dispute actually lies

Same category (one 'name') — no dispute, liable

  • E.g., picked grapes from one vine on Shabbat, unsure which vine — still clearly 'picking grapes,' a single recognized transgression
  • Both Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua agree he is liable

Two categories ('two names') — genuine dispute

  • E.g., picked fruit from a tree, unsure if it was a grapevine or a fig tree — two distinct categories of prohibited labor
  • Here Rabbi Eliezer says liable (sinned either way); Rabbi Yehoshua says exempt (doesn't know which)
Rabbi Yehuda's addition: Even intending to pick figs and mistakenly picking grapes (a true mit'asek — wrong intent, wrong result) still falls under this same Eliezer/Yehoshua dispute, since he indeed intended SOME picking action.

✋ Acting Without Intent (Mit'asek)

Why does forbidden pleasure create liability while Shabbat labor requires real intent?
Rav Naḥman (Shmuel)
One who acts entirely unawares (mit'asek) — no intent to sin whatsoever — is still liable for forbidden fat or forbidden relations, since he derived pleasure regardless of intent. But for Shabbat labor, he's exempt, since the Torah requires 'purposeful labor' (melekhet maḥshevet).
Rava's challenge
But the mishna about circumcising the wrong baby (meant one, circumcised another) treats it as debated between Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua — not a flat exemption!
Rav Naḥman's answer
That case involves inflicting a wound — normally exempt without constructive intent, but a wound is always liable regardless, so it's a special exception, not a real counterexample.
Rav Yehuda's challenge
What about intending to pick figs but picking grapes instead — surely THAT'S a Shabbat mit'asek case, yet it's disputed, not flatly exempt!
Shmuel's resolution
That case is different: he first intended grapes, then forgot and thought 'figs,' but his hand went to the grapes anyway — his ORIGINAL intent was actually fulfilled. Rabbi Eliezer counts that original intent as sufficient; Rabbi Yehoshua requires his final, conscious intent to match. A genuine mit'asek (zero relevant intent at any point) remains exempt for both.
Bottom line: 'Intent' in halakha isn't binary — the Sages distinguish original intent, current intent, and pure accident, each triggering different liability rules.
