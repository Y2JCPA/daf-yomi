📖 Overview: Rabbi Akiva's Questions in the Meat Market

Keritot 15a–15b
שָׁאַלְתִּי אֶת רַבָּן גַּמְלִיאֵל וְאֶת רַבִּי יְהוֹשֻׁעַ בָּאִיטְלֵיס שֶׁל עֵימָאוּם
The scene: The daf opens by finishing the discussion of slaughtering a mother animal and its offspring, testing whether 'differentiated names' or 'differentiated bodies' drives multiple liability. It then shifts to one of Shas's most memorable scenes: Rabbi Akiva recalls asking Rabban Gamliel and Rabbi Yehoshua three difficult questions while they shopped for an animal at the meat market of Emmaus, for the wedding feast of Rabban Gamliel's son. None of the three had a received tradition on any question, but each was answered, or challenged, through an a fortiori inference: whether a sister who is simultaneously father's-sister and mother's-sister generates one offering or three; whether an animal's dangling limb transmits impurity like a severed one; and whether slaughtering five offerings outside the Temple in one lapse of awareness creates one sin offering or five. The daf closes with a detailed baraita sorting out exactly how pots, lapses of awareness, and separate animals combine (or don't) to multiply liability.

🐐 Animal and Offspring — Two Theories Behind One Ruling

Sumakhos says two sets of lashes for slaughtering a mother and its offspring twice — but why?
1️⃣ Case: one slaughters an animal, then its granddaughter, then finally its daughter — the third slaughter completes TWO 'mother and offspring' violations at once
↓
2️⃣ Sumakhos (in R' Meir's name): liable to eighty lashes — two full sets
↓
3️⃣ Rava's theory: this parallels Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Nuri's mother-in-law ruling — liability multiplies wherever the Torah gives the relationships differentiated names, even without differentiated bodies
↓
4️⃣ Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak's competing theory: it's really about differentiated bodies — three separate animals were slaughtered, unlike the single woman in the mother-in-law case (where Rabbi Yoḥanan b. Nuri's logic wouldn't even apply, since Sumakhos might rest on the verse's 'lewdness' passage treating it as one violation)
Open question: The two theories predict different outcomes in edge cases — whether it's the names or the bodies doing the real work of multiplying liability.

🦵 The Dangling Limb — A Passover Surgery Story

Rabban Gamliel and Rabbi Yehoshua answer from a real medical practice in Jerusalem
R' Akiva
What is the status of an animal's dangling limb — does it transmit impurity?
Teachers
We have no tradition on animals — but we know about people: a dangling human limb is pure. Here's how we know.
The account
People afflicted with boils in Jerusalem would visit a doctor on Passover eve, who would cut the diseased limb down to a hairbreadth of flesh, then impale it on a thorn so the patient could pull it fully off himself — without either doctor or patient becoming impure by contact with a severed limb.
Conclusion
Both the patient and the doctor then brought their Paschal offerings in purity — proof the dangling limb, while still attached, imparted no impurity.
R' Akiva's inference
A fortiori: if a human limb (whose severed-limb impurity is severe) causes no impurity while dangling, surely an animal's limb (whose severed-limb impurity is more lenient) causes none either.
Gemara's footnote: The rule works only when the limb is torn off with one forceful pull — gradual separation (as with squeezed-out liquid) may render it 'detached' earlier.

🔪 Slaughtering Five Offerings Outside — Kal Vaḥomer and Its Rebuttal

The most contested of Rabbi Akiva's three questions
❓ Slaughtering 5 offerings outside the Temple in one lapse: 1 sin offering, or 5?
↓
💡 Rabbi Yehoshua's a fortiori: one who eats meat from a SINGLE offering cooked in five separate pots owes five guilt offerings for misuse (me'ila) — surely slaughtering five separate ANIMALS is at least as severe!
↓
🛑 Rabbi Akiva's rebuttal: me'ila carries unique stringencies — it treats 'feeding another' like eating, 'benefiting another' like personal benefit, and even lets separate small benefits over time combine. None of these apply to ordinary sin offerings, so the inference doesn't transfer
↓
🔄 Rabbi Shimon later clarifies: the real question Rabbi Akiva asked wasn't about slaughtering at all — it was about eating notar (leftover offering meat) from five separate offerings, a closer parallel to Rabbi Yehoshua's five-pots case
Unresolved: The Gemara asks whether Rabbi Yehoshua ever accepted Rabbi Akiva's rebuttal — and finds the answer only by checking how later baraitot actually ruled.

📊 How Many Offerings? The Resolving Baraita

A later baraita finally settles how pots, lapses, and separate animals combine

One offering, one lapse

  • 5 pieces of notar from ONE offering, cooked in 5 pots, eaten in ONE lapse → just 1 sin offering
  • Same offering, but eaten across 5 separate lapses → 5 sin offerings, since awareness divides liability

Five offerings, one lapse

  • 5 pieces of notar from 5 SEPARATE offerings, eaten in one lapse → 5 sin offerings (differentiated bodies), per the first tanna
  • Rabbi Yosei b. Rabbi Yehuda dissents: even this case yields only 1 sin offering, since it's still one repeated transgression in one lapse
Contrast with me'ila: Eating from 5 pots of a single offering BEFORE its blood is sprinkled (still fully consecrated) yields 5 separate guilt offerings for misuse, even in one lapse — confirming misuse really does follow different, more stringent rules than ordinary sin offerings.
