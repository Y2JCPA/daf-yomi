📖 Overview: Forbidden Fat and the Grammar of Multiple Liability

Keritot 4a–4b
כׇּל חֵלֶב שׁוֹר וְכֶשֶׂב וָעֵז לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ
The scene: The daf closes out the discussion of what counts as an 'action' for liability (distinguishing blasphemy and conspiring witnesses, whose transgressions are of the heart or of sight rather than of the hand), then turns to the prohibition against eating forbidden fat (chelev). A baraita records a dispute between Rabbi Yishmael, who holds one is flogged separately for the fat of an ox, a sheep, and a goat, and the Rabbis, who hold one set of lashes suffices for all three eaten together. The Gemara works through several proposed readings of why the verse names all three animals, using the hermeneutical principle that 'a matter is derived from its context' to link the fat prohibition to the sacrificial context. It then examines when eating forbidden fat from 'two categories' in one lapse of awareness yields two sin offerings, and closes with Rava's careful count of how many separate prohibitions and karet penalties the Torah attaches to consuming blood.

🤐 Speech vs. Action

Why does the twisted lip of a blasphemer not count as an action, while ordinary speech does?
Challenge
If the twisting of one's lips generally counts as an action, shouldn't it count for the blasphemer too, making him liable to a sin offering?
Rava
Blasphemy is different — it is essentially a matter of the heart. The transgression is the sentiment expressed, not the mechanics of speech.
R' Zeira
Objects from conspiring witnesses (עֵדִים זוֹמְמִין): a baraita exempts them from a sin offering as having 'no action' — yet the verse says 'at the mouth of two witnesses' (Deut. 17:6), meaning they transgress by speaking!
Rava
Conspiring witnesses are also different — their transgression is essentially a matter of sight (what they falsely claimed to have seen), not the act of speaking it.
Pattern: Both exemptions share a logic: liability for a sin offering requires a genuine physical act, and the Gemara looks past the surface mechanics (lips moving) to what the transgression actually consists of.

🐑 One Animal or Three? Lashes for Forbidden Fat

Leviticus 7:23: 'You shall eat no fat of ox, or sheep, or goat' — why name all three?

Rabbi Yishmael

  • One who eats the fat of all three in one lapse is flogged three times
  • The extra names 'ox, sheep, goat' are superfluous unless meant to separate the prohibitions
  • Yet concedes (Rabbi Ḥanina): for a sin offering, only one is brought — this prohibition is not like forbidden relations, where each verse is fully separate

The Rabbis

  • One who eats the fat of all three in one lapse is flogged only once
  • The names are needed to exclude the fat of undomesticated animals and birds, learned from context ('a matter is derived from its context') linking chelev to sacrificial animals
  • Multiple lashes are ultimately derived instead from the parallel karet verse (Lev. 7:25), which does separate the sin offering
The real fault-line: Rabbi Yishmael derives the halakha of a standard prohibition from a karet verse; the Rabbis do not — they only derive one prohibition's halakha from another.

🔄 Why All Three Animals Must Be Written

Rabbi Ḥanina's proof, applied both to lashes and to the sacrificial 'portions burned on the altar' (eimurim)
1️⃣ Could the Torah write only 'ox' and derive sheep and goat? No — one could object: ox is unique in having more wine libations
↓
2️⃣ Could it write only 'sheep'? No — sheep is unique in its tail being burned on the altar
↓
3️⃣ Could it write only 'goat'? No — goat is unique in its greater role in idolatry sin offerings
↓
4️⃣ Could it write any two and derive the third? No — each pair still has a unique feature the third lacks (fitness for the Paschal offering, idolatry offerings, or altar libations)
Conclusion: Since no animal's law can be derived from the others, the Torah had to state all three explicitly — and Rabbi Yishmael reads that explicit repetition as teaching separate liability.

🍲 Two Offerings for Two Categories in One Lapse

A baraita: eating forbidden fat from 'two categories' (שְׁתֵּי שֵׁמוֹת) in one lapse still brings two sin offerings
❓ Rami bar Ḥama to Rav Ḥisda: separate lapses of awareness normally divide liability — but how can 'two categories in ONE lapse' still yield two offerings?
↓
💡 Rav Ḥisda: he ate chelev that was also notar (leftover offering meat) — liable for both chelev and notar
↓
⚠️ Rami bar Ḥama objects: then he should also be liable for eating sacrificial food (a guilt offering for me'ila)!
↓
🔁 Rav Sheshet retries: he ate chelev of a consecrated animal — per Rabbi Yehuda's view of two chelev prohibitions. But Rabbi Yehuda himself concedes only one sin offering applies there!
↓
✅ Reish Lakish (name of bar Ta'utni): he ate the two fats from two separate dishes — per Rabbi Yehoshua, different dishes divide liability even within one lapse
Resolution: Only the 'two dishes' answer survives scrutiny — a purely technical, not substantive, division of the act of eating.

🩸 Counting Blood's Prohibitions

Rava's tallies of how many separate verses the Torah devotes to blood
CountWhat each covers
3 mentions of karetBlood of non-sacred animals (chullin); blood of sacrificial animals (kodashim); blood of covering (dam kisui) — revised from an initial version naming 'blood of exudate'
5 total prohibitionsBlood of chullin; blood of kodashim; blood of covering; blood remaining in the limbs (dam eivarim); blood of exudate (dam hatamtzit)
Rabbi Yehuda's additionHolds blood of exudate also carries karet, since 'any manner of blood' (Lev. 17:10) includes it explicitly
The chelev/blood analogyJust as forbidden fat is discrete from meat (does not combine to an olive-bulk with permitted meat), so blood is discrete from meat — except creeping animals, whose blood does combine with their flesh
Method: Rava treats each distinct verse as a distinct halakha to be accounted for — none is superfluous, each teaches its own scope of the prohibition.
