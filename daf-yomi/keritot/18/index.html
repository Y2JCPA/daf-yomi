📖 Overview: The Architecture of Doubt

Keritot 18a–18b
יְדִיעוֹת סָפֵק מְחַלְּקוֹת לְחַטָּאוֹת
The scene: The daf continues probing exactly what triggers a provisional guilt offering, testing Rav Naḥman's theory that an 'established prohibition' is required against Rabbi Zeira's theory that the doubt must be resolvable, using a striking case where a gentile eats one of two pieces before a Jew eats the other. A detailed baraita then maps out how the order of events — who ate which piece, and whether each act was intentional or unwitting — changes the outcome entirely. The Gemara explains why Rav's foundational case specifically involves eating (tied to Bava ben Buta's daily voluntary offering), before turning to a major dispute: does gaining knowledge of mere uncertainty, before a second unwitting act, divide the two acts the way definite knowledge does? This question is tested against the mechanics of Yom Kippur's atonement, misuse of consecrated property that accumulates over separate days, and a classic case of walking down two paths, one pure and one impure by a hidden corpse.

🥩 Established Prohibition or Resolvable Doubt?

Two theories for Rav's ruling are tested with a clever gentile/Jew scenario
Rav Naḥman
One is liable for a doubtful piece only when the prohibition was genuinely 'established' — i.e., there was definitely a forbidden piece present when he ate.
Rabbi Zeira
One is liable only when the doubt is resolvable — an expert could later examine the remaining piece to determine what was eaten.
The test case
Two pieces present, one forbidden, one permitted. A gentile eats the FIRST piece; then a Jew eats the SECOND, unaware which is which.
Three verdicts
Rava: exempt (only one piece remained when the Jew ate — no 'mitzvot,' plural). Rabbi Zeira: exempt (nothing left to examine). Rav Naḥman: liable — a prohibition was definitely established at some point, even though it's now gone.
Why the test matters: Three seemingly similar theories yield three different verdicts in this one case — proving they are not just restatements of each other.

🔀 Order Matters — Gentile, Dog, or Raven

A baraita's precise rules on sequencing determine liability
ScenarioRuling
Jew eats piece 1, then gentile/dog/raven eats piece 2Jew is liable — two pieces were present when HE ate
Gentile eats piece 1, then Jew eats piece 2Jew is exempt (Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi disagrees: liable) — only one piece remained
Jew eats piece 1 unwittingly, piece 2 intentionallyLiable — for the first, unwitting act
Jew eats piece 1 intentionally, piece 2 unwittinglyExempt (Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi: liable) — only one piece remained when he unwittingly ate
Two different Jews each unwittingly eat one pieceBOTH liable — the second must bring an offering too, purely as a precaution against wrongly convicting the first
The precaution logic: If the second Jew were declared exempt, that would imply the first Jew definitely ate the forbidden piece — wrongly obligating him in a sin offering. So per Rabbi Eliezer's rule allowing voluntary offerings, the second Jew brings a stipulated offering to prevent this error.

🙋 Bava ben Buta's Daily Offering

Why does Rav's foundational case specifically involve eating an uncertain piece?
מִתְנַדֵּב אָדָם אָשָׁם תָּלוּי בְּכׇל יוֹם
The explanation: If Rabbi Eliezer holds that a person may volunteer a provisional guilt offering every single day (with no actual uncertainty required at all), why does Rav frame his ruling around someone who actually ate a doubtful piece? Rav Ashi explains: Rav is describing the position specifically as held by Bava ben Buta, who was known to bring a voluntary guilt offering daily — except the day after Yom Kippur, since that day's atonement already covers all uncertain sins. Bava ben Buta once wished he could bring one even then, but was told to wait until he entered a genuine situation of potential uncertainty. Rav's case supplies exactly that kind of real, if unresolved, uncertainty.

⚖️ Does Uncertain Knowledge Divide Like Definite Knowledge?

A four-way debate over whether learning of a mere possibility divides liability
Rabbi Zeira / Reish Lakish
Yes — Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's baraita means that learning of the DOUBT before a second act divides them into separate sin-offering liabilities (if later both turn out to be forbidden), just as definite knowledge would.
Rava / Rabbi Yoḥanan
No — mere uncertainty never divides sin-offering liability. The baraita only means the PARALLEL offering (provisional guilt offering) follows the same multiplication pattern as sin offerings would, not that uncertainty itself divides anything.
Test case: Yom Kippur
If uncertainty divides liability, does Yom Kippur (which atones like a provisional guilt offering) split an olive-bulk eaten before Yom Kippur from one eaten after, into two sin offerings once both are confirmed forbidden?
Resolution
Multiple objections from mishnayot (Yoma's 'one sin offering' for eating and drinking on Yom Kippur) show that even across a natural time-gap, liability does NOT split this way for most cases — supporting Rava/Rabbi Yoḥanan's general position, with each side's statements carefully reconciled to different tanna'im.
Unresolved tension: The Gemara flags a real difficulty: Rabbi Yoḥanan's formulation (asham depends on chatat) fits his own view naturally, but Reish Lakish's parallel formulation seems backward for his — the Gemara leaves this 'difficult' (קַשְׁיָא).

🛤️ Two Paths, One Doubt

Testing the theories against misuse of consecrated property and a classic impurity case
💰 Misuse (me'ila) accumulates across separate days into one peruta's worth — even across a Yom Kippur in between — because Yom Kippur atones for ritual sin, not for the MONETARY restitution misuse requires
↓
🛤️ Classic case: two paths, one impure (hidden corpse), one pure — unknown which is which
↓
🚶 Walk path 1, don't enter Temple yet; walk path 2, THEN enter → liable (definitely impure by then, from one path or the other)
↓
🚶 Walk path 1, enter Temple, walk path 2, enter again → liable for the second entry (certainly impure at least once)
↓
🚶 Walk path 1, enter, THEN undergo purification (sprinkling + immersion), THEN walk path 2, enter again → still liable, since he was certainly impure on one of the two entries
The deeper test: These impurity-path cases become the Gemara's next proving ground for whether purification acts (like the sprinklings) function as 'dividing knowledge' the same way information about a doubt does.
