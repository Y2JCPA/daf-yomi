📖 Overview: Closing the Maidservant Chapter, Opening Testimony

Keritot 11a–11b
בִּזְמַן שֶׁהָאִשָּׁה לוֹקָה – הָאִישׁ מֵבִיא קׇרְבָּן, אֵין הָאִשָּׁה לוֹקָה – אֵין הָאִישׁ מֵבִיא קׇרְבָּן
The scene: The daf opens by pinning down exactly who qualifies as the 'espoused maidservant' of the mishna — a dispute stretching across several tanna'im about whether she must be half-slave, half-free, or simply a full maidservant betrothed to a Hebrew slave. The Gemara then derives, from the order of a single verse, that the man's liability to a guilt offering is entirely contingent on whether the woman herself is flogged, and works through a new mishna's list of exemptions (minor, sleeping, unwitting) that apply differently in this case than in every other forbidden relation. A confusing baraita about typical and atypical intercourse gets carefully untangled by Rav Sheshet. The chapter then closes, and a fresh mishna opens tractate Keritot's discussion of testimony: how witnesses' statements — consistent, split, or contradicted — determine whether a sin offering or a provisional guilt offering is owed.

🤷 Who Is the 'Espoused Maidservant'?

The mishna's key term draws several competing readings
OpinionDefinition
Rabbi YehudaHalf-maidservant, half-free woman — owned by two masters, one of whom freed his share; 'redeemed and not redeemed' (Lev. 19:20) is read literally
Rabbi YishmaelA full-fledged Canaanite maidservant; the doubled phrase is just idiomatic Torah speech ('the Torah speaks in the language of people')
Rabbi Eliezer ben Ya'akov / Rabbi AkivaAll other forbidden relations are fully enumerated; the maidservant case is the sole 'exception' — specifically a half-slave betrothed to a Hebrew slave
AḥerimA Canaanite maidservant betrothed to a Canaanite slave — derived from 'they shall not be put to death because she was not free'
Supporting text: Rav Ḥisda cites Ezra 10:18–19, where priests married 'foreign women' and brought guilt offerings, as proof that guilt offerings apply specifically to relations with an espoused maidservant.

⚖️ Her Flogging, His Offering — Linked by One Verse

The Gemara derives this dependency from the verse's word order
1️⃣ 'There shall be an inspection (בִּקּוֹרֶת תִּהְיֶה)' — the feminine form תִּהְיֶה teaches she alone is flogged, not him
↓
2️⃣ The verse places 'there shall be an inspection' before 'he shall bring his guilt offering' — teaching that his liability depends on hers: if she is flogged, he brings an offering; if not, he does not
↓
3️⃣ Could she still be flogged even when he brings no offering? No — 'he shall bring his guilt offering' specifies only him, never her
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Yitzḥak adds a further limit: he is liable only for a non-virgin maidservant (נֶחֱרֶפֶת, 'designated' — a term implying she has already 'changed' status)
Result: Unlike ordinary forbidden relations, where man and woman face parallel liability, the maidservant case makes the man's offering wholly derivative of her own punishment.

😴 Exemptions: Forbidden Relations vs. the Maidservant Case

A new mishna: the same exemption categories apply very differently here

All other forbidden relations

  • Minor exempt; adult partner still liable
  • Sleeping partner exempt; awake partner still liable
  • Unwitting brings a sin offering; intentional brings karet — two distinct tracks

The maidservant case

  • If either partner is a minor, both are exempt — their liabilities are 'juxtaposed to each other' in the verse
  • If either partner is asleep, both are exempt — same juxtaposition logic
  • Liability is entirely linked: no independent track for the man
Rav Yehuda's key move: He reinterprets the mishna's opening clauses: 'minor exempt' and 'sleeping exempt' in the general case implicitly means 'the adult/awake partner is liable' — a contrast the maidservant clause then overturns entirely.

🔀 Rav Sheshet Untangles a Confusing Baraita

A baraita seems to equate opposite categories — is it corrupted?
Baraita
'They equated completion with initial stage, intentional with unintentional, typical with atypical, awake with asleep' — but this seems backward for the maidservant case!
Student
Should I strike this baraita as corrupted? None of these equations make sense for either the maidservant case or ordinary forbidden relations.
Rav Sheshet
No — keep it. It means: atypical completion with a maidservant (exempt) is equated to typical initial-stage contact (also exempt) — both fail the verse's requirement of 'lies carnally' (שִׁכְבַת זֶרַע, seed-bearing intercourse).
Rav Sheshet
Likewise: intentional-but-atypical is equated to unintentional; awake-but-atypical is equated to asleep — all four pairs share the same underlying exemption, atypical intercourse.
Net effect: Every atypical-intercourse scenario with a maidservant collapses into the same exemption category as the most obviously exempt scenarios among ordinary forbidden relations.

🧀 Witnesses and the Sin Offering for Forbidden Fat

The new mishna opening the next major topic: testimony and liability
👥 Two witnesses say 'you ate forbidden fat' and he stays silent → liable to a sin offering
↓
⚖️ One witness says 'he ate,' another says 'he did not' (or two women split) → uncertain → provisional guilt offering
↓
🙅 One witness says 'he ate,' and he himself denies it → exempt — his denial overrides a lone witness
↓
🤔 Two witnesses say 'he ate,' and he denies it → Rabbi Meir: liable (a fortiori from the graver penalty of death); the Rabbis: exempt, since he might have meant 'I did it intentionally'
↓
🍽️ Separate stringency: eating different forbidden foods (fat, blood, piggul, notar) in one lapse → liable for each; eating the same food twice in one lapse → only one offering
Torah source for liability despite testimony: 'Or if his sin is known to him' (Lev. 4:28) teaches that being informed by others — not only self-discovery — creates liability for a sin offering, provided he does not contradict them.
