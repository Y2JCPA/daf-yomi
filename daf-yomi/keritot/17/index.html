📖 Overview: Closing the Shabbatot Debate, Opening Uncertainty

Keritot 17a–17b
יֵשׁ אֵם לַמָּסוֹרֶת... יֵשׁ אֵם לַמִּקְרָא
The scene: The daf completes the intricate analysis of Rabbi Akiva's Shabbat dilemma, testing the competing reconstructions of Rava and Rav Ḥisda against small but decisive linguistic details in the mishna's wording — singular or plural 'menstruating woman,' singular or plural 'minor girls.' A parallel baraita even records a wholly different version of Rabbi Akiva's question, framed around repeated intercourse with a menstruating wife rather than Shabbat labor. The chapter then closes, and a new mishna opens the Torah's treatment of the provisional guilt offering (asham talui) — brought whenever genuine uncertainty exists about whether a sin was committed at all. The Gemara spends the rest of the daf on a foundational dispute between Rav Asi and Ḥiyya bar Rav over whether a single doubtful piece of fat, or only a doubt between two pieces, triggers this offering — a dispute rooted in how a single Hebrew word in the Torah is spelled versus how it is read aloud.

🧵 One Body or Many? Testing the Shabbatot Theories

The Gemara hunts for proof in a single word's grammatical number
🔍 Rav Ḥisda's theory requires Rabbi Eliezer's menstrual-woman proof to use the SINGULAR ('menstruating woman') — since his dilemma was about intervening days, not separate bodies
↓
🔍 Rava's theory requires the PLURAL ('menstruating women') — since his dilemma was about whether Shabbatot count as separate bodies
↓
📜 Textual tradition is split: Shmuel and Rav Adda bar Ahava transmit the singular; Rav Natan bar Oshaya transmits the plural — the manuscript evidence itself doesn't settle it
↓
🧩 Rava must still explain the singular version: he posits a case of repeated intercourse with immersions IN BETWEEN, so the immersions function like 'intervening days' even under his own theory
↓
✅ The 'minor girls' proof, by contrast, appears in the plural regardless — fitting Rava's theory naturally (separate girls = separate bodies), while Rav Ḥisda must read it merely as 'minor girls in general,' not one man with several
Net result: Neither theory is fully disproven — each requires its own careful reading of the mishna's grammatical choices.

🔄 A Different Question Entirely?

Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar: Rabbi Akiva never asked about Shabbat at all
R' Shimon b. Elazar
That's not what Rabbi Akiva asked Rabbi Eliezer! He really asked: if one unwittingly has relations with his menstruating wife, then again, in one lapse of awareness — one offering or many?
R' Eliezer
Liable for each! A fortiori from SHABBAT: it has only ONE warning (he is warned about it, but it isn't warned about him), yet each labor is separately liable. A menstruating woman has TWO mutual warnings — surely each act is liable too!
R' Akiva
Not comparable — Shabbat has MANY categories of labor generating multiple sin offerings; a menstruating woman has only ONE type of forbidden act.
R' Eliezer
Then minor girls prove it — no multiplicity of acts there either, yet each girl is separately liable!
R' Akiva
Not comparable — minors are separate BODIES. A repeatedly-violated single wife is not.
R' Eliezer
Then bestiality proves it — no separate bodies there, yet still liable per act!
R' Akiva
I treat the animal case as just as uncertain as the menstruating-wife case itself.
Striking reversal: In this version, the ENTIRE debate about Shabbat is repurposed — the exact same a fortiori chain, but now proving something about repeated forbidden intercourse instead.

❓ New Mishna: The Provisional Guilt Offering

Chapter three opens: what triggers an asham talui (offering for uncertain sin)?
ScenarioRuling
Uncertain whether he ate forbidden fat at allBrings a provisional guilt offering
Definitely ate, but uncertain if the piece met the minimum measureBrings a provisional guilt offering
Permitted and forbidden fat both present; ate one, doesn't know whichBrings a provisional guilt offering
Wife and sister both present; had relations with one, doesn't know whichBrings a provisional guilt offering
Confused Shabbat and a weekday; performed labor on one, doesn't know whichBrings a provisional guilt offering
Parallel structure to sin offerings: Just as eating two pieces of forbidden fat in one lapse yields one sin offering (but eating fat, blood, piggul, and notar together yields separate offerings for each), the same multiplication rules govern the provisional guilt offering for uncertain violations.

📖 Written or Read? Mesorah vs. Mikra

Does the provisional guilt offering require doubt about ONE item or between TWO?
Verse
Leviticus 4:22 is spelled מִצְוַת (mitzvat, singular) but traditionally READ as מִצְווֹת (mitzvot, plural).
Rav Asi
The written spelling governs (יֵשׁ אֵם לַמָּסוֹרֶת) — singular. So even doubt about a SINGLE piece (is it forbidden fat or permitted fat?) triggers the offering.
Ḥiyya bar Rav
The spoken reading governs (יֵשׁ אֵם לַמִּקְרָא) — plural. So the offering requires doubt between TWO distinct pieces, one definitely forbidden and one definitely permitted.
Rav's parallel ruling
Confirms Ḥiyya's approach: two pieces before him, ate one, unknown which → liable. But ONE piece of inherently uncertain status (perhaps forbidden, perhaps not) → exempt.
Why it matters: This single grammatical question determines the entire boundary of when 'uncertainty' is serious enough to require an offering at all.

🧪 Two Theories for Rav's Ruling — And Where They Diverge

Why does a single doubtful piece differ from a doubt between two known pieces?

Rava's theory: the verse requires 'two'

  • Grounded purely in reading מִצְווֹת as plural
  • A single piece never qualifies, no matter what
  • Challenged by Rabbi Eliezer's koy case (single uncertain animal) — resolved as Rabbi Eliezer following the OTHER reading (mesorah, singular)

Rabbi Zeira's theory: the doubt must be resolvable

  • Two known pieces let an expert LATER examine the leftover piece and determine what was eaten
  • A single ambiguous piece can never be resolved this way — nothing remains to test
  • Diverges from Rava's theory in an edge case: one full olive-bulk piece plus a mere half-olive-bulk piece — Rava exempts (not 'two' qualifying pieces), Rabbi Zeira still obligates (the remainder can still be examined)
Rabbi Eliezer's koy exception: Both theories must explain why Rabbi Eliezer obligates an offering even for a single-item doubt (the mixed-species koy) — resolved as his own minority position, following the written mesorah over the two-item requirement.
