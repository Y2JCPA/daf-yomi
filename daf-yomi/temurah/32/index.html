📖 Overview: What Two Sanctities Can and Cannot Do to Each Other

Temurah 32a–32b
אַךְ כׇּל חֵרֶם אֲשֶׁר יַחֲרִם אִישׁ לַה׳ מִכׇּל אֲשֶׁר לוֹ… לֹא יִמָּכֵר וְלֹא יִגָּאֵל, כׇּל חֵרֶם קֹדֶשׁ קׇדָשִׁים הוּא לַה׳
The scene: Where the previous mishna listed the differences between altar consecration and Temple-maintenance consecration, this one lists what they share: neither may be shifted from one sanctity to another, though one may pledge an animal's value. Rav Huna builds a rule on Leviticus 27:28 — that an altar animal cannot be linked to a priestly dedication — and the Gemara dismantles it with two baraitot, forcing Ulla to reread the verse entirely. The daf closes on a dispute between Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish over which consecrated animals were ever included in 'standing and valuation', and therefore which may still be redeemed once they have died.

🙅 Rav Huna's Rule and Its Conclusive Refutation

Can an animal consecrated for the altar also be pledged as a dedication to the priests?
1️⃣ Rav Huna: he has done nothing. Leviticus 27:28 — 'every dedicated item is most holy' — implies that only what is already most holy can be so dedicated
↓
2️⃣ Objection from a baraita: it bars linking Temple-maintenance items and priestly dedications to other sanctities — and pointedly omits the altar case, implying that one does take effect
↓
3️⃣ Rav Huna's defense: the tanna listed only cases where both other sanctities fail; the altar case is mixed, so it was left out
↓
4️⃣ Second objection: a baraita says outright that one consecrates an altar animal by value and dedicates it to priests; another says that if he did so, what he did is done
↓
5️⃣ ✅ The refutation of Rav Huna is conclusive (תְּיוּבְתָּא) — an altar animal may be linked to a priestly dedication
Why the attempt to save him failed: The mishna's own 'one may consecrate their value and one may dedicate them' was first reread as two ways of saying 'for Temple maintenance' — but the baraita spells out that the second phrase means a dedication to priests.

📜 So What Does 'Every Dedicated Item' Teach?

Ulla salvages the verse for a different purpose
Ulla
The verse is not read as Rav Huna read it. 'Every dedicated item is most holy' teaches that one is subject to the laws of me'ilah for a dedication to the priests.
Gemara
Why is a verse needed? The words 'most holy' are right there, and me'ilah applies to all sanctified items!
Answer
By your reasoning, consider Rabbi Yannai: me'ilah is written explicitly only regarding a burnt offering — 'and sin through error in the holy items of the Lord' (Lev. 5:15).
Answer
And Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi needed 'all the fat is the Lord's' (Lev. 3:16) to include offerings of lesser sanctity — and a fortiori the most sacred ones.
Conclusion
So even where 'most holy' is written, a separate verse is still required to bring an item under me'ilah. The same holds for priestly dedications.
Status of the rule: The requirement to pay the value of an altar animal linked to Temple maintenance is rabbinic; Ulla's verse is only supporting ('asmakhta'). By Torah law nothing more than the treasurers' presence is involved.

⏳ A Burnt Offering Consecrated for Temple Maintenance

Ulla: only 'the delay of the treasurers' stands in its way
🐑 One consecrates his burnt offering for Temple maintenance
↓
📖 By Torah law: a burnt offering cannot be consecrated by value at all — the treasurer is now the owner, and an owner must stand over his offering. That is the whole obstacle.
↓
⚖️ By rabbinic law: a baraita forbids slaughtering it until it is redeemed
↓
Proof it is only rabbinic
The same baraita adds: if he transgressed and slaughtered it first, what he did is done — impossible if redemption were required by Torah law
Proof it is not merely rabbinic
The baraita's end: one who benefits misuses on two counts — altar and maintenance — which a rabbinic consecration would not generate
Left standing: The two clauses of the baraita pull in opposite directions, which is precisely why the Gemara must distinguish Torah law from rabbinic enactment here.

⚰️ If It Died Before It Was Redeemed

'Standing and valuation' (Lev. 27:11–12) decides whether redemption is still possible

Rabbi Yoḥanan on the Rabbis

  • Both altar and maintenance animals were included in standing and valuation
  • Anything that cannot be stood and valued cannot be redeemed
  • So if either died — it is buried, not redeemed
  • This is why R' Shimon had to specify 'for Temple maintenance'

Reish Lakish on the Rabbis

  • Only maintenance animals were included — the verse speaks of them
  • Maintenance animal that died → buried
  • Altar animal that died → redeemed
  • Difficulty: then R' Shimon's specification is redundant

Both agree on R' Shimon

  • Maintenance animals were not in standing and valuation → if they died, redeemed
  • Altar animals were → if they died, not redeemed
  • And all agree: an animal blemished from the outset, consecrated for the altar, is redeemable
Reish Lakish's reply: Rabbi Shimon did not know which case the first tanna meant, so he addressed it conditionally — 'if you are speaking of altar animals, then…'. A baraita, however, is taught in line with Rabbi Yoḥanan.

🔍 Reading Leviticus 27:11–12 — Blemished or Non-Kosher?

The baraita that supports Rabbi Yoḥanan works out whom the verse addresses
Proposed subject of the verseConsequenceVerdict
Blemished animalsThey are redeemed through standing and valuation✅ Accepted
A non-kosher animal consecrated for maintenanceIt would be redeemed through standing and valuation🚫 Rejected
Why rejectedLev. 27:27 already states separately: 'if it be of a non-kosher animal, he shall redeem it according to your valuation'📜 Redundant
Result: 'Then he shall set the animal before the priest, and the priest shall value it' governs blemished consecrated animals — which is the foundation of the whole standing-and-valuation dispute above.
