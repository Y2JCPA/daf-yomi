5 questions · Self-graded · Test your learning!
QUESTION 1 OF 5
According to the new mishna, what happens if even ONE disqualified animal (e.g., one used in bestiality) becomes mixed into a large group of otherwise fit sacrificial animals?
A. The disqualified animal is simply removed and the rest remain fit
B. The entire mixture, in any quantity, becomes prohibited from the altar — it is never nullified by a majority
C. Nothing changes; disqualification doesn't transfer through mixtures
D. Only half the group is disqualified
Correct: B — The mishna's central ruling is that these categories of disqualified animals contaminate an entire mixture regardless of ratio, unlike typical halakhic mixtures where a minority can be nullified by a majority.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
According to Rav Shimi bar Ashi, what key distinction separates this mishna from the parallel one in tractate Zevaḥim about mixtures being forbidden?
A. There is no real distinction; they are identical
B. This mishna addresses animals that became intermingled while still NON-SACRED (before consecration); the Zevaḥim mishna addresses animals already consecrated when mixed
C. This mishna only applies to birds
D. The Zevaḥim mishna only applies outside Eretz Yisrael
Correct: B — Rav Shimi bar Ashi explains that the two mishnayot cover different timing scenarios — one for animals mixed before consecration, one for animals already consecrated when mixed — making both rulings necessary.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
Why does the Gemara reject the simple a fortiori inference that bestiality-involved animals should be disqualified just like blemished ones (based purely on 'transgression')?
A. Because bestiality is not actually forbidden
B. Because an ox that plowed together with a donkey (a real transgression) remains fully fit for the altar — proving transgression alone doesn't disqualify an animal
C. Because blemished animals are never disqualified
D. Because the inference is never challenged
Correct: B — The counter-example of plowing with an ox and donkey (a genuine prohibited act) shows that mere involvement in a transgression doesn't automatically disqualify an animal from the altar, forcing a more refined analysis (capital-level transgressions, witnessed by two people).
QUESTION 4 OF 5
According to Rabbi Shimon, why does the Torah need to separately exclude BOTH an animal used in bestiality (rove'a) AND an animal that gored a person (noge'ach), rather than inferring one from the other?
A. They are actually the same case
B. Each has a unique stringency the other lacks — bestiality counts even if forced, while a goring animal's owner pays a ransom (not applicable to bestiality)
C. The Torah never actually excludes noge'ach
D. There's no reason; it's arbitrary repetition
Correct: B — Rabbi Shimon explains that each case has a distinguishing legal feature not shared by the other, so neither exclusion can be inferred from the other — both need their own explicit textual source.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
According to Rava's explanation of Gideon's bullock, how could it have been actually worshipped if worship-related consecration should disqualify it?
A. It was never actually worshipped at all
B. It was one of eight normally-forbidden acts specially and temporarily permitted to Gideon that night, per Rabbi Abba bar Kahana
C. The Torah's rules didn't apply before the Temple was built
D. Gideon was a priest, so special rules applied
Correct: B — Rava explains this was an extraordinary, one-time exception — Rabbi Abba bar Kahana lists eight normally-prohibited acts (including slaughtering outside the Tabernacle, at night, by a non-priest) that were specially permitted to Gideon that single night.
