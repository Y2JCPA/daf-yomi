📖 Overview: Opening Chapter Six — Animals Disqualified From the Altar

Temurah 28a–28b
כָּל הָאֲסוּרִין לְגַבֵּי מִזְבֵּחַ אוֹסְרִין בְּכׇל שֶׁהֵן
The scene: A new mishna opens Chapter Six with a list of animals permanently barred from the altar — those used in bestiality, set aside or worshipped as idols, given as payment to a prostitute or for a dog, crossbred, a tereifa, or born by caesarean section — and rules that even one such animal, mixed into any number of otherwise fit ones, disqualifies the entire group. The Gemara carefully distinguishes this ruling from two parallel mishnayot (in Zevaḥim and Avoda Zara) that might seem to say the same thing, explaining why each teaches something the others don't. It then works through the verse-by-verse derivation excluding each category from Leviticus 1:2's list of valid offering-animals, testing several attempted logical shortcuts (a fortiori inferences) that ultimately fail and require the Torah's own explicit exclusions. The daf closes with a debate over Gideon's seven-year-old bullock — was it actually worshipped, or merely set aside for future worship?

🚫 New Mishna: The Disqualified List

Any one of these, mixed in, ruins an unlimited quantity of otherwise-fit animals
CategoryDefinition
Rove'a / NirvaAn animal that copulated with a person, in either direction
MuktzehSet aside for idol worship — the animal itself is forbidden, but its ornaments may be sold to fund a real offering
Ne'evadActually worshipped as an idol — both the animal AND its ornaments are forbidden (though it may still be eaten by people)
Etnan / MeḥirGiven as payment to a prostitute, or as the price of a dog
Kilayim / Tereifa / Yotzei DofenCrossbred, fatally wounded, or born by caesarean section
The mishna's core ruling: Unlike ordinary halakhic mixtures (where a minority is often nullified by a majority), even ONE such disqualified animal mixed among any number of fit ones renders the whole group unfit for the altar.

🔄 Why Isn't This Mishna Redundant?

Two parallel mishnayot seem to say the same thing — each earns its own place
1️⃣ A mishna in Zevaḥim already says mixtures with 'forbidden-to-benefit' items (dying sin offerings, a stoned ox) are never nullified — why repeat it here for merely 'disqualified-from-altar' items?
↓
2️⃣ Answer: those are items whose very USE is forbidden; this mishna extends the rule even to items merely unfit for the ALTAR (but otherwise permitted to eat) — a real novelty
↓
3️⃣ Further distinction (Rav Shimi bar Ashi): one mishna covers animals that mixed while ALREADY consecrated (repulsive to the altar); this one covers animals that mixed while still NON-SACRED, before consecration
↓
4️⃣ A third parallel (Avoda Zara, on non-sacred items like idolatrous wine) is likewise needed — otherwise one might think only items 'repulsive to the Most High' resist nullification, while ordinary forbidden-benefit items among laypeople WOULD be nullified
Pattern: Each parallel mishna covers a distinct combination of variables (sacred/non-sacred timing, forbidden-to-use/merely-disqualified) — none is truly redundant.

⚖️ Excluding Robe'a and Nirva From the Altar

A tempting a fortiori inference, tested and partly rescued
Proposed inference
If a MERELY blemished animal (no transgression involved) is disqualified, shouldn't an animal used in BESTIALITY (an actual transgression) certainly be disqualified too?
Objection
Not necessarily — an ox that plowed WITH a donkey (a real transgression) remains fully fit for the altar! So transgression alone doesn't disqualify.
Refining the objection
But that plowing violation carries no death penalty; bestiality DOES (for the human participant) — so the comparison may still work for capital-level transgressions.
Rav Ashi's final limit
Even this only works when the transgression was witnessed by TWO people (enabling capital punishment) — a verse ('from the cattle') is still needed to cover cases based on ONE witness or the owner's own admission, where no such stringency applies.
Rabbi Shimon's parallel proof: He derives even the one-witness/admission case by his own a fortiori inference from a blemished animal (disqualified even by a single expert's ruling) — though the Rabbis still require the explicit verse.

📜 One Verse, Four Exclusions

Leviticus 1:2's phrase 'from the cattle, from the herd, or from the flock' does heavy lifting

Excluded by Context

  • 'From the CATTLE (behema)' → excludes rove'a/nirva — bestiality is described using this very word elsewhere (Lev. 20:15-16)
  • 'From the HERD (bakar)' → excludes ne'evad — idol worship is described using 'ox' imagery (Psalms 106:19-20)

Rabbi Shimon's Necessity Argument

  • 'From the FLOCK' → excludes muktzeh; the conjunction 'OR from the flock' → excludes an animal that gored a person
  • Why need BOTH rove'a and noge'ach excluded separately? Each has a unique stringency the other lacks (rove'a: even forced acts count; noge'ach: its owner pays a ransom) — so one exclusion couldn't imply the other
Alternative derivation: The school of Rabbi Yishmael instead derives rove'a/ne'evad from the word 'מָשְׁחָתָם' ('their corruption') used for blemishes — since that term elsewhere always signals sexual immorality or idolatry — freeing up the cattle/herd/flock verse to exclude sick, old, or filthy animals instead.

🐂 Was Gideon's Bullock Actually Worshipped?

Reish Lakish's definition of 'muktzeh' tested against the biblical story
Reish Lakish
'Muktzeh' means set aside specifically for SEVEN YEARS of future idol worship — proven from Gideon's father's 'second bullock of seven years' (Judges 6:25).
Objection
But that bullock wasn't just SET ASIDE — it was reportedly actually WORSHIPPED already! That would make it 'ne'evad,' not merely 'muktzeh'!
Rav Aḥa bar Ya'akov
No — it was set aside FOR future worship, but never actually worshipped in practice.
Rava's alternative
Actually, it WAS worshipped — this was one of EIGHT normally-forbidden acts specially permitted to Gideon that very night (per Rabbi Abba bar Kahana), including slaughtering outside the Tabernacle, at night, and by a non-priest.
Setting up the next daf: Rabbi Abba bar Kahana's list of eight extraordinary permissions granted to Gideon becomes its own extended discussion.
