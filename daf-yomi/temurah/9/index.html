📖 Overview: What Can Be Substituted, and How Many Times

Temurah 9a–9b
מְמִירִין מִן הַצֹּאן עַל הַבָּקָר, וּמִן הַבָּקָר עַל הַצֹּאן
The scene: Chapter Two opens with a mishna listing the category-crossings permitted (though still forbidden and flogged) in temurah: flock for herd, sheep for goats, male for female, unblemished for blemished — all derived from the verse's phrase 'animal for animal.' The Gemara analyzes the doubled word 'good' in the verse to derive two distinct rules: that substituting a good animal for a good one is still flogged, and that an animal blemished from birth can never be substituted for. A second mishna then debates whether one may substitute one animal for many (or many for one), with Rabbi Shimon restricting temurah to strictly one-for-one. The daf closes with Reish Lakish and Rabbi Yoḥanan's dispute over repeat substitution of the same consecrated animal, and a cluster of Rabbi Avin's dilemmas — all left as teiku — about substitution and the added one-fifth payment across chains of changed sanctity.

📖 Why Does the Verse Say 'Good' Twice?

Abaye and Rava each extract two separate rules from the repetition
Question
The verse could have said 'good for bad or bad for it' — why write 'for good' a second time?
Abaye
The extra 'good' teaches: if the animal was good from the start, before consecration, one CAN substitute for it. If bad from the start, one cannot.
Rava
Both instances of 'good' are needed. One teaches that even good-for-good substitution is flogged; the other teaches the 'good/bad from the start' rule.
Abaye's challenge
Why do I need a verse for good-for-good? A kal vachomer suffices: if substituting a good animal for a bad one (an improvement!) is flogged, surely good-for-good is too!
Rava's reply
'One does not administer punishment from a logical inference (קַל וָחוֹמֶר)' — lashes require an explicit verse, not mere logic.
Principle at stake: Rava's rule — אֵין עוֹנְשִׁין מִן הַדִּין — that corporal punishment can never rest on logical inference alone, only on the Torah's explicit words.

🔢 New Mishna: One-for-Two, Two-for-One, or Strictly One-for-One?

Rabbi Shimon dissents from the first tanna

First Tanna

  • One may substitute 1 for 2, 2 for 1, even 1 for 100 or 100 for 1
  • Source: 'בְּהֵמָה בִּבְהֵמָה' — a general term covering any number
  • 'A group is also called behema' (cf. 'behema rabba,' Jonah 4:11)

Rabbi Shimon

  • Substitution works only strictly one animal for one animal
  • Source: 'וְהָיָה הוּא וּתְמוּרָתוֹ' — just as 'it' (הוּא) is singular, so is the substitute
  • A group is called 'behema rabba,' never plain 'behema' alone
Note: Rabbi Shimon first argued from 'הוּא', but conceded his view could equally be derived from 'בְּהֵמָה בִּבְהֵמָה' once he saw how the Rabbis read that phrase.

🔄 Does Rabbi Shimon Allow Substituting Twice?

Reish Lakish and Rabbi Yoḥanan disagree about the scope of his one-for-one rule
Reish Lakish
Rabbi Shimon does concede that one may substitute, and then substitute again, for the same consecrated animal — its original sanctity never went anywhere!
Rabbi Yoḥanan
No — just as Rabbi Shimon forbids substituting two animals for one at once, he equally forbids substituting twice in sequence for the same animal.
Supporting baraitot
Baraitot exist supporting each position — one explicitly barring repeat substitution, the other deriving from 'הוּא וּתְמוּרָתוֹ' that one may substitute even with a hundred animals in sequence.
Unresolved tension: Both readings find textual support, leaving the precise boundary of Rabbi Shimon's 'one-for-one' rule genuinely contested.

❓ Rabbi Avin's Chain-of-Substitution Dilemmas

Each variant is tested and left as teiku
1️⃣ A guilt offering is substituted for once; it becomes blemished, its sanctity is transferred to a second animal — can THAT one be substituted for again?
↓
2️⃣ Abaye sharpens the question: is the real variable 'two bodies, one sanctity' or 'one body, two sanctities'? Rabbi Avin says both matter together
↓
3️⃣ Parallel question about the added ḥomesh (one-fifth): Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi rules only the ORIGINAL consecrator adds a fifth upon redemption, not one whose animal received transferred sanctity
↓
4️⃣ Every combination of 'same body / different sanctity' and 'different body / same sanctity' is raised — and every one ends in תֵּיקוּ
Why unresolved: These dilemmas probe exactly what 'the same act of substitution' means once an animal's sanctity has passed through several stages — a question the Gemara leaves open.
