📖 Overview: When Exactly Is a Sin Offering 'Lost'?

Temurah 22a–22b
אֲבוּדָה בִּשְׁעַת הַפְרָשָׁה — רוֹעָה
The scene: Reish Lakish states that a sin offering whose first year has passed is always left to graze, like an animal standing unreachable in a cemetery — seemingly contradicting the mishna's plain statement that it dies once the owner has atoned with a replacement. After several proposed readings are tested and rejected, Rava settles the matter by distinguishing single-disqualification cases (Reish Lakish's) from double-disqualification cases (lost AND blemished/aged). The Gemara then defines exactly what counts as 'lost' — distinguishing it from stolen or robbed, debating whether an animal merely out of sight (behind a door, mixed into a herd) qualifies, and probing whether blood already collected in a cup counts as 'atonement achieved' even before it's sprinkled. A new mishna then works through an elaborate chain of scenarios involving lost animals, lost money, and their replacements, closing with a dispute between Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and the Rabbis over what happens when both the original and its replacement turn up fit and available.

🔍 What Exactly Counts as 'Lost'?

Abaye's rule, and how visible an animal must be to NOT count as lost
📜 Abaye's tradition: 'lost' triggers death — but NOT stolen, and NOT robbed. Only genuine loss counts
↓
❓ How 'lost' must it be? Rabbi Oshaya: even ONE animal mixed into a large flock, or mixed with just one non-sacred animal, counts as lost
↓
❓ Rabbi Yoḥanan: an animal simply standing 'behind the door' counts as lost
↓
🤔 Does 'behind the door' mean ONLY there (unseen) counts, or does it mean EVEN there (where a turned head could spot it) counts — so anything less visible certainly does too?
↓
⏳ תֵּיקוּ — left unresolved, along with a parallel dilemma about loss known to a distant stranger but not the owner or shepherd
Underlying logic: An owner's hope that a lost (but not stolen/robbed) animal will be recovered is what keeps it in a 'graze' status rather than 'death' — theft removes that hope differently.

🩸 Does Blood in the Cup Count as Atonement Achieved?

Rav Pappa's dilemma about timing
1️⃣ A sin offering is lost; a replacement is designated, slaughtered, and its blood collected in a cup — but not yet sprinkled — when the original is found
↓
2️⃣ Question: does blood 'standing ready to be sprinkled' count AS IF already sprinkled (meaning atonement is effectively achieved, so the original dies)?
↓
3️⃣ Or: as long as it hasn't actually touched the altar, the situation is still 'lost at the time of designation' — so the original grazes
↓
4️⃣ A second version of the dilemma: blood was split into TWO cups, and one cup is lost while the other is sprinkled — does the missing cup 'count' as present via the remaining one?
Resolution: Both versions of this dilemma end in תֵּיקוּ — the precise moment atonement legally 'completes' remains genuinely unresolved.

💰 New Mishna: A Chain of Lost-and-Found Scenarios

Animal vs. money, lost vs. found, sacrificed vs. not — each combination has its own rule
ScenarioOutcome
Animal lost, replacement SACRIFICED, then original foundOriginal dies (atonement already achieved)
Money lost, replacement animal SACRIFICED, then money foundMoney is disposed of — cast into the Dead Sea
Money lost, replacement money designated, no purchase yet, then original money foundBuy a sin offering combining BOTH sums; leftover funds communal gifts
Animal lost, replacement blemished before sacrifice, then original found (also usable)Sell the blemished one; combine proceeds with the other to buy one sin offering; leftover to communal gifts
Guiding principle: Whenever no irreversible act (slaughter/sacrifice) has yet occurred, the law tries to salvage BOTH consecrated resources into one valid offering rather than wasting either.

⚖️ Both Animals Found Fit — Before Atonement

Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi vs. the Rabbis, plus Rabbi Elazar b'Rabbi Shimon's addition
Scenario
One designates a sin offering; it's lost; he designates a replacement; before sacrificing it, the ORIGINAL is found — and both are unblemished and fit.
Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi
One is sacrificed as the sin offering; the OTHER must die — since being lost at all, even briefly, already disqualifies it from ever competing again.
The Rabbis
Not so — a sin offering is left to die ONLY if found AFTER atonement was achieved. Since atonement hasn't happened yet, either animal may still be sacrificed.
Rabbi Elazar b'Rabbi Shimon
Extends the logic further: even a sold, now-non-sacred former sin offering 'dies' in status (can't be un-sold) if the second animal is sacrificed before the FIRST is slaughtered for ordinary consumption — the sale doesn't erase its prior consecrated history.
Underlying question: Does mere LOSS (even briefly, even before any atonement) permanently disqualify an animal, or does only actual completed atonement do so?
