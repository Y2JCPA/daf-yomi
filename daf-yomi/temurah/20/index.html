📖 Overview: Which Female Animals Carry Real Sanctity

Temurah 20a–20b
שֶׁאֵין לְךָ דָּבָר עוֹשֶׂה תְּמוּרָה, אֶלָּא הָרוֹעֶה לְהִסְתָּאֵב
The scene: Rabbi Shimon rules that a female animal designated for a burnt offering can generate a substitute, but one designated for a Paschal or guilt offering cannot — since only an animal that must genuinely graze until blemished (rather than being sold immediately) carries the inherent sanctity temurah requires. Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi partly disagrees regarding the Paschal offering, since its leftover becomes a peace offering (open to females) — but not regarding the guilt offering, whose leftover funds only communal gifts, which never generate temurah. The Gemara tests Rabbi Shimon's logic against tricky cases (a High Priest's substitute cow, a king's or individual's mismatched goat) before turning to a new mishna about the endless line of a guilt offering's substitute, and a debate — reversed, then reversed again — about whether that line can be sacrificed before or only after the original guilt offering achieves atonement.

🐏 Which Female-Designated Animals Make a Substitute?

Rabbi Shimon's principle, and Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's partial dissent
Rabbi Shimon
A female designated for a BURNT offering makes temurah (since a female bird burnt offering exists). But for a PASCHAL or GUILT offering — no temurah, since it may be sold immediately, without waiting for a blemish.
Principle
'No animal generates a substitute except one that must graze to become blemished' — real inherent sanctity is required.
Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi
I disagree about the Paschal offering: since a LEFTOVER Paschal animal becomes a peace offering (open to females), the mother has real sanctity and SHOULD make a substitute.
But not the guilt offering
A leftover guilt offering's proceeds fund only COMMUNAL gift offerings — and a community can never perform temurah, so this case stays as Rabbi Shimon ruled.
Key contrast: The Paschal leftover becomes an INDIVIDUAL peace offering (open to females); the guilt leftover becomes a COMMUNAL offering (which can't generate temurah anyway) — hence the different outcomes.

🧩 Stress-Testing Rabbi Shimon's Logic

Three tricky counter-cases, each resolved the same way
❓ A High Priest designates a COW instead of his bull for Yom Kippur — shouldn't it be sanctified, since the red heifer (a sin-offering-like animal) is female?
↓
💬 No — the red heifer is bedek habayit (Temple upkeep) sanctity, not altar sanctity; bedek habayit never makes temurah
↓
❓ An individual designates a MALE goat for his (female) sin offering — the king's goat sin offering is male, so shouldn't this count?
↓
❓ Or a KING designates a female goat — an individual's sin offering IS female, so shouldn't THAT count?
↓
✅ No to both — a king and an ordinary individual are 'two distinct bodies' (different classes of obligation); one's offering-status can't transfer to the other's mismatched case
The recurring answer: Status only counts as 'existing for the mother' when it belongs to a case that could genuinely apply to THIS specific person and situation — not merely to someone else in a similar role.

📦 Consecrating 'All My Possessions' — What Happens to the Animals?

A related mishna on unspecified consecration, with its own dispute

Rabbi Eliezer

  • Assumes bedek habayit (Temple upkeep) was intended by default
  • Males sold for burnt-offering use; females sold for peace-offering use
  • All proceeds go to Temple maintenance

Rabbi Yehoshua

  • Assumes burnt-offering intent by default
  • Males THEMSELVES sacrificed as burnt offerings; females sold for peace-offering use, proceeds fund burnt offerings
  • Only non-sacrificial property goes to Temple maintenance
The puzzle for Rabbi Yehoshua: If the females were consecrated with burnt-offering intent (deferred sanctity, since females can't be burnt offerings), how can they simply be sold and used for a DIFFERENT purpose (peace offerings)? Rabbi Yoḥanan resolves this by aligning Rabbi Yehoshua with the view that female-for-burnt-offering carries no inherent sanctity at all (per an alternate version of Rabbi Shimon's own position).

📋 New Mishna: The Guilt Offering's Substitute-Line

Three positions on animals descended from a guilt offering's temurah
PositionRuling
The Rabbis (default)Graze until blemished, sold, proceeds fund COMMUNAL gift offerings
Rabbi EliezerLeft to die (not sold at all)
Rabbi ElazarGraze until blemished, sold, but proceeds fund an INDIVIDUAL burnt offering (with semikha, libations, hide rights — unlike communal offerings)
Why both disputed cases are neededGuilt offering after atonement alone might suggest a special decree just for THAT case; substitute-line alone might suggest leniency since it was never eligible to begin with — each needs its own teaching
Real difference between Rabbi Elazar and the Rabbis: An individual offering requires the owner's hands (semikha), his own libations, and (if he's a priest) his own share of hide and service — a communal offering has none of these personal elements.

⏳ Before or After Atonement — Reversed Twice

Rav Naḥman's report, Rava's objections, and the corrected version
First version
Rav Naḥman (citing Rabba bar Avuh): the dispute applies only AFTER atonement; BEFORE atonement, everyone agrees the offspring itself can be sacrificed as a guilt offering.
Rava objects
Two problems: (1) one cannot achieve atonement through an item that came from a transgression (the substitute was created through a forbidden act); (2) a supporting baraita shows only the FIRST offspring is ever sacrificed — this offspring is already 'second-generation,' descended from a substitute!
Corrected version
Actually: the dispute applies only BEFORE atonement (should it graze, or die, out of concern it might still be wrongly offered as a guilt offering?); AFTER atonement, everyone agrees it's sacrificed as a burnt offering, since that concern no longer applies.
Same objection returns
But Rav Ḥananya's baraita (only the first offspring is ever sacrificed) STILL contradicts this corrected version too! The difficulty (קַשְׁיָא) stands unresolved.
Open question: The daf ends without fully reconciling how the 'only first offspring is sacrificed' principle fits with either version of the before/after-atonement distinction.
