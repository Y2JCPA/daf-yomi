📖 Overview: Tereifot, Their Young, and Two Kinds of Hekdesh

Temurah 31a–31b
יֵשׁ בְּקׇדְשֵׁי מִזְבֵּחַ מַה שֶּׁאֵין בְּקׇדְשֵׁי בֶדֶק הַבַּיִת, וְיֵשׁ בְּקׇדְשֵׁי בֶדֶק הַבַּיִת מַה שֶּׁאֵין בְּקׇדְשֵׁי מִזְבֵּחַ
The scene: The daf finishes the previous chapter's argument over whether the young of a tereifa may be sacrificed, and locates the dispute precisely: it depends on whether a tereifa can conceive at all, and on whether a fetus counts as 'the thigh of its mother'. It then extends the question to a chick hatched from a tereifa hen's egg, and to a kosher animal raised on a tereifa's milk. A new mishna opens the last chapter, setting out a two-column comparison: what is true of animals consecrated for the altar but not of property consecrated for Temple maintenance, and the reverse.

🐣 Where Exactly Do Rabbi Eliezer and the Rabbis Disagree?

The location of the dispute depends on a prior question — can a tereifa give birth?

If a tereifa CAN give birth

  • The case is: became a tereifa first, then conceived
  • R' Eliezer: this permitted factor (the father) and that prohibited factor (the mother) together → prohibited
  • The Rabbis: such a combination → permitted, like its father

If a tereifa CANNOT survive to give birth

  • The case is: conceived first, then became a tereifa
  • R' Eliezer: a fetus is the thigh of its mother — barred exactly as she is
  • The Rabbis: a fetus is not the thigh of its mother — no reason to bar it

Rav Huna's added claim

  • The Rabbis concede on a chick from a tereifa dove's egg
  • An animal fetus develops as its own entity in the womb
  • But an egg grows out of the hen's own body — so the taint carries
The move: Rather than choose between the two readings, the Gemara shows each is coherent once you fix the biological premise — a standard way of preserving both traditions.

🪱 Rava's Proof and Abaye's Reversal

A baraita about worms is enlisted, then turned around
Rava
A baraita supports Rav Huna: a ladleful of worms that came from a living person who then died — R' Eliezer says they impart corpse impurity, the Rabbis deem them pure.
Rava
The Rabbis dissent only about worms, which are a mere secretion. About an egg, which is part of the hen's body, even they would concede the chick is barred.
Abaye
No — the opposite is more reasonable. R' Eliezer takes worms to be part of the person, since a person is called a worm in his lifetime: 'the hope of man is a worm' (Job 25:6).
Abaye
But a chick emerges only once the egg rots, and what has rotted is mere dust, no longer ascribed to the hen. Even R' Eliezer should permit it.
Abaye
And it is taught explicitly in a baraita that R' Eliezer concedes the chick from a tereifa's egg is permitted — against Rav Huna.
Outcome: Abaye's explicit baraita is the stronger evidence; Rav Huna's inference from the worms does not hold.

🍼 A Kosher Animal That Suckled From a Tereifa

Rabbi Ḥanina ben Antigonus disqualifies it — but on what theory?
❓ Is it because the animal was simply fattened from a tereifa?
↓
🚫 That cannot be it — otherwise feeding an animal vetches of idolatry would bar it too, and it does not (unless the animal itself was set aside for idolatry)
↓
💡 Rabbi Ḥanina Terita taught before Rabbi Yoḥanan: the case is being given warm, fresh milk every morning
↓
✅ Each suckling sustains it for a full twenty-four hours, so the animal lives entirely off the tereifa — and is therefore disqualified from the altar
Distinction: Not nourishment in general, but total dependence for survival, is what transfers the disqualification.

🔪 Why a Tereifa Sacrificial Animal Is Never Redeemed

Deriving the limits of benefit from a redeemed disqualified offering
רַק בְּכׇל אַוַּת נַפְשְׁךָ תִּזְבַּח וְאָכַלְתָּ בָשָׂר בְּכׇל שְׁעָרֶיךָ
The rule and its source: The mishna holds that sacrificial animals that became tereifot are not redeemed — one does not redeem consecrated animals merely to feed them to dogs, which degrades them. The Sages read Deuteronomy 12:15, which governs redeemed disqualified offerings: 'you may slaughter' — but you may not shear their wool; 'and eat' — but you may not drink their milk. A second version reads it as a timeline instead: benefit is permitted only from the slaughter onward, so shearing and milk, which belong to the animal's lifetime, are excluded. On that second reading the tanna evidently does allow redeeming a consecrated animal to feed dogs, since that too comes after slaughter.

⚖️ Altar Consecrations vs. Temple Maintenance

The opening mishna of the final chapter, set side by side
FeatureConsecrated for the altarConsecrated for Temple maintenance
Creates a substitute (temurah)✅ Yes🚫 No
Piggul, notar, impurity — karet✅ Applies🚫 Does not apply
Slaughter outside the courtyard⚠️ Liable to karet🚫 Not liable
Paying craftsmen from the funds🚫 Forbidden✅ Permitted
Unspecified consecration goes here🚫 No✅ Yes
Me'ilah on by-products (milk, eggs)🚫 Not liable⚠️ Liable
Two clarifications: The 'everything consecrated for the altar makes a substitute' rule was taught only of a principal offering — not of birds, meal offerings, offspring or a substitute itself. And Rabbi Abbahu derives paying craftsmen from maintenance funds from 'And let them make Me a sanctuary' (Ex. 25:8) — it may be built from what is Mine.
