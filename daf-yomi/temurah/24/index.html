📖 Overview: Closing Chapter Four, Opening Chapter Five

Temurah 24a–24b
כֵּיצַד מַעֲרִימִין עַל הַבְּכוֹר?
The scene: Rabbi Hoshaya's ruling — that of two sin offerings designated as a mutual guarantee, atoning with one leaves the other to graze rather than die — is tested against the Rabbis, Rabbi Shimon, and finally pinned specifically to Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's own distinction between 'replacing a loss' and merely 'guaranteeing.' A related baraita distinguishing when an animal was lost (at designation vs. at atonement) is corrected by Rav Pappa's a fortiori logic. A digression on flaying disqualified or firstborn animals 'from the feet' uncovers three separate prohibitions with three distinct rationales. Chapter Four closes and Chapter Five opens with a new mishna on lawful artifice: designating an unborn fetus for a different offering (based on its eventual sex) to redirect it away from automatic firstborn status — along with the Gemara's discussion of whether one may even blemish such a fetus before it is born.

🐐 Two Sin Offerings as a Guarantee — Whose View Is This?

Rabbi Hoshaya's ruling tested against three candidate opinions
Rabbi Hoshaya
One who designates TWO sin offerings as mutual insurance atones with either one; the other simply grazes (not dies).
Test: the Rabbis?
Redundant — if even a true REPLACEMENT-for-loss isn't treated as lost by the Rabbis, obviously a mere guarantee isn't either.
Test: Rabbi Shimon?
Contradicted — Rabbi Shimon says ALL FIVE sin-offering-dies cases apply unconditionally, with no guarantee exception.
Conclusion: Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi
This must be Rebbi's own view: his 'dies' ruling applies ONLY when replacing something already lost — never to money or animals set aside merely as advance insurance.
A near-miss test: Rabbi Elazar b'Rabbi Shimon's own ruling (that a sold, blemished sin offering still 'dies in status' if the replacement is sacrificed first) initially looks like Rebbi's view extended too far — but is reattributed to his father Rabbi Shimon's unconditional position instead.

⏱️ Correcting a Baraita on Timing

Rav Shimi bar Ziri's version vs. Rav Pappa's logical fix
📜 First version: lost at DESIGNATION (before atonement) → Rebbi: dies; Rabbis: grazes. Lost at ATONEMENT → Rabbis: dies; Rebbi: grazes
↓
❓ Rav Pappa's objection: if the Rabbis are lenient (grazes) even for the MORE severe case (lost at designation), how could they be STRICT (dies) for the LESS severe case (lost at atonement)? That's backwards!
↓
✅ Corrected version: lost at designation → Rebbi: dies; Rabbis: grazes (as before). Lost at atonement → EVERYONE agrees: dies
The logical principle used: A kal vachomer (a fortiori inference): whatever the lenient position permits in the harder case must also be permitted in the easier case — any contradiction to that pattern signals a transmission error.

🦶 Why Flaying 'From the Feet' Is Sometimes Forbidden

Three separate cases, three separate rationales

On a Festival (all agree)

  • Forbidden simply because it's needless extra EFFORT unfit for the day
  • Applies to any animal, sacred or not
  • Uncontroversial — no dispute needed

A Firstborn — Beit Shammai

  • A blemished firstborn retains real sanctity (an Israelite can't even share it with a priest)
  • So flaying it 'from the feet' (a labor-like act) is forbidden
  • Limited to firstborn — sanctified from birth itself

Disqualified Consecrated Animals — R' Elazar b'R' Shimon

  • Even after redemption, sanctity can persist strongly enough to reach the money — as when a sold sin offering 'dies in status'
  • Alternative reasons offered: it LOOKS like performing labor with a sacred animal (Eretz Yisrael sages); or a decree against DELAYING slaughter to find a hide-buyer, risking illicit flock-raising
Why the sources can't simply merge: Beit Shammai's firstborn-specific reasoning (sanctified 'from the womb') doesn't obviously extend to redeemed disqualified animals, and vice versa — each case needs its own tanna and its own logic.

🐄 New Mishna: Artifice to Avoid Firstborn Status

Redirecting a fetus's future sanctity before it's even born
Declaration / outcomeResulting sanctity
'If male, a burnt offering' — born maleBurnt offering (olah)
'If female, a peace offering' — born femalePeace offering (shelamim)
Gave birth to one male AND one femaleMale → burnt offering; female → peace offering
Gave birth to TWO malesOne sacrificed as a burnt offering; the other SOLD to someone else who needs one — its sale proceeds are non-sacred
Gave birth to a tumtum or hermaphrodite (Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel)No sanctity takes hold on them at all
Why this works: By designating the fetus's sanctity BEFORE birth based on a condition (its eventual sex), the owner redirects it entirely away from automatic firstborn status — a lawful use of foresight, not deception.

🤰 May One Blemish the Fetus Before It's Born?

Rav Yehuda's ruling, limited carefully to the present era
❓ Rav Yehuda: it's permitted to blemish a firstborn fetus before it emerges — it has no sanctity yet
↓
⚠️ Objection: but the mishna itself only allows REDIRECTING sanctity (to a burnt/peace offering), not ABOLISHING it via blemish — doesn't that prove blemishing beforehand is forbidden?
↓
💬 Rav Yehuda clarifies: that mishna describes Temple times, when sacrifice is possible; my ruling is for TODAY, when firstborns can't be sacrificed anyway — so pre-emptive blemishing is the only way to make it usable at all
↓
✅ Even so: why not fear that blemishing right as 'most of the head' emerges (the exact moment sanctity begins) might strike an ALREADY-sacred animal? Answer: this concern doesn't override the greater risk of violating shearing/labor prohibitions while waiting for a natural blemish
Practical logic: Since today's firstborn can only be eaten once blemished anyway, allowing an early blemish avoids the greater risk of accidentally working or shearing it during a long wait.
