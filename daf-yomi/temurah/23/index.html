📖 Overview: Untangling the Rebbi/Rabbis Dispute From Every Angle

Temurah 23a–23b
מִילְּתָא דִּפְסִיקָא לֵיהּ קָתָנֵי, מִילְּתָא דְּלָא פְּסִיקָא לֵיהּ לָא קָתָנֵי
The scene: The daf offers two competing accounts of what Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and the Rabbis actually disagree about: Rav Huna frames it around whether the owner consulted the court before sacrificing one of two available animals, while Rabbi Abba frames it around whether atonement was achieved specifically with the LOST animal. Each framing is tested against outside sources — a baraita about preventing leftover meal offerings from becoming notar, and the Yom Kippur mishna about a rejected scapegoat's paired sin offering — and each survives only by being pinned to one side of the dispute. The mishna itself seems to refute both framings until the Gemara explains that it simply teaches the clear-cut case and leaves the disputed nuances for elsewhere. The daf closes with Rabbi Ami's ruling about money set aside merely 'as a guarantee' rather than to replace something lost, which turns out to matter specifically for Rabbi Shimon's more stringent position.

⚖️ Two Different Accounts of the Same Dispute

Rav Huna and Rabbi Abba each locate the Rebbi/Rabbis disagreement differently
Rav Huna (citing Rav)
Everyone agrees: if the owner just TOOK one animal and sacrificed it (without consulting the court), the other dies. They disagree only when the owner ASKS the court which to sacrifice — Rebbi: sacrifice the non-lost one (no special ordinance for consecrated items); Rabbis: sacrifice the LOST one (there IS such an ordinance).
Rabbi Abba (citing Rav)
Everyone agrees: if atonement is achieved via the NON-lost animal, the lost one dies. They disagree only when atonement is achieved via the LOST one — Rebbi: the replacement then dies too (it's 'as if' it were the lost one); Rabbis: the replacement merely grazes.
Same conclusion, different maps: Both framings try to explain the mishna's two differently-worded clauses as reflecting the same underlying Rebbi/Rabbis dispute — just locating the disagreement in a different variable.

🍞 Testing Rav Huna: Does a Protective Ordinance Exist?

Rav Mesharshiya challenges the claim that 'no ordinance' is even a real position
SourceWhat it seems to showResolution
Baraita on leftover meal offerings ('יֹאכְלוּ'/'יֹאכְלוּהָ')The Torah itself builds in mechanisms (eating with non-sacred food, or without it) to prevent leftovers from becoming notar — proof the Sages DO care about wasting consecrated itemsThis baraita reflects only the RABBIS' view, not Rebbi's — so it doesn't disprove Rebbi's position
Implication for RebbiIf Rebbi truly holds 'no ordinance for consecrated items,' does this baraita refute him?No — Rebbi can still hold his view for the specific sin-offering scenario; the meal-offering rule is a separate halakha
Key move: A general concern for preventing waste (as in the meal-offering case) doesn't automatically mean every sage endorses a specific 'ordinance' resolving THIS dispute the same way.

🐐 The Yom Kippur Goats Test Rabbi Abba's Framing

A second pair's sin-offering goat, treated like an animal 'designated to replace a loss'
Mishna (Yoma)
If the scapegoat dies, a new pair is drawn. The High Priest atones with the SECOND pair's sin-offering goat; the first pair's surviving goat grazes until blemished — since it's a COMMUNAL offering, it doesn't die.
Inference
This implies: were it an INDIVIDUAL's offering, it WOULD die. But the second-pair goat is like a replacement for something 'lost' (the first goat, functionally rejected) — and atonement is being achieved through the REPLACEMENT, not the lost one!
Problem for Rabbi Abba
Rabbi Abba says everyone agrees: atonement via the NON-lost (i.e., the replacement) means the lost one simply dies — no dispute needed here at all. So why would this scenario even reflect Rebbi's view specifically?
Resolution
This mishna must follow Rebbi ALONE — since only Rebbi holds that 'designating a replacement for something functionally lost' counts as if the ORIGINAL were lost, making the first-pair goat's death-by-default meaningful only under his view.
The deeper point: Rav's own principle — 'living animals are not [permanently] rejected' — determines which goat ultimately provides atonement, feeding directly into this dispute.

🔄 The Mishna Itself Seems to Refute BOTH Views

Resolved by one elegant principle about what a mishna chooses to teach
1️⃣ The mishna says: lost animal, replacement SACRIFICED, original found → dies. It says nothing about consulting the court, or about which one achieved atonement
↓
2️⃣ By implication: if the replacement was NOT yet sacrificed, the original simply grazes — regardless of who consulted whom, regardless of which animal is 'the lost one'
↓
3️⃣ This seems to refute BOTH Rav Huna (who needs 'consulting the court' to matter) AND Rabbi Abba (who needs 'atoning via the lost one' to matter)!
↓
4️⃣ Resolution: the mishna teaches only the ABSOLUTE, unconditional case (sacrifice always causes death) — and simply omits the more nuanced, disputed scenarios that depend on those specific variables
A second identical challenge: The exact same pattern of refutation-and-resolution recurs for the mishna's parallel case about lost MONEY (rather than an animal) — settled the same way.

💰 Money Set Aside 'As a Guarantee' — Not to Replace a Loss

Rabbi Ami's ruling, and why it specifically matters for Rabbi Shimon

For Rebbi and the Rabbis

  • Both agree: money designated as a mere GUARANTEE (not to replace something already lost) funds communal gifts if unused
  • For Rebbi: obvious, since his stringent 'dies/Dead Sea' rule applies only to true replacement-for-loss cases
  • For the Rabbis: an a fortiori inference — if even replacement-for-loss money isn't treated as 'lost,' surely guarantee-money isn't either

For Rabbi Shimon

  • Rabbi Shimon is MORE stringent — ANY atonement via a second animal kills the first, no exceptions
  • One might think he'd extend this even to mere 'guarantee' money
  • Rabbi Ami teaches: even Rabbi Shimon concedes guarantee-money funds communal gifts — proven from the Temple's own thirteen collection horns, six of which funded such gift offerings (per Rabbi Yehuda's report, defended against Rabbi Neḥemya's challenge via Yehoyada the priest's own teaching)
Why Rabbi Ami's ruling was needed: Without it, one might assume Rabbi Shimon's stringency about REPLACEMENT-for-loss money extends to simple GUARANTEE money too — he teaches that even Rabbi Shimon draws this line.
