📖 Overview: Opening Masechet Temurah

Temurah 2a–2b
הַכֹּל מְמִירִין, אֶחָד אֲנָשִׁים וְאֶחָד נָשִׁים
The scene: Masechet Temurah opens with the mishna's declaration that 'everyone' substitutes a non-sacred animal for a consecrated one — the substitution takes effect even though it is forbidden, and the substituter is flogged. The Gemara first resolves the mishna's apparent self-contradiction (does 'everyone substitutes' mean it is permitted?) using Rav Yehuda's reading and the verse in Leviticus 27:10. It then works through who exactly is included in 'everyone': an inheritor (disputed by Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehuda, each deriving the end of consecration from its beginning or vice versa), a woman (included via a doubled verb or an added conjunction), and finally raises open dilemmas about minors and gentiles that carry into the next daf.

⚖️ Resolving the Mishna's Contradiction

'Everyone substitutes' sounds permitted; 'if he substituted' sounds forbidden
Gemara
This mishna is self-contradictory: 'הַכֹּל מְמִירִין' implies substitution may be done ab initio, but then it says 'if one substituted' — only after the fact!
Gemara
And how could you even think 'everyone substitutes' means ab initio? The verse itself says: לֹא יַחֲלִיפֶנּוּ וְלֹא יָמִיר אֹתוֹ, 'he shall not alter it, nor substitute it' (Leviticus 27:10)!
Rav Yehuda
Read the mishna this way: 'Everyone can apply consecrated status to a non-sacred animal' by substitution — not that it is permitted, but that if one did it, the substitution takes effect and he is flogged.
Result: Temurah is a case where a forbidden act nonetheless achieves its result — the animal becomes consecrated even as the act is punished.

👨‍👩‍👧 Does an Inheritor Substitute?

Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehuda argue by comparing the start and end of consecration

Rabbi Meir

  • An inheritor places hands (semikha) on an inherited offering
  • An inheritor can effect substitution
  • Derives end-of-consecration (semikha) from the beginning (temurah): since an heir substitutes, he also places hands

Rabbi Yehuda

  • An inheritor does not place hands
  • An inheritor cannot effect substitution
  • Derives beginning (temurah) from the end (semikha): since an heir does not place hands, he also cannot substitute
Mishna's ruling: Since our mishna's 'everyone' includes the inheritor, it follows Rabbi Meir, not Rabbi Yehuda.

📜 Deriving Semikha From 'His Offering'

The word קׇרְבָּנוֹ appears three times regarding placing hands on a peace offering
❓ From where do we derive that only the owner places hands (semikha)?
↓
📖 'קׇרְבָּנוֹ' — 'his offering' — is written three times (Leviticus 3:2, 8, 12)
↓
Instance 1
His offering — not a gentile's offering
Instance 2
His offering — not another's offering (only the owner)
Instance 3
His offering — not his father's offering (an heir cannot place hands)
↓
🔀 Rabbi Meir needs the third instance for joint owners (בַּעֲלֵי חוֹבְרִין) instead, so for him an heir can place hands
Note: Rabbi Yehuda holds joint owners are not included at all, since their offering is not 'his own' individually.

🙋 Including a Woman in the Prohibition

Two tannaim, two different textual hooks for the same conclusion
OpinionTextual sourceWhat it teaches
Rabbi YehudaThe doubled verb אִם הָמֵר יָמִירIncludes a woman, since the passage otherwise speaks only in the masculine
Rabbi MeirThe extra conjunction וְאִם ('and if')Includes a woman; he does not need the doubled verb for this, using it instead for an inheritor
Both agreeNumbers 5:6, 'a man or woman' — general equation of punishmentsNot sufficient here, since temurah is a punishment that does not apply equally to everyone (the public and partners are excluded, 13a)
Why a special verse was needed: Since the general rule equating men and women applies only to punishments that apply equally to all, and temurah's flogging does not apply to the community or to partners, a woman's liability needed its own textual source.

👶🌍 Open Dilemmas: Minor and Gentile

Rami bar Ḥama's questions, framed by the halakhot of consecration
1️⃣ A minor who reached 'the age of vows' (עוֹנַת נְדָרִים) can consecrate an animal as a mufla — 'shall clearly utter' (Leviticus 27:2) includes him
↓
2️⃣ Question: since he can consecrate, can he also effect substitution — even though minors are not subject to punishments?
↓
3️⃣ Similarly: 'ish ish' — 'any man' — includes gentiles, who can bring vow and gift offerings like a Jew
↓
4️⃣ Question: since a gentile's consecration takes effect, can he also effect substitution, though he too is never subject to Torah punishments?
Rava's answer for the gentile (from a baraita): A baraita on animals consecrated by gentiles states plainly: אֵין עוֹשִׂין תְּמוּרָה — they do not effect substitution — though Rabbi Yosei limits Rabbi Shimon's leniency about misuse to altar-consecrations.
