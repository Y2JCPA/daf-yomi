📖 Overview: Finishing Standing and Valuation, and the Burial List

Temurah 33a–33b
וְהֶעֱמִיד אֶת הַבְּהֵמָה לִפְנֵי הַכֹּהֵן, וְהֶעֱרִיךְ הַכֹּהֵן אֹתָהּ בֵּין טוֹב וּבֵין רָע
The scene: The daf finishes the Rabbi Yoḥanan–Reish Lakish dispute by mining a single phrase. Rav shows how 'whether it be good or bad' supports Reish Lakish, and how the restrictive word 'it' (אֹתָהּ) must exclude something from standing and valuation — the two amoraim differ over what. Two baraitot are then thrown at each position, and each amora answers by fixing which kind of blemish the baraita means, with Reish Lakish going so far as to reverse a text. A new mishna then opens with two lists: sacred items that must be buried, and forbidden items that must be burned.

📏 Reading 'Whether It Be Good or Bad'

The same six words are made to support opposite conclusions

Rav for Reish Lakish

  • What has no distinction between good and bad? Temple maintenance — its sanctity is identical blemished or not
  • So the verse addresses maintenance consecrations
  • 'And the priest shall value it' then excludes altar animals entirely

Rav for Rabbi Shimon

  • What does divide between good and bad? Altar consecrations
  • So the verse addresses altar animals
  • Difficulty: the verse would then have to read 'value it between good and bad' — the phrasing as written favors the Rabbis

What 'it' excludes for R' Yoḥanan

  • Not altar animals — those are included
  • Rather an animal blemished from the outset and consecrated for the altar
  • Difficulty: the school of Levi taught that everything is included, even such an animal, and even wild animals and geese
The mechanism: The restrictive 'it' (אֹתָהּ) has to exclude something. Each amora spends it on a different case, and that choice is what their whole dispute reduces to.

📚 Two Baraitot on Consecrated Animals That Died

Each is a threatened refutation, and each is absorbed
BaraitaThe Rabbis' rulingHow the amoraim absorb it
First: unblemished → buried, blemished → redeemed (altar); maintenance → always buriedSeems to refute R' YoḥananR' Yoḥanan: it means blemished from the outset, which the Rabbis do exclude
Same baraita, read the other waySeems to refute Reish LakishReish Lakish: consecration preceded the blemish — and he reverses R' Shimon's clause in the text
Second: all buried (altar); maintenance redeemed if blemishedSeems to refute R' Yoḥanan againR' Yoḥanan: again, blemished from the outset
Second, on Reish Lakish's readingR' Shimon does not dispute the altar caseReish Lakish: he does dispute it — the text is to be understood accordingly
The test used throughout: Whichever reading makes Rabbi Shimon's disagreement land in a sensible place is preferred — if a reading leaves him objecting to the wrong clause, or to none, it is rejected.

🐕 May One Redeem a Sacrificial Animal to Feed It to Dogs?

Rabbi Yirmeya presses Rabbi Zeira on a consequence of Reish Lakish
R' Yirmeya
On Reish Lakish's reading, an animal consecrated while unblemished, which then developed a blemish and died, is still redeemed. Shall we conclude that one may redeem sacrificial animals in order to feed them to dogs?
Gemara
No. When the baraita says the animals 'died', it does not mean natural death. The case is that he transgressed and slaughtered the blemished animals before redeeming them — and slaughter renders them fit for human consumption once redeemed.
Baraita
So it is taught: sacrificial animals that developed a blemish and were slaughtered — Rabbi Meir says they are buried, holding like Rabbi Shimon that altar animals are in standing and valuation.
Baraita
And the Rabbis say: since an altar animal is not in standing and valuation, even one already slaughtered may be redeemed.
R' Yirmeya
Then why does Rabbi Shimon bury unblemished maintenance animals, if he holds they were never in standing and valuation?
R' Zeira
Because they are fit for the altar. A baraita teaches that unblemished animals consecrated for maintenance are redeemed only for the altar — sold to someone obligated in an offering, never into non-sacred use.
Why it matters: Redeeming an offering into dog food would degrade consecrated property; the Gemara avoids that conclusion by reading 'died' as 'was improperly slaughtered'.

🐐 An Animal Blemished Before It Was Ever Consecrated

Rav Pappa (or Rava) challenges Rabbi Yoḥanan from a mishna in Bekhorot
📜 Bekhorot 14a: an animal whose permanent blemish preceded consecration is not sanctified as an offering — it is sold to buy a valid one
↓
🐑 Consequences: firstborn status and priestly gifts apply, it may be shorn and worked, its offspring and milk are permitted, no karet outside, no temurah — and if it died, it is redeemed
↓
❓ Rav Yehuda in Rav's name says this mishna follows Rabbi Shimon. But R' Yoḥanan said everyone agrees such an animal is excluded — so why single out R' Shimon?
↓
Abaye's first answer
The 'Rabbis' who include even such an animal are a tanna of the school of Levi, not the Rabbis of the mishna
Abaye's second answer
Rav holds like Reish Lakish, so the mishna's latter clause cannot follow the Rabbis — or Rav does hold like R' Yoḥanan and did say 'R' Shimon and those who dispute him'
R' Shimon's own concession: He agrees that an animal blemished from the outset and consecrated for the altar is redeemed if it died — 'the priest shall value it' excludes it.

⚱️ The New Mishna: Buried, or Burned?

Two lists of items from which benefit is forbidden
1️⃣ Buried: a sacrificial animal's miscarried fetus, and a miscarried placenta
↓
2️⃣ Also buried: the stoned ox, the heifer whose neck is broken (egla arufa), the leper's birds, and the impure nazirite's shorn hair
↓
3️⃣ Also buried: the firstborn donkey whose neck was broken, meat cooked in milk, and non-sacred animals slaughtered in the courtyard
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Shimon dissents: non-sacred animals slaughtered in the courtyard are burned, like disqualified offerings — and by rabbinic decree so is a wild animal slaughtered there
↓
5️⃣ Burned: chametz on Pesach, impure teruma, orla and kilei hakerem — foods burned, liquids buried; and one may even light a fire with impure teruma bread and oil
The organizing idea: Disposal follows the item's nature: what can be consumed by fire is burned, what cannot is buried — and the dispute over courtyard-slaughtered animals is about whether they are treated as sacred refuse or ordinary forbidden matter.
