📖 Overview: How Forbidden Things Are Destroyed

Temurah 34a–34b
זֶה הַכְּלָל: כׇּל הַנִּקְבָּרִין לֹא יִשָּׂרְפוּ, וְכׇל הַנִּשְׂרָפִין לֹא יִקָּבְרוּ
The scene: The mishna's two lists — buried and burned — end with a hard rule: the methods may not be exchanged. The reason is not ceremonial but practical: ash of a burned item may be used, ash of a buried item may not, so burning what should be buried risks a leniency down the line. Tavi then presses Rav Naḥman with a contradiction between this mishna and one in Orla about a nazirite's hair and a firstborn donkey's, drawing four separate answers from four Sages. The daf — and the tractate — closes on when the ash of consecrated property is permitted, and when it is barred forever.

✂️ Tavi's Contradiction: Buried Here, Burned There

Our mishna buries the nazirite's hair; Orla 3:3 burns the garment it was woven into
Tavi
Our mishna: the hair of a nazirite is buried. But Orla teaches that one who weaves a sit of firstborn wool, or a nazirite's hair, into a garment — the garment is burned!
Rav Naḥman
No contradiction. Ours is an impure nazirite, who shaves and starts afresh; Orla speaks of the pure nazirite who completes his term — his hair goes under the peace-offering pot.
Tavi
That settles nazirite against nazirite. But the firstborn donkey against firstborn donkey still stands — ours buries its hair, Orla burns it.
Rav Sheshet
(via Tavi, when Rav Naḥman fell silent) There, the hair was woven into a sack; here it is the hair itself.
R' Yosei b. Ḥanina
The same distinction — woven into a sack versus the hair on its own.
R' Elazar
And the same nazirite distinction as Rav Naḥman's — Orla means the pure nazirite, ours the impure one.
Note: Rav Naḥman's silence on the donkey is preserved in the text — he asks Tavi whether he has heard an answer from others rather than supplying one.

🧵 Why Isn't the Forbidden Hair Simply Nullified?

Rav Naḥman presses Rav Sheshet on the woven-sack answer
❓ If the hair is woven into a sack, let it be nullified by the majority of permitted material — why burn the sack at all?
↓
💡 Rav Pappa: the case is where the forbidden hair was woven into an ornamental design of a bird — a significant item, and significant items are not nullified
↓
❓ Then why burn the whole sack? Remove the bird designs and keep the rest!
↓
R' Yirmeya
Orla follows Rabbi Yehuda, who permits burning what should be buried as a personal stringency
Rav Naḥman's retort
Even Rabbi Yehuda would not burn a sack he could simply repair — the objection was about removal, not stringency
↓
✅ R' Yirmeya clarifies: if removal is possible, fine — his answer is only a third resolution for where it is not
Design as significance: A woven bird is treated as a thing in its own right rather than as scattered fibres, which is why the majority does not swallow it.

🪵 Are the Ashes Permitted?

The reason behind the no-swapping rule, and the sources that seem to contradict it

The rule's rationale

  • Ash of a burned item — permitted
  • Ash of a buried item — forbidden
  • So burning what should be buried might lead one to use the ash

Two challenges

  • A corpse and menstrual blood that dried and crumbled are pure — so is buried matter's residue permitted? (Answered: pure of impurity, not permitted for benefit)
  • Rav Pineḥas: a bird burnt offering's crop and feathers leave the laws of me'ilah — yet they are buried

And the reverse challenge

  • A baraita: ashes of all burned prohibited items are permitted — except an ashera and consecrated property
  • Ashera and hekdesh taught separately because an ashera can be nullified by a gentile; hekdesh never can
  • So the ash of consecrated property is barred forever
Two resolutions: Rami bar Ḥama: ash is permitted where the consecrated item was already disqualified, or where burning it incurred me'ilah — the misuse itself desanctifies it; the baraita speaks of a fire that fell by itself. Rav Shemaya: the baraita is about the daily removal of the ashes.

🌅 Terumat HaDeshen — The Ash That Stays Forbidden

Reading Leviticus 6:3 word by word
1️⃣ 'And he shall take up the ashes to which the fire has consumed the burnt offering on the altar, and he shall put them beside the altar'
↓
2️⃣ 'And he shall put them' — he places them down gently, not casting them
↓
3️⃣ 'them' — he puts down all of them, not a portion
↓
4️⃣ The superfluous 'and' — he shall not scatter them
↓
5️⃣ This is the case in which the ash of consecrated property is prohibited forever — the removed ashes rest beside the altar and are never put to use
Closing the tractate: Temurah ends where it has been heading all along — on the precise fate of sanctity that can no longer serve its purpose, and on the care with which even its residue is handled.
