📖 Overview: Sin Offerings Left to Die, and a Community That Cannot

Temurah 15a–15b
חַטַּאת הַיָּחִיד שֶׁכִּפְּרוּ בְּעָלֶיהָ — מֵתָה, וְשֶׁל צִבּוּר — אֵינָהּ מֵתָה
The scene: A new mishna states a halakha from Sinai: five kinds of disqualified sin offerings must be left to die rather than redeemed, including one whose owner already achieved atonement with a replacement. This applies to an individual's sin offering but not the community's — a point Rabbi Yehuda disputes, and which Rabbi Shimon defends by analogy to other cases unique to individuals. The Gemara untangles a contradiction in how Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon are each quoted on the animal recovered after a replacement was already brought. Rav Pappa then explains why a communal sin offering never 'dies' even when some contributors have died: a community itself cannot die. This leads to Ezra 8:35's puzzling sin offerings — sacrificed generations after the sin, by people whose idolatry was intentional — explained as a provisional wartime-like edict. The daf closes with a moving digression on the generations of unblemished Torah scholars and a story about a suspiciously misjudged goat.

🔄 Whose Ruling Is It — Graze or Die?

A baraita seems to contradict what the mishna already told us
Baraita (as first read)
If one's lost sin offering is later found (after a replacement was already brought and sacrificed), Rabbi Yehuda says the extra animal should GRAZE until blemished; Rabbi Shimon says it should DIE.
Objection
But that's backwards! Our mishna already told us Rabbi Yehuda holds the animal DIES, and it's Rabbi Shimon's five-case list that includes this very scenario as one that dies!
First resolution
Don't swap the names — instead distinguish the CASES: the baraita discusses an animal found lost only at the time of SEPARATING the replacement (found before the replacement was sacrificed) — hence it may graze. The mishna discusses one still lost at the time of ATONEMENT itself.
Alternative resolution
Or: both sources discuss the same timing, but two versions of Rabbi Yehuda exist — one following Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's stricter view (dies even if found before sacrifice), one following the Rabbis' more lenient view (graze, since found before sacrifice).
Lesson: Apparent contradictions between parallel sources are often resolved by identifying a subtle difference in the case, not by assuming an error.

👥 Why Doesn't a Communal Sin Offering 'Die'?

Rav Pappa's principle: אֵין הַצִּיבּוּר מֵתִים — a community does not die
❓ If SOME of the community's contributors die before the offering is sacrificed, shouldn't the same 'owner died' rule apply?
↓
💬 Rav Pappa: No — a community, as an entity, never 'dies,' regardless of individual deaths
↓
📜 Proof: Communal Festival/New Moon goat offerings are funded from the Temple treasury chamber (תְּרוּמַת הַלִּשְׁכָּה), collected once a year
↓
✅ Some of THAT year's donors surely die before their offering is sacrificed months later — yet the offerings proceed without issue. The 'owner died' rule simply doesn't touch communal offerings
Why not just 'sons replace fathers'? The Gemara rejects deriving this from Psalms 45:17 ('your sons instead of your fathers'), since that would extend the leniency even to individuals, which is not the halakha.

📜 Ezra's Sin Offerings, Generations Late

How could returning exiles bring sin offerings for a sin they hadn't unwittingly committed?
הוֹרָאַת שָׁעָה הָיְתָה
The puzzle and its answer: Ezra 8:35 records twelve goat sin offerings brought by those returning from Babylonian exile — for the sin of idolatry committed generations earlier, in King Zedekiah's time. Two problems: sin offerings normally atone only for UNWITTING sin, yet idolatry under Zedekiah was intentional; and if the offering was for the dead generation, how could 'a community doesn't die' even apply, since these seem to be for people long gone? Rabbi Yoḥanan resolves the first difficulty: this was a הוֹרָאַת שָׁעָה, a provisional emergency ruling permitting sin offerings even for intentional sin. And the Gemara notes a supporting detail — the offering's odd count (12 bulls and goats for the 12 tribes, but 96 rams and 77 lambs corresponding to no obvious structure) itself signals this was an improvised, one-time exception rather than a standard sacrifice.

🐐 A Digression: The Last Unblemished 'Clusters'

From Yosef ben Yo'ezer's death to a story about wrongful suspicion
1️⃣ 'From the death of Yosef ben Yo'ezer and Yosef ben Yoḥanan, the אֶשְׁכּוֹלוֹת (clusters — scholars 'containing all') ceased' — and Torah was no longer studied exactly 'like Moses'
↓
2️⃣ Challenge: doesn't a baraita say 3,000 halakhot were already forgotten in Moses' own mourning period? Answer: what wasn't forgotten stayed learned at that level
↓
3️⃣ Story: a pious man used a goat's milk as medicine (tied to his bed); his friends suspected him of harboring 'an armed bandit' (a crop-destroying animal) — until they found this was his ONLY flaw
↓
4️⃣ The man himself, dying, agreed: his sole sin was violating the Sages' ban on raising small livestock in inhabited Eretz Yisrael
The point: Later generations (Rabbi Yehuda ben Bava, Rabbi Yehuda bar Ilai) could still be flawless in this way — proving unblemished piety didn't end with the earliest 'clusters.'
