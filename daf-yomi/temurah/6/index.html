📖 Overview: Locating the Real Dispute, Then Blemished Offerings

Temurah 6a–6b
כִּי קָא מִיפַּלְגִי אַבָּיֵי וְרָבָא בְּשִׁינּוּי קוֹנֶה
The scene: The daf completes the long run of test cases for Abaye and Rava's dispute — robbery of altered goods, seized collateral, and pe'ah given from dough — each reconciled with its own verse. Rav Aḥa then asks the pointed question: if every case has been explained away, what do Abaye and Rava actually disagree about in practice? The Gemara settles on fixed interest, though even that is reframed as a dispute over verses (Rabbi Elazar vs. Rabbi Yoḥanan) rather than the theory itself, concluding that Abaye and Rava differ only in their default assumption when no verse says otherwise. The daf then pivots to a new topic: the five (or six) separate prohibitions violated by consecrating a blemished animal for the altar, and a detailed study of the Yom Kippur scapegoat's own blemish-exemption.

💰 So What DO They Disagree About?

Rav Aḥa's challenge to Rav Ashi
1️⃣ Rav Aḥa, son of Rava, asks Rav Ashi: now that every case has been explained away with its own verse, where do Abaye and Rava actually differ in practice?
↓
2️⃣ First answer: fixed interest (ribbit ketzutza) — must a court forcibly return it to the borrower?
↓
3️⃣ But this too turns out to map onto an earlier dispute of Rabbi Elazar (returned) and Rabbi Yoḥanan (not returned), each with independent verses — not a pure consequence of 'effective vs. ineffective'
↓
4️⃣ Final answer: Abaye and Rava disagree only theoretically, in שִׁינּוּי קוֹנֶה — the default assumption when the Torah gives no specific verse either way
Bottom line: Abaye's default: a forbidden act, absent a stated exception, is presumed effective. Rava's default: it is presumed ineffective.

📈 Fixed Interest: Two Amoraic Layers

Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Yoḥanan's dispute maps loosely onto Rava and Abaye

Rabbi Elazar (≈ Rava)

  • Fixed interest paid IS repossessed by the court
  • Source: 'that your brother may live with you' — the interest must return so the debtor can live alongside the lender
  • The forbidden act did not effectively transfer ownership

Rabbi Yoḥanan (≈ Abaye)

  • Even fixed interest is NOT repossessed by the court
  • Source: Ezekiel 18:13 — the usurer 'shall not live' (punished with death), but the money is not said to be returned
  • Also compared to a bloodshedder — an irreversible wrong
Complication: Abaye himself holds fixed interest IS forcibly returned in a mixed cloak/interest case — showing this dispute is driven by its own verses, not simply by the general theory.

🐐 Does 'Unto the Lord' Include or Exclude the Scapegoat?

Two verses about burning a blemished animal, read in opposite directions
Baraita
'Nor make an offering by fire of them... unto the Lord' (Lev. 22:22) — 'unto the Lord' includes the scapegoat: even it may not be blemished, though it is never burned on the altar.
Objection
But elsewhere, in the law of slaughtering outside the Temple, 'unto the Lord' excludes — animals not exclusively dedicated to God (like the scapegoat) are excluded from that prohibition!
Rava
No contradiction: context decides the direction. There, the surrounding phrase already included broadly, so 'unto the Lord' narrows it. Here, the surrounding phrase ('fire') already excludes the scapegoat, so 'unto the Lord' broadens it back in.
Further question
But if the scapegoat is chosen only by a lottery between two fit goats, how could it ever have been blemished to begin with?
Rava's answer
The verse addresses a specific case: the scapegoat became blemished mid-day, after selection, and the priest transferred its sanctity to a replacement — that replacement still may not be blemished.
Point: Even the scapegoat, chosen by lottery for a different fate than sacrifice, must still be physically unblemished.

🚫 Consecrating a Blemished Animal: Five Violations

Leviticus 22:20 is read to prohibit even the act of consecration itself
Violated prohibitionWhat it forbids
בַּל תַּקְדִּישׁוּConsecrating a blemished animal for the altar at all
בַּל תַּקְרִיבוּBringing it as an offering
בַּל תִּשְׁחֲטוּSlaughtering it as an offering
בַּל תִּזְרְקוּSprinkling its blood on the altar
בַּל תַּקְטִירוּ (כּוּלּוֹ / מִקְצָתוֹ)Burning all — or even part — of it on the altar
(R' Yosei b'R' Yehuda adds:) קַבָּלַת הַדָּםCollecting its blood in a vessel
Source: Each violation is drawn from a distinct phrase in Leviticus 22:20–22, showing that one act of misconduct can breach several separate biblical prohibitions.
