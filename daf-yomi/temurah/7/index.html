📖 Overview: Counting Violations, Then a New Mishna

Temurah 7a–7b
אֵין הַכֹּהֲנִים מְמִירִין לֹא בְּחַטָּאת וְלֹא בְּאָשָׁם וְלֹא בִּבְכוֹר
The scene: The daf finishes the blemished-animal discussion: Reish Lakish questions whether the five prohibitions apply only to an animal that became blemished after consecration, or even one blemished from birth, likening the latter to a worthless date palm; Rabbi Yoḥanan replies that consecrating a born-blemished animal is disgraceful precisely because superior animals were available. Abaye and Rava then dispute whether burning a whole blemished offering and burning part of one count as one violation or two. The daf then introduces a new mishna: priests and Israelites each substitute only for their own offerings, and priests cannot substitute for a sin offering, guilt offering, or firstborn they received as a gift — with Rabbi Akiva grounding the rule in temurah's own verse, which requires that sanctity take hold 'in the house of the owner.'

🌴 Is a Born-Blemished Animal Like a Date Palm?

Reish Lakish challenges whether all five prohibitions really apply equally
Reish Lakish
Perhaps the five prohibitions apply only to an animal that became blemished after being fit. One consecrated blemished from birth is like consecrating a mere date palm — his real intent is its value, which is permitted!
R' Ḥiyya bar Yosef
But the verse itself lists שָׂרוּעַ וְקָלוּט — 'too long or too short' — congenital blemishes, right in the passage of prohibition!
Rabbi Yoḥanan
The real difference is disgrace (בִּזְיָא מִילְּתָא): a date palm was never a candidate for the altar at all. But this person had unblemished animals available and chose a blemished one instead — that is what makes him liable.
Rava adds
If disgrace is the reason, then even one who consecrates it explicitly for its value, to buy libations, should be flogged too — the act itself remains disgraceful.
Result: A baraita is found supporting Rava: consecrating a blemished animal even 'for a gift' (i.e., for Temple upkeep) is included in the prohibition, not just consecration as an altar offering.

🐑🐐 New Mishna: Who Substitutes for What?

Priests and Israelites each substitute for their own consecrated animals
📜 Mishna: priests substitute for their own offerings; Israelites for theirs. Priests do NOT substitute for a sin offering, guilt offering, or firstborn they received
↓
❓ R' Yoḥanan ben Nuri: why not the firstborn? Unlike sin/guilt offerings, priests DO acquire a firstborn during its lifetime, while it is still with the Israelite owner!
↓
💬 R' Akiva: 'both it and its substitute shall be holy' — sanctity must take hold in the house of the (original) owner
↓
✅ Since the firstborn was never consecrated in the priest's house — it was born already sacred, in the Israelite's house — the priest cannot be the one to substitute for it
Key verse: הוּא וּתְמוּרָתוֹ יִהְיֶה קֹּדֶשׁ — the same phrase used throughout the tractate now anchors a rule about whose 'ownership' substitution requires.

🏠 Rabbi Yoḥanan ben Nuri vs. Rabbi Akiva

Same mishna, two different lenses on 'ownership'

R' Yoḥanan ben Nuri

  • Focuses on acquisition timing
  • Priests acquire a firstborn during its life, unlike sin/guilt offerings
  • Sees no reason to equate the firstborn with sin/guilt offerings

Rabbi Akiva

  • Focuses on where sanctity first took hold
  • A firstborn's sanctity begins automatically at birth, in the Israelite's home
  • Only the person in whose house consecration happened can substitute — so the priest cannot
Halakhic outcome: Priests cannot substitute for a sin offering, guilt offering, or firstborn received from an Israelite, though the reasoning differs between the two tanna'im.

💰 Selling an Unblemished Firstborn: Now or When the Temple Stood?

Rav Naḥman and Rava argue over a mishna about firstborn sales
Mishna
A firstborn offering may be sold unblemished only while alive; if blemished, alive or slaughtered. A priest may betroth a woman with it.
Rav Naḥman
This applies only nowadays, with no Temple, since the priest fully owns it. When the Temple stands, an unblemished firstborn is destined for the altar — it may not be sold alive at all!
Rava objects
But if the mishna limits the sale to 'while alive,' implying not once slaughtered — that only makes sense when the Temple stands, since nowadays a firstborn is never slaughtered as an offering at all!
Rav Naḥman clarifies
The mishna is still about nowadays; it isn't excluding a slaughtered case, just teaching that even today one may sell it alive.
Point at stake: Whether a priest's ownership of an unblemished firstborn is full ownership (allowing sale) even while the Temple stands and the animal awaits sacrifice.
