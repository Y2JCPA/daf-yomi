📖 Overview: Closing Chapter Three, Opening Chapter Four

Temurah 21a–21b
הֵם קְרֵיבִין, וְאֵין תְּמוּרָתָן קְרֵיבָה
The scene: A new mishna describes the substitute of a firstborn or animal tithe offering: unlike other substitutes, it is never sacrificed on the altar, but grazes until blemished and is then eaten (by priests for a firstborn, by owners for a tithe) — with several practical differences from ordinary consecrated animals, including that unblemished ones may even be brought from outside Eretz Yisrael. The Gemara asks whether inflicting a blemish on such a substitute carries liability, resolving it by analogy to the 'ninth' animal miscounted during tithing. A striking contradiction — the mishna permits unblemished firstborns from outside the land, yet a story shows ben Antigonus's were rejected — is resolved by attributing each ruling to a different tanna among three elders, whose logic for various 'must be eaten in the Temple's presence' halakhot (second tithe, firstborn, first fruits) is explored at length. The daf ends Chapter Three and opens Chapter Four with a mishna recapping which sin offerings are left to die.

🐄 New Mishna: The Firstborn/Tithe Substitute Is Different

Four ways it departs from every other kind of substitute
FeatureOrdinary substitutesFirstborn/tithe substitute
Where sold once blemishedButchers' market (איטליז), weighed by the litraOnly from the home — never in the market, never by weight
Redemption available?Yes — sold, proceeds sacred, animal non-sacredNo redemption at all — it stays consecrated until eaten in its blemished state
Brought from outside Eretz Yisrael?Yes, generallyOnly if it arrives unblemished; if blemished, eaten locally by owner/priest
Who eats it once blemished?N/AFirstborn's substitute: the priests. Tithe's substitute: the owners
Rabbi Shimon's reasoning: Firstborn and tithe animals have 'sustenance in their own place' — they can be eaten once blemished without needing to reach the Temple — unlike ordinary consecrated animals, which must be redeemed for a proper offering.

🩹 Is One Liable for Blemishing This Substitute?

A dilemma resolved by analogy to the mistakenly-counted 'ninth' tithe animal
❓ Since the substitute of a firstborn/tithe is never sacrificed on the altar, does the Torah's prohibition against blemishing a sacrificial animal even apply to it?
↓
🔀 Compare: the 'ninth' animal mistakenly counted as the tenth during tithing — also consecrated (can't be worked or sheared) but also never reaches the altar
↓
📖 For the ninth: the verse 'the TENTH shall be holy' excludes it — so blemishing it carries NO liability
↓
✅ Same logic applies here: 'THEY (the firstborn/tithe) are holy' (Num. 18:17) — THEY are sacrificed, not their substitute — so blemishing the substitute carries no liability either
Pattern: Wherever a verse explicitly limits altar-sacrifice to the 'original' item and excludes a derivative, that same exclusion extends to the blemishing prohibition too.

🌍 Why Were Ben Antigonus's Firstborns Rejected?

A direct contradiction with the mishna's own ruling, resolved by dividing the halakha between two tanna'im
Mishna
Firstborn and tithe offerings should not be brought from outside Eretz Yisrael ab initio, but if unblemished ones DID arrive, they ARE sacrificed.
Historical account
But ben Antigonus actually brought firstborns up from Babylonia — and the Temple did NOT accept them!
Rav Ḥisda resolves
No contradiction: the mishna follows Rabbi Yishmael (accepts them after the fact), while ben Antigonus's rejection follows Rabbi Akiva (never accepts them, even unblemished).
Rabbi Akiva's proof
'You shall eat... the tithe of your grain... and the firstborn of your herd' (Deut. 14:23) — only from a place you bring UP grain tithe (i.e., Eretz Yisrael) may you bring a firstborn at all.
Deeper stakes: This dispute belongs to a set of three teachings by three elders (Rabbi Yishmael, Rabbi Akiva, ben Azzai), each addressing how far consecrated status extends geographically.

📜 Three Elders' Methods: Analogy vs. Juxtaposition

Each elder tries — and refines — an inference before landing on the right verse

Rejected: Simple Analogy (Kal Vachomer-style)

  • 'Second tithe requires bringing to a place, like a firstborn — so it too can't be eaten today'
  • Refuted: a firstborn uniquely requires blood/portions on the altar; second tithe has no such requirement
  • The two cases aren't comparable enough to infer directly

Accepted: Direct Verse Juxtaposition

  • Deuteronomy 14:23 lists tithe of grain AND firstborn in one verse
  • This textual juxtaposition (הֶקֵּשׁ) directly links their halakhot, bypassing the need for logical analogy
  • Rabbi Yoḥanan's rule: sacrificial law generally can't be derived from a law that was ITSELF derived — but second tithe is non-sacred, so this restriction doesn't block the inference
Ben Azzai's parallel move: He similarly first tries (and rejects) a simple analogy to prove second tithe must be eaten specifically within Jerusalem's walls, before landing on the same verse's juxtaposition to reach that conclusion.

Chapter Four opens by restating and refining the earlier list
CaseOutcome
