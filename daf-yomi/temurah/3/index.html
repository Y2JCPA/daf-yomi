📖 Overview: Gentiles, Misuse, and Lashes Without an Action

Temurah 3a–3b
כׇּל לֹא תַעֲשֶׂה בַּתּוֹרָה, עָשָׂה בָּהּ מַעֲשֶׂה — חַיָּיב, לֹא עָשָׂה בָּהּ מַעֲשֶׂה — פָּטוּר
The scene: The daf opens by closing (unresolved, as teiku) Rami bar Ḥama's dilemma about a gentile who consecrates an offering that a Jew then brings for atonement. It then works through the baraita's list of halakhot that do not apply to gentiles' consecrated animals — misuse, piggul, notar, tumah, and temurah — each traced to its own verse, with Rabbi Yosei dissenting about property consecrated for Temple maintenance. The Gemara then turns to Rav's broad principle that a prohibition violated without a physical action carries no lashes, and lists its exceptions: oaths, substitution, and cursing another with God's name. A long analysis of oaths about the past versus the future follows, asking exactly which false statements are punished.

❓ A gentile consecrates an animal, but a Jew achieves atonement with it — who controls whether it can undergo temurah?

Follow the consecrator
The gentile consecrated it — he cannot effect substitution, so no one can
Follow the one atoning

🏛️ Rabbi Yosei's Stringency

Not all consecrated property of a gentile is treated the same

Kodshei Mizbe'ach (altar offerings)

  • Sanctity inheres in the animal's body (kedushat haguf), like teruma
  • The 'sin'–'sin' analogy applies
  • No liability for misuse of a gentile's offering

Kodshei Bedek HaBayit (Temple upkeep)

  • Sanctity inheres in the item's value (kedushat damim) — it will be sold
  • Not comparable to teruma
  • One is liable for misuse, even of a gentile's donation
Rabbi Yosei says: 'With regard to all of them I see fit to be stringent' — reading Leviticus 22:18 as equating a gentile's ('stranger's') offering fully with a Jew's.

🚷 Rav's Rule: No Action, No Lashes — Except Three

Rav Yehuda cites Rav; Rabbi Yosei HaGelili via Rabbi Yoḥanan lists the exceptions
1️⃣ General principle: for any Torah prohibition, if one transgressed with a physical act, he is flogged; without an act, he is exempt
↓
2️⃣ Challenge: temurah involves no action (only speech), yet the mishna flogs the substituter!
↓
3️⃣ Answer: our mishna follows Rabbi Yehuda, who holds a 'prohibition without an action' is flogged — though it disagrees with him about the inheritor
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Yosei HaGelili's list of true exceptions to the general rule: one who takes an oath, one who effects temurah, and one who curses another using God's name — all flogged despite no action
Rabbi Yoḥanan's refinement: Don't actually list temurah among the 'no-action' exceptions — speech itself performs an act of consecration in that case.

🤥 Which False Oaths Bring Lashes?

Rabbi Yoḥanan, Reish Lakish, and Rabbi Abbahu narrow the case
Objection
If a false oath about the future ('I will eat this loaf today') goes unfulfilled, is the oath-taker flogged? We learned that Rabbi Yoḥanan and Reish Lakish both say: he is not flogged!
R' Yoḥanan
Not flogged, because it is a prohibition without an action — and no prohibition without an action brings lashes.
Reish Lakish
Not flogged, but for a different reason: there was only an uncertain forewarning (no single moment of transgression).
R' Abbahu
So what false oath does bring lashes without an action? It must be about the past: 'I swear I ate it' (when he didn't), or 'I swear I did not eat it' (when he did).
Rava
The Torah explicitly likens a false oath (שֶׁקֶר) to an oath in vain (שָׁוְא) — just as 'in vain' refers to the past, so must the false oath that is flogged.
Conclusion: Only a false oath about a past fact is punished by lashes without an accompanying action; an unfulfilled future oath is not.
