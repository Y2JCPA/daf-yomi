📖 Overview: Testing 'Does a Forbidden Act Take Effect?'

Temurah 5a–5b
וְהָיָה הוּא וּתְמוּרָתוֹ יִהְיֶה קֹּדֶשׁ
The scene: Having introduced the dispute between Abaye (a forbidden act still takes effect) and Rava (it accomplishes nothing; the lashes alone punish the transgression), the Gemara runs an extended series of test cases through both positions: the rapist forced to remarry his divorced victim, teruma separated from poor produce to cover choice produce, teruma separated across different crop types, consecrating a blemished animal, a widow's marriage to a High Priest, and — central to the tractate — temurah itself, whose verse 'both it and its substitute shall be holy' becomes Rava's own proof that substitution is a special case where the Torah explicitly makes the act effective. Each time a mishna seems to refute one opinion, the Gemara finds a specific verse that limits that ruling to its own case, restoring the general rule.

💍 First Test Case: The Rapist Who Divorced His Victim

Deuteronomy 22:29, 'he may not send her away all his days'
Challenge
An Israelite rapist who divorces his victim remarries her and is not flogged. If Rava is right that transgressions are always effective, shouldn't the divorce have worked, making him liable for divorcing her?
Rava
It's different there — the verse says 'all his days,' teaching that he remains bound to remarry her forever. Since he must undo it, the divorce is treated as if it never fully took hold, so no lashes.
For Abaye
And what does 'all his days' teach you, who already says the act works? Without it, I would think he may choose whether or not to remarry her.
Answer
'All his days' teaches that remarriage is obligatory, not merely available — a distinct halakha for each opinion.
Pattern to watch: This exchange sets the template for the whole daf: a mishna seemingly refutes one opinion, and a specific verse rescues it as a special case.

🫒🌾 More Test Cases: Effective or Not?

Each mishna's outcome is matched to a verse that limits it to its own halakha
CaseMishna's rulingReconciling verse
Teruma from poor produce for choice produceEffective (his teruma is teruma)"You shall bear no sin by reason of it" (Num. 18:32) — 'sin' implies it must have consecrated status
Teruma from one crop type for anotherNot effective"The first part of them" (Num. 18:12, repeated) — 'reishit' for each type separately
Redeeming an item devoted (ḥerem) to a priestNot effective — given straight to the priest"It is most holy" (Lev. 27:28) — 'as it is, it shall remain'
Teruma separated before bikkurimEffective, though forbidden"From all your tithes you shall set apart" (Num. 18:29) — teaches effectiveness regardless of order
Note the symmetry: Whichever way the mishna rules, that side's proponent must show the ruling is a special exception, not a refutation of the general theory.

🐐 Temurah Becomes the Test Case

The tractate's own verse settles a piece of the Abaye–Rava debate
❓ Substitution is forbidden, yet the mishna says it takes effect and the animal becomes holy — doesn't this refute Rava?
↓
📜 Rava answers: the very same verse states explicitly, וְהָיָה הוּא וּתְמוּרָתוֹ יִהְיֶה קֹּדֶשׁ — 'both it and its substitute shall be holy'
↓
✅ This is a Torah-stated exception: temurah's effectiveness is not proof that forbidden acts work generally, only that this specific forbidden act does
↓
🤔 And for Abaye, who needs no special verse for effectiveness? He reads 'הוּא וּתְמוּרָתוֹ' as teaching something else: that the original animal retains its sanctity too (not just the substitute)
Why it matters: Temurah is the paradigm case for the whole sugya — the one place the Torah spells out in so many words that a forbidden substitution 'sticks.'

👑 Two More Cases, Two More Special Verses

A widow's marriage to a High Priest; a blemished animal consecrated for the altar

Widow to High Priest

  • Forbidden (Lev. 21:14–15), yet the marriage IS valid — offspring follows the 'flawed' status
  • Rava: "he shall not profane his seed" (לֹא יְחַלֵּל) implies the union took effect enough to produce a profaned child
  • For Abaye: the doubled language teaches two profanations — for him and for her

Blemished Animal to Altar

  • Forbidden (Lev. 22:20), yet consecration IS effective
  • Rava: "for a vow it shall not be accepted" (לֹא יֵרָצֶה) — only its sacrifice fails, so consecration itself succeeded
  • For Abaye: without this verse, one might think the blemished offering could even be sacrificed
Method repeats: In case after case, Rava needs to locate the verse that makes an exception; Abaye needs to locate the verse teaching some other detail.

⚖️ The Sugya's Method, Step by Step

How the Gemara tests a general theory against many mishnayot
1️⃣ A mishna rules that a forbidden act either succeeded or failed
↓
2️⃣ If it succeeded, this seems to refute Rava (nothing forbidden should work)
↓
3️⃣ If it failed, this seems to refute Abaye (everything forbidden should work)
↓
4️⃣ Each time, the challenged sage points to a verse unique to that halakha, preserving his general rule as the default everywhere else
Takeaway: By the end of this sugya, temurah stands as Rava's own proof-text — the one forbidden act the Torah says outright takes effect.
