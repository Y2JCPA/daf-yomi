📖 Overview: Rectifiable Prohibitions and Temurah's Exception

Temurah 4a–4b
לָאו שֶׁנִּיתָּק לַעֲשֵׂה — לָא לָקֵי
The scene: The daf finishes the discussion of cursing another with God's name, then untangles which of Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina ruled that separating teruma before bikkurim (first fruits) carries lashes, using a parallel dispute about tithing baskets out of order. Rabbi Avin explains the exemption: it is a prohibition 'that can be rectified' by a later positive mitzva (לָאו שֶׁנִּיתָּק לַעֲשֵׂה), and such prohibitions normally carry no lashes. Abaye challenges this from temurah itself, which is also rectifiable yet does carry lashes — resolved because temurah involves two prohibitions with only one positive mitzva to rectify them. The daf closes with a broader tannaitic dispute about the Paschal offering's leftover meat, and Abaye and Rava's fundamental question: when the Torah forbids an act, does the act still take effect if performed anyway?

🗣️ Two Verses Needed: Prohibition and Warning

A punishable prohibition requires both an issur and a distinct azhara (warning of punishment)
CaseProhibitionIts warning (azhara)
Cursing another with God's nameDeuteronomy 28:59, 'the Lord will make your plagues wondrous'"You shall not curse the deaf" (Leviticus 19:14) — a separate verse
Pronouncing God's name in vainWould need the same verse as aboveNone available — "You shall fear the Lord" (Deut. 6:13) is a positive command, which cannot serve as a warning
Conclusion: Because a warning verse exists for cursing another but not for uttering the Name in vain, Deuteronomy 28:59 must be referring to the former, not the latter.

🧺 Who Says Teruma-Before-Bikkurim Is Flogged?

Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina disagree; the Gemara identifies each side
❓ One of the two says 'lokeh', the other 'eino lokeh' — but which is which?
↓
📖 Parallel case (Mishna Demai): two baskets of untithed produce; 'the tithe of this is in that, and that is in this' — first basket tithed, second not
↓
⚖️ Rabbi Elazar there rules: flogged, for separating second tithe before first tithe (wrong order)
↓
✅ Therefore Rabbi Elazar is the one who holds teruma-before-bikkurim is flogged; Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina holds it is not
Resolving the earlier attribution: Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina's earlier statement (3a) about 'the one who advances teruma' must then be read as referring to the exemption, not the punishment.

🔄 Two Reasons a Prohibition Might Carry No Lashes

The Gemara distinguishes two independent grounds for exemption

לָאו שֶׁאֵין בּוֹ מַעֲשֶׂה

  • A prohibition violated with no physical action at all
  • General rule: no lashes
  • Example (R' Ya'akov's view): Paschal leftovers

לָאו שֶׁנִּיתָּק לַעֲשֵׂה

  • A prohibition the Torah pairs with a later positive mitzva that rectifies it
  • General rule: no lashes (Rabbi Avin, from 'out of all your tithes you shall set apart')
  • Example: teruma before bikkurim — one must still separate the fruits afterward
Why this matters for temurah: Substitution also involves an act rectifiable by a positive mitzva ('both it and its substitute shall be holy'), yet the mishna flogs the substituter — an apparent exception that needs its own explanation.

🐐 Why Temurah Still Gets Lashes

Rav Dimi answers Abaye's challenge, then meets a counter-challenge from the case of the rapist
1️⃣ Abaye to Rav Dimi: if 'rectifiable' prohibitions carry no lashes, why is the temurah-substituter flogged? Temurah too is rectified by 'both shall be holy'!
↓
2️⃣ Rav Dimi: the verse states two prohibitions — 'he shall not exchange it... nor substitute it' — but only one positive mitzva to rectify. One positive mitzva cannot uproot two prohibitions
↓
3️⃣ Challenge from the rapist (ones): one prohibition ('he may not send her away'), one positive mitzva ('she shall be his wife') — yet a priest who divorces his victim is still flogged!
↓
4️⃣ Resolved: a priest's case is different for an unrelated reason — the Torah increased the severity of priestly prohibitions due to their greater holiness
Net result: Rectifiable prohibitions are normally lash-free, but exceptions exist for their own structural reasons — two prohibitions for one remedy (temurah), or heightened priestly sanctity (the rapist-priest).

❓ Does a Forbidden Act Still 'Take Effect'?

Abaye and Rava dispute the deeper logic behind all these lashes
Abaye
Whenever the Merciful One says 'do not do this,' if one did it anyway, the act takes effect. If it didn't take effect, why would he be flogged for accomplishing nothing?
Rava
No — the act accomplishes nothing at all. He is flogged simply because he transgressed the word of the Merciful One, regardless of whether anything actually happened.
Big idea: This dispute frames the whole tractate's puzzle: temurah is the clearest case where a forbidden act (Abaye's view) plainly does take effect — the animal becomes consecrated even though the act itself is punished.
