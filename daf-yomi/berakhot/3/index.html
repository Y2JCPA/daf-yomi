📖 Overview: Watches in Heaven and on Earth

Berakhot 3a–3b
שָׁלֹשׁ מִשְׁמָרוֹת הָוֵי הַלַּיְלָה, וְעַל כָּל מִשְׁמָר וּמִשְׁמָר יוֹשֵׁב הַקָּדוֹשׁ בָּרוּךְ הוּא וְשׁוֹאֵג כָּאֲרִי
The scene: The daf opens by tidying up the previous discussion: Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Eliezer each seem to contradict themselves about when the evening Shema begins, and the Gemara answers that two tannaim transmitted their views differently. It then asks why Rabbi Eliezer spoke of 'watches' at all instead of hours, and the answer unlocks a rich aggada: the night is divided into watches in heaven as on earth, and over each one God roars like a lion in grief over the destroyed Temple. Rabbi Yosei's encounter with Elijah in a Jerusalem ruin teaches three practical halakhot and gives that grief a voice. The Gemara then debates whether the night has three or four watches, and closes with King David, woken at midnight by his self-playing lyre and consulted by his sages at dawn.

🦁 The Three Watches and Their Signs

Rabbi Eliezer's baraita: God roars three times in Jeremiah 25:30 — one roar per watch — and each watch has an earthly sign
WatchEarthly signWhich point does the sign mark?
FirstThe donkey brays (חֲמוֹר נוֹעֵר)Its end — a sign for the start of the first watch would be pointless, since that is simply evening
SecondDogs bark (כְּלָבִים צוֹעֲקִים)The middle of the middle watch
ThirdA baby nurses from its mother and a wife converses with her husbandIts beginning — or, in the alternative answer, the end of every watch is listed
Why list the end of the last watch? If all three signs mark the ends of the watches, what use is a sign for the end of the last one, when it is already day? The Gemara answers: it helps one lying in a dark house who cannot see the dawn. When he hears a woman speaking with her husband and a baby nursing, he knows the night is over and must rise and recite Shema. Rabbi Eliezer used the language of watches at all in order to teach that there are watches in heaven just as there are watches on earth.

🏚️ Elijah at the Door of the Ruin

Rabbi Yosei prays in a ruin of Jerusalem; Elijah guards the entrance, then questions him
Elijah
Greetings to you, my rabbi. My son, why did you enter this ruin?
R' Yosei
To pray. I was afraid that travelers on the road would interrupt me.
Elijah
You should have prayed on the road — and if you feared interruption, you should have recited the abbreviated prayer (תְּפִלָּה קְצָרָה).
R' Yosei
From that exchange I learned three things: one does not enter a ruin; one may pray on the road; and one who prays on the road recites the short prayer.
Elijah
My son, what voice did you hear in that ruin?
R' Yosei
A heavenly voice cooing like a dove: אוֹי שֶׁהֶחֱרַבְתִּי אֶת בֵּיתִי — 'Woe, that I destroyed My house, burned My Temple, and exiled My children among the nations.'
Elijah
By your life, it says this not only now but three times every day. And whenever Israel enters synagogues and study halls and answers 'May His great name be blessed', the Holy One shakes His head: 'Happy is the king so praised in his house — but what is there for the father who exiled his children, and woe to the children exiled from their father's table.'
Rav's version: Rav Yitzḥak bar Shmuel in the name of Rav taught the same lament over each of the three watches: God sits and roars like a lion, saying 'Woe to Me, that I destroyed My house, burned My Temple, and exiled My children among the nations of the world.'

⚠️ Three Reasons Not to Enter a Ruin

The Sages taught three reasons; the Gemara shows that each covers a case the other two miss

Suspicion (חֲשָׁד)

  • Concern: a man alone in a ruin may be suspected of meeting a woman there
  • Needed even for a new, sturdy ruin where there is no fear of collapse
  • Two people? Normally no suspicion — but two known to be immoral remain suspect

Collapse (מַפּוֹלֶת)

  • Concern: an old ruin may fall in on those inside
  • Needed when two upstanding people enter together — there is no suspicion, and demons do not threaten a pair
  • Only the danger of collapse remains

Demons (מַזִּיקִין)

  • Concern: demons harm a person who is alone
  • Needed for a new ruin entered by two upstanding people — but only in the demons' own haunt, where even two must worry
  • Or: one person in a new ruin out in a field, where a woman is unlikely to be found and there is no collapse
Method: Every listed reason must add a case, or it would be redundant. The Gemara tests each reason by constructing the one scenario in which it alone applies.

🌙 Three Watches or Four?

Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi says four; Rabbi Natan says three. Each has a verse.
❓ Into how many watches is the night divided?
↓
Rabbi Natan: three
Judges 7:19 — Gideon reached the camp 'at the beginning of the middle watch'. 'Middle' means one before it and one after it.
Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi: four
Psalms 119:62 'At midnight I rise' and 119:148 'My eyes forestall the watches' — David rose two watches before dawn, at midnight. Only four watches make that work.
↓
🔁 Rabbi replies to Gideon: 'middle' can mean one of the two middle watches. Rabbi Natan: the verse says the middle watch, not 'one of the middle ones'.
↓
🤴 Rabbi Natan on David: he follows Rabbi Yehoshua, that kings rise at the third hour. From midnight David was awake six hours of night and two of day — two watches ahead of the other kings.
↓
✅ Rav Ashi's alternative for Rabbi Natan: even one and a half watches are called 'watches' in the plural.
Same chain of tradition: Rabbi Zerika, Rabbi Ami, and Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi also taught: before the dead one speaks only of matters concerning the dead. Rabbi Abba bar Kahana, in two versions: only words of Torah are restricted, ordinary talk is permitted — or even Torah is restricted, and all the more so ordinary talk.

🎵 David at Midnight: Did He Rise, and Did He Know?

The Gemara probes 'At midnight I rise to give thanks' from every angle
1️⃣ Objection: David rose in the evening — 'I rose with the neshef and cried' (Ps. 119:147), and Proverbs 7:9 shows that neshef means evening
↓
2️⃣ Resolutions: Rabbi Oshaya citing Rabbi Aḥa — 'midnight never passed me in sleep'; Rabbi Zeira — until midnight he dozed like a horse, then grew strong as a lion; Rav Ashi — Torah until midnight, songs and praises afterward
↓
3️⃣ But neshef also means morning (I Samuel 30:17)! Rava: there are two neshefs — the night passes and day comes, the day passes and night comes
↓
4️⃣ Did David even know when midnight was? Moses said only 'about midnight' (Ex. 11:4) — God had said 'at midnight', so Moses was uncertain; could David know better?
↓
5️⃣ David had a sign: a lyre (כִּנּוֹר) hung over his bed; at midnight the north wind played it, and he rose and studied Torah until dawn
↓
6️⃣ At dawn the Sages came: 'Your people need sustenance.' 'Sustain one another.' 'A handful does not satisfy a lion.' 'Then go out with the troops' — consulting Ahitophel, the Sanhedrin, and the Urim VeTummim (I Chron. 27:34)
Takeaway: David's midnight is anchored to a sign he could hear, not a calculation he could make — and once the night's Torah was done, the king turned immediately to his people's livelihood.
