📖 Overview: Cooked, Pressed, and Boiled

Berakhot 38a–38b
וְהִלְכְתָא ״הַמּוֹצִיא לֶחֶם מִן הָאָרֶץ״
The scene: The daf continues the chapter's tour of foods that have been transformed. A dough roasted in the ground is not bread, unless someone treats it as the basis of a meal; date honey is only 'sweat' of the fruit, but dates pressed into a cake are still dates; a barley drink is food when thick and medicine when thin, yet either way it earns a blessing because one enjoys it. Then the mishna's own word hamotzi comes under scrutiny: does it mean 'brings forth' or 'brought forth', and the halakha settles on hamotzi. The second amud takes up boiled vegetables, where the Babylonian tradition (ha'adama) faces Ulla's report of Rabbi Yoḥanan (shehakol), and Rabbi Zeira must decide which of Rabbi Yoḥanan's students transmitted him accurately.

🥣 Shetita: Medicine, Food, and the Need for a Blessing

Rav Yosef attacks Rav Ḥisda's 'thin = medicine' from the laws of Shabbat
📜 Rav Ḥisda: thick shetita is food → mezonot (Shmuel); thin shetita is medicine → shehakol (Rav)
↓
❓ Rav Yosef: a mishna says all agree one may stir shetita on Shabbat and drink Egyptian beer. If it were medicine, is medicine permitted on Shabbat?
↓
💡 Abaye: a mishna permits eating any ordinary food or drink for healing on Shabbat — because the person's intention is eating. Here too his intention is eating (another version: eating, and the cure comes by itself)
↓
❓ Then it is plainly food — why did Rav and Shmuel need to say a blessing is required?
↓
✅ Necessary: the Shabbat mishna covers one who intends to eat. Here he intends healing from the outset, and one might think medicine needs no blessing. Rav and Shmuel teach: כֵּיוָן דְּאִית לֵיהּ הֲנָאָה מִינֵּיהּ בָּעֵי בָּרוֹכֵי — since he enjoys it, he must bless

📖 Hamotzi or Motzi? A Blessing Hangs on a Tense

The Rabbis vs. Rabbi Neḥemya, as explained by Rava, and a guest who played it safe
Baraita
What does one say over bread? הַמּוֹצִיא לֶחֶם מִן הָאָרֶץ. Rabbi Neḥemya says: מוֹצִיא לֶחֶם מִן הָאָרֶץ.
Rava
All agree motzi means 'brought forth' in the past: 'God who brought them forth (מוֹצִיאָם) from Egypt' (Num. 23:22). They dispute hamotzi.
The Rabbis
Hamotzi also means 'brought forth': 'Who brought forth (הַמּוֹצִיא) for you water from the flint rock' (Deut. 8:15).
R' Neḥemya
Hamotzi means 'is bringing forth', present tense: 'I am the Lord your God who is bringing you forth (הַמּוֹצִיא) from under the burdens of Egypt' (Ex. 6:7) — unfit for a blessing about the past.
The Rabbis
That verse means: when I bring you out, I will do something so that you will know that I am the One who brought you out — past tense after all.
R' Zeira
The Sages praised the son of Rav Zevid as an expert in blessings. When he came and said motzi, Rabbi Zeira scoffed: had he said hamotzi he would have taught us the verse's meaning and that the halakha follows the Rabbis. What did motzi teach? Answer: he chose it to keep himself out of the dispute.
Halakha: וְהִלְכְתָא ״הַמּוֹצִיא לֶחֶם מִן הָאָרֶץ״ — we follow the Rabbis, for whom hamotzi also means 'brought forth'.

🥬 Boiled Vegetables (Shelakot): Three Positions

The mishna lists vegetables next to bread — does cooking change their blessing?

Babylonia: Ha'adama

  • Rabbenai in Abaye's name: vegetables are taught alongside bread — just as bread is transformed by fire, vegetables keep ha'adama after fire
  • Rav Ḥisda in the name of 'Rabbeinu' (Rav): boiled vegetables → בּוֹרֵא פְּרִי הָאֲדָמָה
  • Rav Naḥman in the name of 'Rabbeinu' (Shmuel): the same

From Eretz Yisrael: Shehakol

  • Ulla in Rabbi Yoḥanan's name: boiled vegetables → שֶׁהַכֹּל נִהְיֶה בִּדְבָרוֹ
  • Boiling changes them; they are no longer what they were
  • Rav Naḥman proposed it mirrors R' Meir vs. R' Yosei on boiled matza — the Gemara rejects this: R' Yosei only demands the taste of matza

Rav Ḥisda's Own View

  • What is ha'adama raw becomes shehakol boiled — Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak: garlic and leeks
  • What is shehakol raw becomes ha'adama boiled — cabbage, chard, pumpkin, inedible until cooked
  • Cooking can improve or damage; the blessing follows the result
Conclusion of the Gemara: Everyone agrees boiled vegetables get ha'adama; the matza dispute is about taste, not about whether boiling ruins a food.

🫒 Did Rabbi Yoḥanan Really Say Shehakol? Rabbi Zeira Weighs the Witnesses

Two students, one master, and the evidence of a lupin and a salted olive
1️⃣ Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba reports Rabbi Yoḥanan: boiled vegetables → ha'adama. Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet reports him: shehakol. Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak: Ulla fixed his error by following Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Zeira: what is Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet doing beside Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba? The latter was meticulous in learning from his teacher; the former was not
↓
3️⃣ Furthermore, Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba reviewed his learning before Rabbi Yoḥanan every thirty days; Rabbi Binyamin bar Yefet did not
↓
4️⃣ Furthermore, a lupin boiled seven times and eaten as dessert — they asked Rabbi Yoḥanan, and he said ha'adama
↓
5️⃣ Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba adds: I saw Rabbi Yoḥanan eat a salted olive and bless before and after. If cooked food stays itself: ha'etz before, me'ein shalosh after. If not: shehakol before — but what after? Rejected: perhaps he said borei nefashot
↓
6️⃣ Rabbi Yitzḥak bar Shmuel objects: for bitter herbs on Passover one may use the vegetables and stalks but not pickled, boiled or cooked ones! Answer: there we need the taste of maror, and it is lacking
Cliffhanger: Rabbi Yirmeya asks Rabbi Zeira how Rabbi Yoḥanan could bless after a salted olive at all, since the pit was removed and not eaten — the question continues on the next daf.
