📖 Overview: Names, Languages, and Where Intent Ends

Berakhot 13a–13b
שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל ה׳ אֱלֹהֵינוּ ה׳ אֶחָד — עַד כָּאן צְרִיכָה כַּוָּונַת הַלֵּב, דִּבְרֵי רַבִּי מֵאִיר
The scene: The first chapter closes by finishing the Sages' answer to Ben Zoma: 'Israel' becomes primary and 'Jacob' secondary, just as later troubles make earlier ones forgotten. That leads to the renaming of Abram and Sarai and whether calling Abraham 'Abram' is a transgression. The second chapter opens with a mishna on reading Shema with intent, greeting people between and within paragraphs, and why the three portions are ordered as they are. The Gemara asks whether mitzvot require intent, records the dispute over reciting Shema in Hebrew or any language, and then works through a sequence of baraitot about how much of Shema needs intent — concluding with Rabbi Meir's view, endorsed by Rava, that the first verse is what matters. Stories about Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's one-verse Shema and about falling asleep after it show that principle in practice.

📛 Three Renamings, Three Different Rules

The Gemara moves from 'Israel is primary, Jacob secondary' to the names of Abraham and Sarah
PersonMeaning of the changeUsing the old name
Abram → AbrahamFirst father (אָב) of Aram only; finally father of the whole world — 'father of a multitude of nations' (Gen. 17:5)Bar Kappara: transgresses a positive mitzva ('your name will be Abraham'); Rabbi Eliezer: a negative one ('shall no longer be called Abram')
Sarai → SarahFirst princess of her own nation; finally princess of the whole worldNo transgression — the command 'do not call her Sarai' (Gen. 17:15) was said to Abraham alone, not as a general rule
Jacob → IsraelIsrael primary, Jacob secondary — 'Do not remember the former events' (Isa. 43:18): the kingdoms' subjugation eclipses even the ExodusPermitted — Scripture itself reverts: 'And God said to Israel… Jacob, Jacob' (Gen. 46:2)
Objection and answer: Rabbi Yosei bar Avin: Nehemiah 9:7 says 'Who chose Abram'! Answer: there the prophet is recounting God's praises from the beginning — what the situation was originally, before the name was changed. Rav Yosef adds that 'Behold, I do a new thing' is the war of Gog and Magog, like the traveler who forgot the wolf and the lion once the snake had come.

🗣️ Shema in Hebrew Only, or in Any Language?

Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi and the Rabbis each have a verse — and each must explain the other's
Rabbi
Shema must be recited as it is written (כִּכְתָבָהּ). 'And these words… shall be' (Deut. 6:6) — בַּהֲוָיָיתָן יְהוּ, as they are they shall remain.
Rabbis
In any language. 'Hear (שְׁמַע), Israel' — in any language that you hear and understand.
Gemara
Then what does Rabbi do with 'Hear'? — Let your ears hear what your mouth utters: recite audibly. And the Rabbis? They follow the view that one who did not make it audible still fulfilled his obligation.
Gemara
And what do the Rabbis do with 'shall be'? — That one may not recite Shema out of order (לְמַפְרֵעַ). Rabbi learns that from 'the words' rather than 'words'; the Rabbis do not expound that extra letter.
Gemara
Does this mean Rabbi holds the whole Torah may be read in any language, so a special verse was needed for Shema? Not necessarily — 'shall be' was needed to counter 'Hear'. And the Rabbis, that the whole Torah must be Hebrew? Not necessarily — 'Hear' was needed to counter 'shall be'.
Note: Each side's verse is 'necessary' only because the other side's verse exists, so neither view can be extended to Torah reading in general.

🎯 How Much of Shema Requires Intent?

From 'mitzvot require intent' to Rabbi Meir's first verse
❓ Mishna: one reading the Torah when the time for Shema arrives fulfils it 'if he focused his heart'. Does this prove mitzvot require intent?
↓
🚫 No — 'focused his heart' may mean intent to read. But he is already reading! — He is reading to proofread the text (לְהַגִּיהַּ), so intent to read the words is all that is needed
↓
📜 Baraita: 'These words… upon your heart' — 'these' limits intent to the first paragraph (Rabbi Eliezer). Rabbi Akiva: 'which I command you this day, upon your heart' — the whole paragraph requires intent. Rabbi Yoḥanan: halakha like Rabbi Akiva
↓
Rav Zutra
First paragraph: intent and recitation; from there on, recitation without intent
Rabbi Yoshiya
First paragraph: recitation and intent; from there on, intent without recitation ('to speak of them' there refers to teaching Torah)
R' Aḥa (R' Yehuda)
Once he focused his heart in the first paragraph, no more is needed — Rabbi Yoḥanan rules like him
↓
✅ Rabbi Meir: 'Hear, Israel, the Lord is our God, the Lord is One' — intent of the heart is required to here. Rava: the halakha follows Rabbi Meir
Related: Rav Yehuda: recite until 'upon your heart' standing in one place; Rabbi Yoḥanan: the whole first paragraph — consistent with his ruling like Rabbi Aḥa. Rabbi Yitzḥak uses 'and you shall place these words' for tefillin of the arm opposite the heart.

😴 The First Verse in Practice

Stories and rulings that turn Rabbi Meir's principle into daily conduct
1️⃣ Sumakhos: whoever lengthens Eḥad has his days and years lengthened. Rav Aḥa bar Ya'akov: in the dalet. Rav Ashi: provided he does not rush the ḥet
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Yirmeya saw Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba lengthening greatly: 'Once you have crowned Him above, below and in the four directions, you need no more'
↓
3️⃣ 'Shema Yisrael…' — this was Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's Shema. Rav never saw him accept the yoke of Heaven; Rabbi Ḥiyya: when he passes his hands over his face in the lesson, that is his Shema
↓
4️⃣ Did Rabbi complete it later? Bar Kappara: no — that is why he sought a lesson mentioning the Exodus. Rabbi Shimon his son: yes — and he still mentioned the Exodus 'at its time'
↓
5️⃣ Rav: said the first verse and was overcome by sleep — he has fulfilled his obligation. Rav Naḥman to his servant Daru: 'for the first verse trouble me; beyond that, do not'. Rabba likewise strove only for the first verse
↓
6️⃣ Rav Yosef: one lying flat on his back (פְּרַקְדָּן) may not recite Shema. Sleeping so, leaning slightly, is allowed; reciting, even leaning, is not — Rabbi Yoḥanan did so only because he was corpulent
Takeaway: The verse of God's unity is the core of accepting Heaven's kingship; the rest is recitation whose lapse does not undo the mitzva.

🤝 Greeting Someone in the Middle of Shema

The mishna's two positions, and the Gemara's repair of its wording

Rabbi Meir

  • Between paragraphs: greet out of respect (כָּבוֹד) — and needless to say, respond
  • Within a paragraph: greet out of fear (יִרְאָה) — and needless to say, respond
  • As written, 'respond' seemed redundant, and the two views collapsed into one; the Gemara says the mishna is incomplete (חַסּוֹרֵי מְחַסְּרָא) and restores it

Rabbi Yehuda

  • Within a paragraph: greet out of fear, respond out of respect
  • Between paragraphs: greet out of respect, respond to any person
  • Also: between VaYomer and Emet VeYatziv one may not interrupt at all
The breaks: Between the first and second blessing; the second blessing and Shema; Shema and VeHaya im Shamoa; VeHaya im Shamoa and VaYomer; VaYomer and Emet VeYatziv. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa: Shema comes first to accept the yoke of Heaven before the yoke of mitzvot; VeHaya im Shamoa precedes VaYomer because it applies day and night, while tzitzit applies only by day.
