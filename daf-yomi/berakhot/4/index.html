📖 Overview: David's Piety and the Rabbis' Fence

Berakhot 4a–4b
אֵיזֶהוּ בֶּן הָעוֹלָם הַבָּא — זֶה הַסּוֹמֵךְ גְּאוּלָּה לִתְפִלָּה שֶׁל עַרְבִית
The scene: The daf finishes the aggada of King David's war council, then revisits whether Moses and David knew the exact moment of midnight. It explores David's self-description as 'pious': rising while other kings sleep, dirtying his hands to permit wives to their husbands, and admitting error before his teacher Mefivoshet — while the dotted word 'luleh' shows he still feared that sin might forfeit his reward. The Gemara then returns to the mishna: the Rabbis' 'until midnight' is a protective fence, illustrated by the tired man coming home from the field. That baraita launches a dispute between Rabbi Yoḥanan and Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi over whether the evening Shema precedes the Amida, and the daf closes with the merit of Ashrei, the flights of the angels, and Shema upon one's bed.

🎼 Who Knew When Midnight Was?

Three approaches to Moses' 'about midnight' (Ex. 11:4) and David's lyre
ViewMosesDavid

Rav AshiSpoke at midnight of the 13th: 'tomorrow, like midnight now (כַּחֲצוֹת), I will go out' — a comparison, not an approximationNot addressed
The Master's rule: Accustom your tongue to say 'I do not know' (לַמֵּד לְשׁוֹנְךָ לוֹמַר אֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ), lest you become entangled in deceit. The daf also completes the council of war: Benayahu ben Yehoyada is the Sanhedrin — called Kereti because decisive and Peleti because wondrous in its rulings — and Evyatar is the Urim VeTummim. They consulted Ahitophel, then the Sanhedrin, then the Urim VeTummim, and only then Yoav the general.

🙏 'Keep My Soul, for I Am Pious'

Levi and Rabbi Yitzḥak explain David's claim in Psalm 86; the Gemara tests it against the dotted luleh
One sage
David said: all the kings of East and West sleep until the third hour — but I, 'at midnight I rise to give thanks' (Ps. 119:62).
The other
Kings sit in honored groups — but my hands are soiled with blood, miscarried fetus, and placenta to render a woman pure for her husband. And I consult my teacher Mefivoshet on every ruling: 'Did I judge well? Convict well? Acquit well? Declare pure and impure well?' — and I am not ashamed.
Gemara
The verse: 'I speak Your testimonies before kings and am not ashamed' (Ps. 119:46). His name was really Ish Boshet, called Mefivoshet because he 'embarrassed' David in halakha. Because David accepted correction, he merited a son Kilav — really Daniel — who embarrassed Mefivoshet in turn (Prov. 23:15, 27:11).
Objection
Can David call himself pious? 'If only [luleh] I had believed to see the goodness of the Lord in the land of the living' (Ps. 27:13) is dotted — David doubted whether he had a share among the righteous!
Answer
He knew he was pious; he feared lest sin cause him to lose it (שֶׁמָּא יִגְרוֹם הַחֵטְא).
Proof
Rabbi Ya'akov bar Idi: God promised Jacob 'I will guard you wherever you go', yet 'Jacob was very afraid' of Esau — lest sin cause. And 'until Your people cross... until the people You acquired cross' (Ex. 15:16): Israel deserved a miracle in Ezra's day like Joshua's, but sin caused its absence.
Big idea: Piety, in David's mouth, is forgoing royal dignity for Torah and for other people — while never assuming the reward is guaranteed.

🛡️ Why 'Until Midnight'? The Rabbis' Fence

Whose reading of 'when you lie down' do the Rabbis of the mishna follow?
❓ If the Rabbis read 'when you lie down' like Rabbi Eliezer, say 'until the end of the first watch'; if like Rabban Gamliel, say 'until dawn'. Why midnight?
↓
💡 They agree with Rabban Gamliel — by Torah law, until dawn. 'Until midnight' is a fence to distance a person from transgression (לְהַרְחִיק אֶת הָאָדָם מִן הָעֲבֵירָה)
↓
Without the fence
A man comes home from the field: 'I'll eat a little, drink a little, sleep a little, then recite Shema and pray' — sleep overtakes him and he sleeps all night.
With the fence
He goes straight to the synagogue: if he reads Scripture, he reads; if he learns Mishna, he learns; then Shema and prayer; then home to eat and bless.
↓
❓ The baraita adds: 'whoever transgresses the words of the Sages is liable to death.' Why say that here of all places?
↓
Two answers: (1) the compulsion of sleep (אוֹנֶס שֵׁינָה) makes extra vigilance necessary; (2) to exclude the view that the evening prayer is optional — it teaches that it is obligatory
Support: The baraita's order — 'recites Shema and prays' — supports Rabbi Yoḥanan: at night too, Shema comes before the Amida.

🔗 Redemption Beside Prayer: Two Amoraim, Two Orders

Rabbi Yoḥanan: one who juxtaposes redemption to the evening prayer is assured a place in the World-to-Come

Rabbi Yoḥanan

  • Order at night: Shema, blessing of redemption, then the Amida
  • Logic: the redemption from Egypt began already in the evening, though it was completed only in the morning
  • Verse: 'lying down' is likened to 'rising' — just as in the morning Shema is followed by prayer, so at night

Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi

  • The prayers were instituted between the two Shemas: morning Shema, then the prayers, then the evening Shema
  • Logic: the evening redemption was not the full redemption, so there is no need to join it to prayer
  • Verse: just as the morning Shema is next to rising from bed, the evening Shema is next to lying down in bed

Objection and answer

  • Mar son of Ravina: at night there are two blessings after Shema — Hashkivenu interrupts between redemption and prayer!
  • Answer: since the Sages instituted Hashkivenu, it counts as one extended redemption (גְּאוּלָּה אֲרִיכְתָּא)
  • Just as 'Lord, open my lips' (Ps. 51:17), said before every Amida, counts as extended prayer rather than an interruption
Result: The dispute can rest on logic (when did the redemption begin?) or on how to read 'when you lie down and when you rise'; either way, additions the Sages instituted are treated as part of the original blessing.

📜 Why Ashrei Three Times a Day?

Rabbi Elazar in the name of Rabbi Avina: one who recites 'A Psalm of David' (Ps. 145) three times daily is assured the World-to-Come
1️⃣ Is it because the psalm runs through the alphabet? Then say Psalm 119, whose acrostic appears eight times over
↓
2️⃣ Is it because of 'You open Your hand and satisfy every living thing' (145:16)? Then say the Great Hallel: 'Who gives bread to all flesh' (136:25)
↓
3️⃣ Rather — because it has both: the acrostic and the praise of God's sustenance of all creation
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Yoḥanan: why is there no verse beginning with nun? Because of 'Fallen [nafla], she shall rise no more, the virgin of Israel' (Amos 5:2)
↓
5️⃣ In the West they read it softly: 'She has fallen, but shall fall no more — rise, virgin of Israel!' Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak: even so, David supported the fallen in the very next verse — 'The Lord upholds all who fall' (samekh)
Also on the daf: Rabbi Elazar bar Avina: Michael arrives in one flight ('one of the seraphim flew to me'), Gabriel in two ('caused to fly swiftly'), Elijah in four, the Angel of Death in eight — and in a plague, one. And Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: even after Shema in the synagogue, it is a mitzva to recite it on one's bed — 'say to your heart upon your bed and be still' (Ps. 4:5).
