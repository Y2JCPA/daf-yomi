📖 Overview: Modesty, Health, and Reverence

Berakhot 62a–62b
תּוֹרָה הִיא, וְלִלְמוֹד אֲנִי צָרִיךְ
The scene: The daf collects the Sages' teachings on private conduct, beginning with three startling stories of students who followed their teachers in order to learn even the most intimate behavior, each defending himself with the same line: it is Torah, and I must learn. It then explains why one wipes with the left hand and not the right, catalogues the dangers and protections of the bathroom, and debates how far one must distance himself in an open field. Health maxims follow — Ben Azzai's advice to relieve oneself morning and evening, Shmuel on the value of a bowel movement at dawn, and Bar Kappara's sayings sold for dinars. The theme of modesty then turns biblical: Saul's modesty in the cave saved him from David, and David's own words brought on the census and its plague. The daf ends on the mishna's rules of reverence for the Temple Mount — no staff, no shoes, no shortcut, and certainly no spitting.

📚 'It Is Torah, and I Must Learn'

Three baraitot and stories with one refrain
1️⃣ Rabbi Akiva: I once entered the bathroom after my teacher Rabbi Yehoshua and learned three things — one does not face east and west but north and south; one does not uncover himself standing but sitting; and one does not wipe with the right hand but with the left
↓
2️⃣ Ben Azzai said to him: You were impertinent to your teacher to that extent? He replied: תּוֹרָה הִיא, וְלִלְמוֹד אֲנִי צָרִיךְ — it is Torah, and I must learn
↓
3️⃣ Ben Azzai then said the same of himself: I once entered after Rabbi Akiva and learned those same three things. Rabbi Yehuda challenged him, and he gave the identical answer
↓
4️⃣ Rav Kahana lay beneath Rav's bed and heard him chatting and laughing with his wife. He remarked that Rav's mouth was like one who had never tasted a cooked dish
↓
5️⃣ Rav said: Kahana, you are here? Leave — this is not a desirable mode of behavior. Rav Kahana answered him too: it is Torah, and I must learn
The claim: Conduct itself is Torah. The students are not curious; they hold that a teacher's ordinary behavior carries halakha that cannot be learned any other way.

✋ Why Not the Right Hand?

Four amoraim, and the tannaitic dispute the Gemara says they parallel
SageReasonSupport
RavaThe Torah was given with the right hand'At His right hand was a fiery law unto them' (Deuteronomy 33:2)
Rabba bar bar ḤanaThe right hand is close to the mouth — one eats with it—
Rabbi Shimon ben LakishOne ties the phylacteries onto the left hand with the right—
Rav Naḥman bar YitzḥakOne points to the cantillation notes of the Torah with it—
Tannaim: R' Eliezer / R' Yehoshua / R' AkivaBecause he eats with it / because he writes with it / because he points to the notes of the Torah with itThe Gemara notes the amoraic reasons run parallel to this dispute
Context: This is the third of the three things Rabbi Akiva and Ben Azzai each said they learned by observing their teachers — the Gemara now asks for its reason.

🐍 Modesty, Demons, and Distance

What protects a person, and how far he must go
קַבָּלָה דְּבֵית הַכִּסֵּא — צְנִיעוּתָא וּשְׁתִיקוּתָא
Protection: Rabbi Tanḥum bar Ḥanilai: anyone modest in the bathroom is saved from three things — snakes, scorpions and demons; some say his dreams will also be settling for him.
Tiberias: in one bathroom there, two who entered together were harmed even by day, yet Rabbi Ami and Rabbi Asi entering alone were not. Asked whether they were afraid, they answered: we have a tradition — the tradition of the bathroom is modesty and silence; the tradition for suffering is silence and prayer.
Precautions: Abaye's mother raised a lamb to accompany him (not a goat, since sa'ir could be confused with a goat-demon). Rava's wife, the daughter of Rav Ḥisda, would rattle a nut in a copper vessel for him; once he became head of the yeshiva she built him a window and rested her hand on his head.

👑 Saul's Modesty and David's Two Punishments

From the cave at Ein Gedi to the census and the plague
🕳️ Saul enters the cave 'to cover his feet' (I Samuel 24:3). A baraita: there was a fence within a fence and a cave within a cave; Rabbi Elazar adds that he covered himself with his garment like a sukka
↓
🗡️ David tells Saul: by Torah law you deserve to be killed, since you are a pursuer — but the modesty that was in you spared you
↓
The robe
David cut the corner of Saul's robe. Rabbi Yosei son of Rabbi Ḥanina: whoever treats clothing with contempt will not benefit from it — 'they covered him with clothes, but he could get no heat' (I Kings 1:1)
The word
David said 'if the Lord has incited you against me' (I Samuel 26:19). Rabbi Elazar: God replied, Do you call Me an inciter? I will make you stumble in what even schoolchildren know
↓
🧮 Satan incited David to count Israel, and he took no ransom money as Exodus 30:12 requires — so a plague came 'from the morning until the appointed time'. Shmuel the elder in Rabbi Ḥanina's name: from the slaughter of the daily offering until the sprinkling of its blood; Rabbi Yoḥanan: precisely until noon
↓
🕊️ 'Take for Me a great one [rav] among them' — Avishai ben Tzeruya, equal to the majority of the Sanhedrin, died and atoned for the nation. And what did God 'behold' before relenting? Rav: Jacob. Shmuel: Isaac's ashes. Rabbi Yitzḥak Nappaḥa: the atonement money. Rabbi Yoḥanan: the Temple — and the Gemara concludes it stands to reason that He saw the Temple
Rabbi Elazar and the Roman: Earlier on the daf, a Roman pushed Rabbi Elazar out of a bathroom; a serpent came and tore out the Roman's intestines. Rabbi Elazar applied to him 'I will give man [adam] for you' (Isaiah 43:4) — do not read adam but Edom.

🚫 May One Spit in a Synagogue?

Rava's analogy to shoes, and the challenge from the shortcut
Mishna
One may not enter the Temple Mount with his staff or his shoes, nor make it a kappandarya, a shortcut — and spitting is forbidden by an a fortiori inference.
Rava
Kappandarya means just what it sounds like: a shortcut. Rav Ḥana bar Adda read it as an acrostic of what people say: 'instead of going around the rows of houses, I will enter through this one.'
R' Yehoshua b. Levi
Whoever spits on the Temple Mount, even today, is as though he spat in the pupil of God's eye — 'and My eyes and My heart shall be there perpetually' (I Kings 9:3).
Rava
But spitting in a synagogue is permitted, just like shoes: shoes are forbidden on the Temple Mount and permitted in a synagogue, so spitting too.
Challenge
Rav Pappa — some say Ravina, some say Rav Adda bar Mattana — asked: instead of deriving it from the shoe, derive it from the shortcut, which is forbidden in a synagogue!
Rava
The tanna himself derived spitting from the shoe: if a shoe, which is not contemptuous, was forbidden at holy ground — 'put off your shoes from off your feet' (Exodus 3:5) — then spitting, which is contemptuous, all the more so. Rabbi Yosei bar Yehuda derives it instead from 'none may enter the king's gate clothed with sackcloth' (Esther 4:2).
Synagogue shortcuts: Rav Naḥman in Rabba bar Avuh's name: one who entered a synagogue not intending to use it as a shortcut may then do so. Rabbi Abbahu: if a path was there originally, it is permitted. Rabbi Ḥelbo in Rav Huna's name: one who enters to pray may leave through the other side, as in Ezekiel 46:9.
