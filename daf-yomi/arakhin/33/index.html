📖 Overview: Courtyard Houses and the Levites' Special Standing

Arakhin 33a–33b
בָּתֵּי הַחֲצֵרִים נוֹתְנִין לָהֶם כֹּחַ יָפֶה שֶׁבְּבָתֵּי עָרֵי חוֹמָה וְכֹחַ יָפֶה שֶׁבַּשָּׂדוֹת
The scene: The daf closes out the historical digression about the land's sanctity and turns to a third category of dwelling: houses in unwalled courtyards (villages too small to count as true walled cities), which combine advantageous rules from both fields and walled-city houses. The Gemara works out exactly how those rules combine, especially for a consecrated courtyard-house redeemed by someone other than the owner. It then defines precisely which places qualify as 'unwalled courtyards,' before turning to the Levites' unique, permanent right to redeem their houses and fields at any time — a right so strong the Gemara must work out exactly which of a Levite's relatives can inherit it.

🏡 New Mishna: The Best of Both Worlds

Houses in unwalled courtyards borrow rules from two other categories
Rule borrowed from...Applied to courtyard houses
Walled-city housesMay be redeemed immediately and throughout the first twelve months
Ancestral fieldsLeave the buyer's possession at the Jubilee Year, OR by a per-annum deduction from the price
NOT from fieldsUnlike fields, no two-year minimum wait before redemption — derived from 'redemption shall be his,' immediately
NOT from walled-city housesUnlike walled-city houses, never become the buyer's permanently after a year — they always return by the Jubilee
Source: Leviticus 25:31 explicitly juxtaposes unwalled-courtyard houses to 'the fields of the country' — inheriting the field's Jubilee-return rule while keeping the walled-house's immediate redeemability.

🔙 A Consecrated Courtyard-House: Does It Ever Come Home?

Rav Huna and Rabbi Oshaya disagree about what happens when someone ELSE redeems it from hekdesh
Rav Huna
The phrase 'and it shall go out in the Jubilee' is specifically needed for: an owner consecrates his courtyard-house, ANOTHER person redeems it from the Temple treasury, and the Jubilee arrives. Result: it returns to the ORIGINAL owner — neither locked to the redeemer (like a walled house) nor lost to the priests (like a field).
R' Oshaya
Not so — 'he gives the money and it stands assured to him' (Lev. 27:19) means whoever redeems consecrated property owns it fully and finally, in every category.
Rav Zeira's challenge
Why require that ANOTHER redeemed it first? Shouldn't an unredeemed courtyard-house simply return to the owner at the Jubilee regardless?
Abaye
No — an utterly unredeemed house never leaves hekdesh 'for free'; that would let people say consecrated property exits without any redemption at all.
The verdict: A later baraita is found stating Rav Huna's position explicitly, refuting Rabbi Oshaya.

📜 What Counts as 'Houses of Unwalled Courtyards'?

New mishna: a small settlement can qualify — even behind an ancient wall
1️⃣ Minimum size: two courtyards, each containing two houses — derived from the plural 'houses' and plural 'courtyards' in Lev. 25:31
↓
2️⃣ Surprisingly: even a place that IS surrounded by a wall from Joshua's era still counts as 'unwalled' if it's this small — the verse's 'which have no wall' teaches that size can override an actual wall
↓
3️⃣ Why not just 'a house and a courtyard' (one each)? The Torah could have written 'courtyards' alone if a single house sufficed — 'houses' in the plural is deliberate

🕎 A Levite's Perpetual Right of Redemption

New mishna: even inheritance across tribal lines has its own rules
❓ An Israelite inherits a walled-city house from his LEVITE grandfather — does he redeem it 'always' like a Levite, or only within the standard one year?
↓
📜 Rebbi: the perpetual right requires BOTH conditions — the seller must be a Levite AND the house must be in a Levite city (Lev. 25:32-33)
↓
Rebbi's ruling
This Israelite grandson redeems only within one year, like any Israelite — he lacks the 'Levite' half of the requirement
The Rabbis' ruling
Any inheritor of a Levite-city house redeems it perpetually, Levite or not — location alone suffices
↓
⚠️ Excluded even under the lenient reading: a Levite who is a mamzer or Gibeonite-born — disqualified from the community, so not 'a Levite' for this purpose

🏛️ Levite Cities: Fields, Lots, and Open Land

A different mishna protects the Levites' surrounding open land
גְּאוּלַּת עוֹלָם תִּהְיֶה לַלְוִיִּם
The rule: In Levite cities, one may not convert a field into an open lot (migrash), an open lot into a field, part of the city into open land, or open land into city — the boundaries are frozen. Rabbi Elazar limits this rigidity to Levite cities specifically; in ordinary Israelite cities, one MAY convert a field into a lot or a lot into part of the city (just not the reverse), precisely so that Israelite cities can keep growing rather than shrinking. Priests and Levites, meanwhile, may sell and redeem their fields and houses always — 'the Levites shall have a perpetual right of redemption' (Lev. 25:32) — overriding the two-year field rule, the Jubilee-forfeiture-to-priests rule, and the one-year walled-house rule, all at once.
