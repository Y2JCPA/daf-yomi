📖 Overview: Coercion, Consent, and Closing a Chapter

Arakhin 21a–21b
חַיָּיבֵי עֲרָכִין מְמַשְׁכְּנִין אוֹתָן, חַיָּיבֵי חַטָּאוֹת וַאֲשָׁמוֹת אֵין מְמַשְׁכְּנִין אוֹתָן
The scene: The daf finishes the discussion of consecrating a rented house, then turns to a new mishna: when may the court repossess a debtor's property to satisfy his Temple obligations? Valuations are always collected; sin and guilt offerings never are, since the sinner needs atonement badly enough to bring them himself; burnt and peace offerings are collected, since nothing compels a person to bring them promptly. The Gemara qualifies this with exceptions and explores how a person can be 'coerced' into an offering — or a divorce — 'until he says: I want to.' It closes the chapter with a mishna opening a new topic: the public proclamation period before selling orphans' or Temple-consecrated land, and why the announcement is made both morning and evening.

🏠 Consecrating a Rented House

Only an owner can consecrate — so how does the baraita let a renter trigger rent payments to the Temple?
❓ Baraita: 'if the one living in it consecrates it, he pays rent to the Temple.' But a renter isn't the owner!
↓
📜 Leviticus 27:14, 'when a man shall sanctify his house' — just as a house is in one's possession, so must be anything he consecrates
↓
💡 Resolution: read the baraita as 'if the landlord consecrates it, the renter (still living there) pays the rent to the Temple treasury'
↓
🤔 But then the renter is misusing consecrated property (me'ila) by living there — and once he misuses it, doesn't the rent payment leave sacred status immediately?
↓
✅ The landlord said: 'when the rent comes to me, let it be consecrated' — future proceeds, not the house itself. Rav Yehuda/Rav: this follows Rabbi Meir, who allows consecrating something 'not yet in the world'

💰 New Mishna: When Does the Court Repossess Property?

Not every unpaid Temple obligation is treated the same
ObligationCourt repossesses?Reasoning
Valuations (arakhin)YesA fixed debt to the Temple treasury
Sin offerings & guilt offerings (ḥata'ot, ashamot)No — usuallyNeeded for atonement, so the sinner brings them without delay
Burnt offerings & peace offerings (olot, shelamim)YesNot required for atonement, so one might delay bringing them
Nazirite's sin offeringYes (exception)Once his burnt/peace offering is brought he may already drink wine — risk of negligence about the sin offering
Woman after childbirth's / leper's burnt offeringNo (exception)Each is a prerequisite the person needs before eating sacrificial food or completing purification — no risk of delay

🙋 When Must the Beneficiary Consent?

Shmuel and Ulla on offerings brought by someone else on your behalf
Shmuel
A burnt offering requires the beneficiary's consent (Lev. 1:3, 'of his volition') — even when another person separates the animal for him, at the time of separation.
Objection
A baraita: for sin/guilt offerings, consent matters only if given knowingly; for burnt/peace offerings, it works out even without consent!
Shmuel
That baraita concerns the moment of atonement (the actual sacrifice) — consent was already given earlier, at separation. That's where I require it.
Ulla
I hold the opposite emphasis: sin/guilt offerings need consent at separation; burnt/peace offerings need it only at atonement — for both pairs, atonement-stage consent is universally required.
Rav Pappa
The conflicting baraitot aren't really contradictory — one addresses the separation stage, the other the atonement stage; and Shmuel and Ulla each read the same texts in mirror-image ways.

🤝 Coerced 'Until He Says: I Want To'

The mishna's principle applies equally to sacrifices and to divorce
1️⃣ Mishna: though atonement requires bringing an offering 'of his own volition' (Lev. 1:3), the court coerces him until he says 'I want to'
↓
2️⃣ Baraita: 'he shall sacrifice it' teaches coercion is allowed; 'of his volition' teaches it cannot be entirely forced — reconciled by coercing him until he consents verbally
↓
3️⃣ 'And so too with bills of divorce' — the court may coerce a husband obligated by the Sages to divorce, until he says 'I want to'
↓
4️⃣ Rav Sheshet: if a husband first files a declaration (moda'a) that any coerced get is invalid, that declaration stands — unless he later explicitly retracts it, mere compliance is not enough

📢 Closing the Chapter: Proclaiming a Sale

New mishna opens the topic that dominates the next several dapim
שׁוּם הַיְּתוֹמִים שְׁלֹשִׁים יוֹם, וְשׁוּם הַהֶקְדֵּשׁ שִׁשִּׁים יוֹם, וּמַכְרִיזִין בַּבֹּקֶר וּבָעֶרֶב
The rule: The court proclaims the sale of orphans' inherited land for thirty days, and the sale of consecrated Temple property for sixty days — announcing it both morning and evening. The Gemara (Rav Yehuda, citing Rav) explains the timing: morning, when laborers head out to the fields, so an interested buyer can ask them to inspect the land for him; and evening, when laborers return to the city, reminding that same buyer to go ask them what they found.
