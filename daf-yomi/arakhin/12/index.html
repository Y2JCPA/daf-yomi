📖 Overview: Song, and the Date of Destruction

Arakhin 12a–12b
כְּשֶׁחָרַב הַבַּיִת בָּרִאשׁוֹנָה, אוֹתוֹ הַיּוֹם תִּשְׁעָה בְּאָב הָיָה
The scene: Continuing yesterday's question about the Levites' song, the Gemara asks whether wine libations poured on the altar without an accompanying animal offering require song, testing the question against the mournful verse the Levites sang as the Temple fell — ultimately rejected as no real proof, since that verse wasn't even the psalm assigned to that day. The Gemara then works through the baraita's precise dating of the First Temple's destruction — the ninth of Av, a Sunday, the year after a Sabbatical year, during the priestly watch of Yehoyariv — anchoring the calculation to a dated prophecy of Ezekiel and to disputed counts of Nebuchadnezzar's exiles. It tests whether the same 'year after Shmita' holds for the Second Temple's destruction 420 years later, a calculation that only works under Rabbi Yehuda's view of how Jubilee years are counted, before an alternative answer notes that the priestly watch of Yehoyariv could not literally have existed by the time of the Second Temple at all.

🍷 Do Standalone Wine Libations Require Song?

A dilemma left over from yesterday's discussion
❓ Wine libations poured on the altar WITHOUT an accompanying animal offering — do they require the Levites' song?
↓
💭 Attempted proof: as the Temple fell, the Levites sang — yet no obligatory OR voluntary burnt offering was being sacrificed that day. Perhaps it was sung for libations alone?
↓
Rava/Rav Ashi reject this
The verse they sang belongs to Wednesday's psalm, not Sunday's — a spontaneous lament, not an actual accompanying song
Reish Lakish's fallback
Levites may sing on the platform with NO offering at all — so 'standing on their platform' proves nothing either way
↓
⚠️ If song is optional without any offering, why not allow it for libations too? Answer: doing so risks people assuming song is optional even for a full offering — the question remains open

📅 Dating the First Temple's Destruction

Four converging details, all anchored to a dated prophecy of Ezekiel
DetailValue
Day of the monthThe 9th of Av
Day of the weekMotzei Shabbat (Saturday night)
Position in the Shmita cycleThe year after a Sabbatical year
Priestly watch on dutyThe watch of Yehoyariv
The anchor verse: Ezekiel 40:1 dates a prophecy '25 years into the exile...at the beginning of the year, on the tenth of the month, 14 years after the city was struck' — read as pinpointing a Jubilee year, since a Jubilee begins on the tenth of Tishrei, Yom Kippur.

🔢 420 Years — Did the Second Temple Fall the Same Way?

Testing whether the Second Temple's destruction also lands in the year after Shmita
1️⃣ The Second Temple stood 420 years; the Gemara checks whether this also lands in the year after a Sabbatical year
↓
2️⃣ A straightforward count (8 Jubilees of 50 years = 400, plus 2 more Shmita cycles = 14, plus 6 remaining) puts the destruction IN a Shmita year, not after it
↓
3️⃣ Under Rabbi Yehuda's view — that a Jubilee year doubles as year one of the NEXT cycle, so each cycle is only 49 years — the extra years shift the total to land in the year AFTER Shmita, matching the baraita
↓
4️⃣ But applying that same math to the FIRST Temple throws its date off — resolved only by excluding the years of the Ten Tribes' exile under Sennacherib from the Jubilee count, since a Jubilee requires all twelve tribes in their land

🎭 Or Maybe the Details Simply Don't All Match

A simpler alternative resolution
A simpler answer: Perhaps the baraita's phrase 'and likewise for the second [Temple]' never claimed an identical Shmita-year timing — only the same day of the week and month, the 9th of Av on a Sunday. This must be the more limited reading regardless, since the baraita's detail that the priestly watch of Yehoyariv was on duty literally cannot apply to the Second Temple: only four families of priests returned from the Babylonian exile, and Yehoyariv's original watch, from before the exile, no longer existed by then.

⛓️ Rebuilding the 24 Priestly Watches After the Exile

How four returning families became twenty-four again
1️⃣ Only four of the original twenty-four priestly families returned from Babylonia: Yedaya, Ḥarim, Pashḥur, and Immer
↓
2️⃣ The prophets among the returnees divided these four families into twenty-four new watches, to restore the original structure
↓
3️⃣ Slips bearing the twenty-four new watch-names were mixed together and placed in a lottery receptacle (קַלְפִּי)
↓
4️⃣ Yedaya's representative drew first, receiving his own watch's portion plus five more — six watches in total for his family, and so on for the others
