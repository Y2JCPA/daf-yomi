📖 Overview: Valuing and Redeeming an Ancestral Field

Arakhin 25a–25b
הַמַּקְדִּישׁ שָׂדֵהוּ בִּשְׁעַת הַיּוֹבֵל, נוֹתֵן בְּזֶרַע חוֹמֶר שְׂעוֹרִים חֲמִשִּׁים שֶׁקֶל כָּסֶף
The scene: Having established the timing rules around the Jubilee, the daf now works through the mechanics of consecrating and redeeming an ancestral field: how the field is measured (excluding unusable crevices and boulders), how the price is calculated per year remaining until the Jubilee, and — crucially — who is legally able to redeem it in a way that keeps it in the family. A new mishna maps out four different redeemers and four different outcomes, sparking Gemara dilemmas about daughters, wives, and fields consecrated too close to the Jubilee to be redeemed at a discount. The daf ends with a three-way tannaitic dispute over what happens to a field nobody ever redeemed.

🌾 Measuring and Pricing the Field

New mishna: the base valuation and its adjustments
RuleDetail
Base price50 sela (shekels) per beit kor — the area needed to sow a kor of barley by hand, average density
Crevices/boulders ≥10 handbreadthsExcluded from the measured area (not fit for sowing)
Crevices/boulders <10 handbreadthsIncluded — considered mere 'cracks' or 'ridges' of the field itself
Consecrated 2–3 years before JubileePay a sela and a pundeyon (1/48 of a sela) per remaining year
Payment methodMust be paid as one lump sum — 'I'll pay year by year' is not accepted
Owner vs. anyone else redeemingOnly the owner adds an extra one-fifth to the payment

👨‍👧 Why 'Another Man' Excludes a Son but Includes a Brother

The Gemara mines Leviticus 27:20's phrase for who does NOT trigger transfer to the priests
❓ 'To another man' (Lev. 27:20) — the field goes to priests only if redeemed by 'another,' not by the owner's son. But is a brother also excluded?
↓
📜 The word 'man' (ish) in the verse already includes a brother — so 'another' must be adding something more specific: it excludes the son
↓
Why include the son?
He 'stands in his father's place' for two things: designating a Hebrew maidservant, and inheriting a Hebrew slave (who need not go free)
Why not the brother?
His only comparable role is levirate marriage (yibum) — but yibum applies only when there is no son, proving a son outranks a brother
Left open: Can a daughter redeem and preserve the field for her father, like a son? Rabba bar Avuh's dilemma is resolved: no — for inheritance purposes a daughter (where there's a son) is 'like another.' Whether a woman's field is preserved by her husband or by her son remains an unresolved teiku.

💰 New Mishna: Who Redeems Decides Who Keeps It

Four scenarios, four different outcomes at the Jubilee
1️⃣ The owner himself redeems it → it never leaves his hands, even at the Jubilee
↓
2️⃣ His son redeems it → it returns to the father (the original owner) at the Jubilee
↓
3️⃣ Another person or relative redeems it, and the owner then redeems it from them → it still goes to the priests at the Jubilee
↓
4️⃣ A priest redeems it and still holds it at the Jubilee → he may NOT keep it as his own; it's divided among all his priestly brethren

🤔 Rami bar Ḥama's Dilemma

What if the field was consecrated too close to the Jubilee to be redeemed at a discount?
אִם לֹא יִגְאַל אֶת הַשָּׂדֶה... וְהָיָה הַשָּׂדֶה בְּצֵאתוֹ בַיּוֹבֵל
The question: If someone consecrated his ancestral field less than two years before the Jubilee — meaning it can only be redeemed at the full valuation, never at a year-prorated discount — and he never redeemed it, does it still transfer to the priests when the Jubilee arrives? Rav Ḥisda first floats the idea that only a field 'fit for deduction' transfers to the priests (so this one wouldn't), but then reverses himself: the verse's actual condition is simply whether the field was fit for redemption at all — and this field was, just not at a discount. So yes, an unredeemed field consecrated within two years of the Jubilee still goes to the priests.

⛪ What Happens to a Field No One Ever Redeemed?

New mishna: three tanna'im, three outcomes at the Jubilee
R' Yehuda
The priests enter and take the field — but they must pay its value to the Temple treasury. I derive this by verbal analogy ('holy'–'holy') from a consecrated house, which is also redeemed only for payment.
R' Shimon
The priests enter and take it for free — no payment required. My analogy is to the Shavuot lambs, a gift the priests receive gratis, since this field too is 'a gift to the priests.'
R' Eliezer
The priests don't enter at all! It stays with the Temple treasury, called a 'forsaken field,' until the next Jubilee — and again after that, until someone finally redeems it.
Gemara
Each tanna's verbal analogy is chosen precisely: R' Yehuda compares consecration-for-Temple-upkeep to another Temple-upkeep case (a house), not to an altar-offering; R' Shimon compares a priestly gift to another priestly gift, not to a non-gift like a house.
