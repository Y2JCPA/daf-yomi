📖 Overview: Collusion, Guarantors, and What Cannot Be Repossessed

Arakhin 23a–23b
הַמַּקְדִּישׁ נְכָסָיו וְהָיְתָה עָלָיו כְּתוּבַּת אִשָּׁה, רַבִּי אֱלִיעֶזֶר אוֹמֵר: כְּשֶׁיְּגָרְשֶׁנָּה יִדּוֹר הֲנָאָה
The scene: A new mishna imagines a person who consecrates all his property while still owing his wife her marriage contract (ketubah). Since the ketubah debt is secured by his property, she could, after a divorce, collect it out of the consecrated funds — and then quietly remarry him, having together extracted value from the Temple treasury. Rabbi Eliezer worries about exactly this scheme; Rabbi Yehoshua does not. The Gemara probes the dispute's real basis through several reinterpretations, tells a story about a guarantor's son caught in the same bind, and then turns to two further mishnayot: how the Temple redeems consecrated property burdened by debts, and — crucially — what basic possessions the treasury may never take from a debtor, even one who owes valuations.

🤝 The Collusion Vow: Necessary or Not?

Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua, extended by Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel to guarantors
R' Eliezer
One who consecrates his property while owing his wife her ketubah must, upon divorcing her, vow that benefit from her is forbidden to him — otherwise she collects from the Temple's funds and he quietly remarries her.
R' Yehoshua
No such vow is necessary.
Rabban Shim'on b. Gamliel
The same concern applies to a guarantor of a woman's ketubah: if her husband divorces her and cannot pay, he too must vow benefit from her forbidden, lest they collude against the guarantor's property and then remarry.
Gemara
What's the root of the Eliezer/Yehoshua dispute? R' Eliezer holds a person would scheme against Temple property; R' Yehoshua holds he would not.

🕵️ Testing the Dispute's Real Basis

The Gemara tries several explanations before settling on one
❓ Rav Huna: a dying man's claim 'I owe so-and-so 100 dinars' is believed even after consecrating everything — 'a person does not collude against hekdesh.' Doesn't this contradict Rabbi Eliezer?
↓
💡 Attempt 1: the dispute is only about a healthy person; all agree a dying person gains nothing personally, so he would never lie ('a person sins only for his own benefit')
↓
Attempt 2
Both agree collusion is a real concern; they disagree only whether a public vow can later be nullified
Final answer
They disagree whether a vow can be dissolved when it concerns consecrated property — R' Eliezer says no, so the vow truly blocks collusion
↓
📜 Support: Rabbi Elazar b. Rabbi Shimon links this exactly to Beit Shammai (mistaken consecration is still valid) vs. Beit Hillel (it is not, so it can be dissolved)

😔 The Case of Moshe bar Atzrei's Guarantee

A real-world test of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's ruling
1️⃣ Moshe bar Atzrei guaranteed his daughter-in-law's ketubah; his son Rav Huna, a struggling Torah scholar, needed money
↓
2️⃣ Abaye: let someone advise Rav Huna to divorce his wife, have her collect her ketubah from his father the guarantor, then remarry her — netting the family cash
↓
3️⃣ Rava objects: but the mishna requires the collusion-vow first, which would bar remarriage! Abaye: divorce outside court, and no vow is administered
↓
4️⃣ It later emerged Rav Huna was a priest — forbidden to remarry a divorcée regardless. Abaye: 'poverty follows the poor'
Legal footnote: The scheme only worked because Moshe bar Atzrei was a kablan (one who accepts unconditional guarantor responsibility) rather than an ordinary arev — the halakha ultimately follows that a guarantor is liable even if the debtor has no property, except an ordinary guarantor of a ketubah, since he 'performs a mitzva' and loses nothing himself.

🎒 What the Treasury Must — and Must Not — Leave Behind

A valuations-debtor keeps basic necessities; someone who consecrates ALL his property does not

Kept by an Arakhin Debtor

  • Food for 30 days, clothing for 12 months
  • A made bed, sandals, and tefillin (Lev. 27:8, 'sustain him')
  • Two tools of his craft; R' Eliezer: a farmer keeps his plow-team, a donkey-driver his donkey
  • If he has many of one tool and few of another, none are sold to balance it out

Taken from One Who Consecrates ALL His Property

  • Even his tefillin — since he declared 'all my property,' nothing is excluded
  • The Rabbis hold plow-oxen and a donkey are not 'craft tools' but ordinary property, so they too are taken
But never his family's: The mishna is explicit that basic provisions are never left for his wife or children — only for the debtor himself, per the verse's own wording, 'sustain him.'
