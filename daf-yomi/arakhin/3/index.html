📖 Overview: The 'Everyone' Sugya Continues

Arakhin 3a–3b
הַכֹּל חַיָּיבִין בְּסוּכָּה, כֹּהֲנִים לְוִיִּם וְיִשְׂרְאֵלִים
The scene: The Gemara keeps mining every mishnaic 'everyone' (hakol) for its hidden addition. It works through megilla reading and zimun (adding women, slaves, and a minor who knows to Whom he blesses); the impurities of ziva, tzara'at, and a corpse, each folding in a day-old infant via an extra word in its verse; who may sanctify the Parah Adumah ashes or sprinkle its water; who may slaughter; and who can be compelled to move to Eretz Yisrael or Jerusalem. The sugya then turns to a repeated question — why is it not obvious that priests, Levites, and Israelites are obligated in sukka, tzitzit, tefillin, and shofar? — and shows how each mitzva's own verse could have exempted priests specifically, so the baraita must state their inclusion explicitly.

🩹 Does a Minor Become Impure? The Verse's Hidden Yes

Tzara'at (leprous marks) as the test case
❓ 'Everyone becomes impure with leprous marks' (נגעים) — what does this add?
↓
💭 Might think: the verse says אִישׁ צָרוּעַ — a leprous MAN — so an adult, yes; a minor, no
↓
But the verse also says
אָדָם כִּי יִהְיֶה בְעוֹר בְּשָׂרוֹ — 'a person,' including everyone regardless of age
So what does 'ish' teach?
That a MALE leper tears his garments and lets his hair grow wild — a woman does not
↓
✅ A minor does become impure through tzara'at; 'ish' teaches a separate halakha about mourning practices
Same technique for ziva and corpse impurity: A day-old infant with a zav-like discharge becomes impure (Rabbi Yehuda, from the doubled 'ish ish'); one who contracts corpse impurity is impure at any age, from 'the persons that were there' (Numbers 19:18) — though a minor is excluded from the karet penalty tied to the word 'ish'.

🐄 Parah Adumah and Slaughter: Three More 'Everyone' Clauses

Each broad statement hides a different, specific addition

Sanctifying the Ashes

  • Mishna: everyone is fit except a deaf-mute, imbecile, and minor
  • Rabbi Yehuda: a minor IS fit
  • The Rabbis instead deem a woman fit (and a minor unfit)

Sprinkling the Water

  • 'Everyone is fit to sprinkle' adds one who is uncircumcised (עָרֵל)
  • Rabbi Elazar: an uncircumcised man's sprinkling is valid

Slaughtering

  • 'Everyone slaughters' adds a Samaritan (Kuti) — his slaughter is valid
  • And also adds an apostate Jew (Yisrael mumar)
Also from 'everyone': One may compel his household to move even a slave to Eretz Yisrael or Jerusalem (or from a pleasant home to a poor one there), but none may be compelled to leave — not even from a poor home in Jerusalem to a pleasant one elsewhere.

⛺ Are Priests Really 'Obviously' Obligated?

The Gemara keeps asking why a baraita bothers to mention priests, Levites, and Israelites by name
Gemara
'Everyone is obligated in sukka, including priests, Levites, and Israelites' — isn't that obvious?
Answer
Not obvious: sukka is compared to one's home, where a man dwells with his wife. Priests, occupied with Temple service, might have been exempt entirely!
Gemara
So how are they included after all?
Answer
They are exempt only during their actual service — off-duty they are obligated, just like travelers, who are exempt from sukka by day but obligated at night.
Gemara
Same question for tzitzit: priests wear diverse kinds (wool and linen) in their vestments during service — shouldn't that exempt them from tzitzit altogether?
Answer
Only during service is diverse kinds permitted to them; off-duty they may not wear it, so they remain obligated in tzitzit the rest of the time.
Pattern: Each mitzva's own verse could plausibly have carved priests out; the baraita has to say 'including priests' precisely because the exemption is a live possibility, not an obvious non-issue.

🎺 Tefillin of the Head, and Trumpets vs. Shofar

Two more cases where an apparent exemption for priests is dismantled
1️⃣ Priests cannot wear arm-tefillin during service — their vestments must touch the skin directly with nothing interposing. So perhaps they're also exempt from head-tefillin?
↓
2️⃣ No: the two mitzvot don't depend on each other — 'absence of the arm-tefillin does not prevent the head-tefillin, and absence of the head-tefillin does not prevent the arm-tefillin'
↓
3️⃣ Objection: doesn't the High Priest's mitre also interpose on the head, just like the vestments do on the arm? Answer: his hair was visible between the frontplate (tzitz) and the mitre — exactly where tefillin sit
↓
4️⃣ Same logic for shofar: priests sound trumpets all year for the offerings, but trumpets and shofar are different mitzvot entirely — that doesn't exempt them from sounding the shofar on Rosh HaShana
Takeaway: An exemption from one mitzva never automatically bleeds into a similar-looking mitzva; each requires its own verse and its own proof.
