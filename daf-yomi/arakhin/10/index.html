📖 Overview: Trumpets, Hallel, and the Temple Orchestra

Arakhin 10a–10b
אֵין פּוֹחֲתִין מֵעֶשְׂרִים וְאַחַת תְּקִיעוֹת בַּמִּקְדָּשׁ, וְלֹא מוֹסִיפִין עַל אַרְבָּעִים וּשְׁמֹנֶה
The scene: The daf opens a new mishna cataloguing more Temple minimums and maximums: the daily trumpet blasts (twenty-one to forty-eight), the lyres (two to six) and flutes (two to twelve) of the Levitical orchestra, and a dispute over the musicians' own lineage. The Gemara notes the mishna's trumpet count follows the Rabbis' view that tekiah, teruah, and tekiah are each counted separately, against Rabbi Yehuda's view that the three form a single unit. It then explains why the flute plays specifically on the twelve days that overlap with the eighteen days of full Hallel, working through why Sukkot gets Hallel every day but Pesach only the first, why Shabbat and Rosh Chodesh are skipped, why Rosh HaShana and Yom Kippur are somber days without song, and why Chanukah's Hallel for a miracle does not extend to Purim. The daf closes with Temple-craft anecdotes: instruments whose 'improved' gold plating or professional repairs only made their sound worse, restored only once the improvements were undone.

🎺 Rabbis vs. Rabbi Yehuda — How Do You Count a Blast?

The same sequence of sounds, two different tallies
Rabbis
A tekiah is one unit, a teruah another, and the closing tekiah a third — hence a daily range of 21 to 48 blasts.
R' Yehuda
No — tekiah, teruah, and tekiah together count as ONE unit, so the range is only 7 to 16.
Rabbis' proof
'And when congregating the assembly, sound a tekiah, and not a teruah' (Numbers 10:7) — if the three were one unit, that verse would demand only HALF a mitzva!
R' Yehuda replies
That congregating-tekiah is merely a signal to gather, not a mitzva-blast at all. A true mitzva blast always comes as a complete set of three.

🎶 Why Hallel Some Days and Not Others?

The flute plays on the twelve days that overlap with full Hallel
❓ Full Hallel is recited every day of Sukkot but only the FIRST day of Pesach — why?
↓
💡 Sukkot's additional offerings change each day (the bull-count shifts); Pesach's stay identical — so only Pesach's first day, when the Festival pattern begins, gets Hallel
↓
Shabbat
Its offerings differ too — but it is never called a 'mo'ed' (festival), so no Hallel
Rosh Chodesh
IS called mo'ed, but carries no labor-prohibition — song requires a day 'sanctified' like a Festival
↓
🕊️ Rosh HaShana/Yom Kippur meet both conditions, yet still NO Hallel: R' Abbahu — the angels asked why, and God answered, 'can Israel sing while the books of life and death lie open before Me?'

🕎 Chanukah, Purim, and a Miracle Outside the Land

Three attempts to explain why Purim gets no Hallel
Gemara
Chanukah has no special offering and no labor-prohibition, yet we say Hallel — because of the miracle.
Objection
Then why not Purim too? Its miracle is just as real!
R' Yitzchak
We do not sing Hallel for a miracle that occurred OUTSIDE the Land of Israel.
Rav Naḥman b. Yitzchak objects
But the Exodus was also a miracle outside the Land — and we say Hallel for it every Pesach!
Resolution
Before Israel entered the Land, every land was fit for song; entry gave the Land unique sanctity, excluding others — until exile restored their fitness. Rav Naḥman: reading the Megilla IS itself a form of Hallel. Rava: after the Exodus we could say 'servants of the LORD,' no longer Pharaoh's — but after Purim we remained subjects of Ahasuerus.

🔧 Temple Instruments — When 'Improvements' Made Things Worse

Craftsmanship anecdotes about the Temple's musical and ritual vessels
1️⃣ The Temple flute (reed, from the days of Moses) was gold-plated by royal command — its sweet sound was ruined; removing the plating restored it
↓
2️⃣ The copper cymbal, damaged, was repaired by artisans from Alexandria — its sound worsened; undoing the repair restored it
↓
3️⃣ The copper mortar used to grind incense spices suffered the same fate: an Alexandrian repair dulled its performance, and removing that repair brought it back
↓
4️⃣ Both vessels survived from the FIRST Temple, damaged beyond true repair — echoing the Shiloach pool, widened to increase its flow but only diminished by it: 'let not the wise glory in his wisdom, nor the mighty in his might'
