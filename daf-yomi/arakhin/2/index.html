📖 Overview: The Opening Mishna of Arakhin

Arakhin 2a–2b
הַכֹּל מַעֲרִיכִין, וְנֶעֱרָכִין, נוֹדְרִין וְנִידָּרִין
The scene: Masechet Arakhin opens with the mishna: everyone takes vows of valuation, obligating themselves to donate the Torah-fixed sum for a person's age and sex (Leviticus 27:3–7), and everyone can themselves be valuated. It applies to priests, Levites, Israelites, women, and slaves; a tumtum and hermaphrodite may vow but cannot be valuated, since only a definite male or female qualifies; a deaf-mute, imbecile, and minor can be valuated but cannot vow, since they lack presumed mental competence. The Gemara then works through the mishna clause by clause, asking what each 'everyone' (hakol) is meant to add beyond the obvious, and applies the identical technique to parallel mishnayot about sukka, lulav, tzitzit, tefillin, appearing in the Temple, and shofar — pausing along the way to debate whether an heir can place hands on, or substitute for, an inherited offering.

📜 The Opening Mishna: Four Categories

Two separate abilities — vowing and being valuated — do not always travel together
GroupVows valuationIs valuatedReason
Priests, Levites, Israelites, women, slavesYesYesThe baseline group named in the mishna
Tumtum & hermaphrodite (androginos)YesNo'Only a definite male or a definite female are valuated'
Deaf-mute, imbecile, minorNoYes'Because they lack the presumed mental competence' (da'at)
Two more additions: 'Everyone valuates' adds a discriminating minor on the brink of adulthood (מוּפְלָא סָמוּךְ לְאִישׁ); 'everyone is valuated' adds a repulsive man or one afflicted with boils, who have no market value yet are still valuated.

⚖️ Can an Heir Place Hands or Effect Substitution?

The same 'his offering' vs. 'hamer yamir' dispute runs through Menachot and Temura
Baraita
An heir places hands (סומך) on his father's offering, and an heir can effect substitution (מימר) for an offering he inherited.
R' Yehuda
No — he expounds קׇרְבָּנוֹ, 'his offering' (Leviticus 3:2), meaning only one's own offering, not his father's. The initial stage of consecration (substitution) is derived from the final stage (placing hands): neither works for an heir.
Rabbis
We read הָמֵר יָמִיר, the doubled 'if he shall substitute' (Leviticus 27:10), as including the heir — and we derive placing hands from substitution, the reverse direction.
R' Yehuda
I use that same doubled 'hamer yamir' differently: to include a woman among those who may effect substitution, since the whole passage is otherwise phrased only in the masculine.
Rabbis
We already learn that a woman may substitute from the extra letter in וְאִם הָמֵר יָמִיר, 'and if he shall substitute' — a letter Rabbi Yehuda does not expound at all.
Bottom line: Every extra letter or doubled verb in these verses is read by one side or the other as including either the heir or the woman — never both from the same word.

🔍 The Gemara's Method: What Does 'Everyone' Add?

A recurring question applied clause by clause to the mishna
❓ Mishna: 'everyone is valuated' (נֶעֱרָכִין) — what extra case does this add?
↓
💭 Might think: a repulsive man or one with boils has no market value (no דָּמִים), so like anyone excluded from assessment, he should be excluded from valuation too
↓
If only 'according to your valuation'
No monetary value → no valuation, since valuation is linked to assessment
But the verse also says
'נְפָשֹׁת' — Persons — anyone with any amount of life is valuated, regardless of market price
↓
✅ Conclusion: the repulsive man and the one with boils ARE valuated, taught by the word 'nefashot' in the same verse
Same technique elsewhere: The Gemara asks the identical 'what does this add?' question about a child less than a month old, learned from 'everyone is the object of a vow.'

🕎 Same Pattern, Five Mitzvot: What 'Everyone' Includes

The Gemara imports its method into other 'hakol' (everyone) statements across Shas

Sukka & Lulav

  • 'Everyone is obligated in sukka' — adds a minor who no longer needs his mother at night
  • 'Everyone is obligated in lulav' — adds a minor who knows how to wave it

Tzitzit & Tefillin

  • 'Everyone is obligated in tzitzit' — adds a minor who knows how to wrap himself in a garment
  • 'Everyone is obligated in tefillin' — adds a minor who knows to keep them in a state of cleanliness; his father buys him a pair

Shofar

  • 'Everyone is obligated to hear the shofar' — adds a minor who has reached the age of training (חִינּוּךְ)
  • One does not stop such a minor from sounding it on the Festival
The pattern: Wherever a mishna says 'everyone,' the Gemara assumes it is teaching one specific, non-obvious inclusion — usually a minor at a particular stage of development.

🕊️ Who Must Appear in the Temple? The Half-Slave Case

'Everyone is obligated in the mitzva of appearance' (Chagiga 2a) — reconciled with a famous Beit Hillel/Beit Shammai dispute
1️⃣ Mishna: 'everyone is obligated in re'iya' adds one who is half-slave, half-freeman — but Ravina says such a person is actually exempt
↓
2️⃣ Per Ravina, the addition must instead be one who was lame on day one of the Festival and healed on day two — obligated once healed
↓
3️⃣ Background: Beit Shammai challenged Beit Hillel's ruling that a half-slave serves his master one day and himself the next — 'you fixed the master's share, but not his own: he can marry neither a maidservant nor a free woman'
↓
4️⃣ Resolution: for the betterment of the world, the court forces the master to free him fully, and he writes a bill for half his value — Beit Hillel retracted to agree with Beit Shammai, and only then is he obligated to appear
Two mishna versions: The 'included' and 'exempt' readings of the half-slave case map onto the earlier and later versions of the Beit Hillel/Beit Shammai mishna in Pesachim.
