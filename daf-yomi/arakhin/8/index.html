📖 Overview: A Chapter of Minimums and Maximums

Arakhin 8a–8b
אֵין פֶּתַח בְּטוֹעָה פָּחוֹת מִשִּׁבְעָה, וְלֹא יָתֵר עַל שִׁבְעָה עָשָׂר
The scene: The daf finishes the discussion of a poor man's two valuation vows made in one breath, left unresolved as teiku, and then turns to a run of mishnayot each structured just like the arakhin mishna: 'no less than X, no more than Y.' A woman uncertain whether her bleeding fell in her days of menstruation or the eleven days of ziva needs between seven and seventeen clean days to resolve her status, depending on how many days she bled; the Gemara clarifies this range is not about her permission to her husband, which is always resolved within seven days, but about pinning down her halakhic category. Marks of leprosy require a minimum one-week quarantine for a person and can take up to three weeks for a house, prompting Rav Pappa's homiletic reading of a psalm. The daf closes with a fresh mishna listing several more such ranges: the number of thirty-day months added to a year, the days within which the two loaves and the showbread must be eaten, and the earliest and latest day for a circumcision.

🪙 Two Simultaneous Valuation Vows — Splitting the Five Sela

Continuing yesterday's case of a poor man with only five sela
1️⃣ Recap: paying the SECOND vow first (4 of 5 sela), then the FIRST vow (the last 1) fulfills both — 'a later creditor who collects first keeps what he collected'
↓
2️⃣ But paying the FIRST vow with 4 sela and the SECOND with the last 1: the second is fulfilled, but the first is NOT — all 5 were available when it alone was due
↓
3️⃣ New dilemma (Rav Adda bar Ahava): if he vows BOTH valuations in one breath, so they take effect simultaneously, do the 5 sela split evenly — 2½ toward each — or does each vow independently claim all 5?
↓
4️⃣ The Gemara leaves this open: תֵּיקוּ — unresolved

🩸 The 'Petach' of an Uncertain Woman — How Many Clean Days?

A woman unsure whether her bleeding fell in her days of niddah or her days of ziva
Days she saw bloodClean days requiredWhy
1 day17 clean daysWorst case: that single day could be the very start of a new cycle
4 days16 clean daysCould span the end of her ziva days into the start of niddah
7 days13 clean daysA longer bleed shortens the maximum possible remaining uncertainty
13 days7 clean days (the floor)The longest bleed needs the fewest additional clean days to resolve
The mishna's headline: Whatever the count of days she bled, resolving her status never takes fewer than seven clean days, and never more than seventeen.

❓ Why Not Just Always Count Seven?

Rav Adda bar Ahava challenges the whole elaborate system
R' Adda
Why all this counting? Let her simply count seven clean days and be permitted to her husband, as any woman may!
Rabba
We are not asking about her permission to her husband — that IS always resolved within seven days. We are asking a separate question: is she a niddah or a zava?
R' Adda presses
What difference does the label make, if she's permitted either way after seven?
Rabba explains
A great difference: if she is a niddah, she is pure after seven days regardless of how many are clean. If her bleeding fell during the days of ziva for one or two days, she is pure after just ONE clean day; for three or more, she needs seven — as a full zava.
The real question: 'Petach' is not about marital purity — it is about pinning down which category she was ever in, a determination that can take up to seventeen days.

🏠 Quarantine for Leprous Marks — Person vs. House

The mishna's next 'no less, no more' pair

נִגְעֵי אָדָם (Marks on a Person)

  • Minimum quarantine: one week
  • A priest issues a definitive ruling after seven days

נִגְעֵי בָתִּים (Marks on a House)

  • Maximum quarantine: three weeks
  • Some house-mark cases require the full three weeks to resolve
Rav Pappa's homiletic reading of Psalms 36:7: 'Your righteousness is like the mighty mountains' — the quick, one-week resolution for a person; 'Your judgments are like the great deep' — the extended, three-week uncertainty of a house. The Gemara ties this to a dispute between Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina over how God weighs merit against sin.
