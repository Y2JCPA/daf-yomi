📖 Overview: Valuations, Assessments, and Consecrated Animals

Arakhin 20a–20b
חוֹמֶר בָּעֲרָכִין מִבַּנְּדָרִים: הָאוֹמֵר ״עֶרְכִּי עָלַי״ וָמֵת — יִתְּנוּ הַיּוֹרְשִׁים
The scene: The daf opens by resolving Rava's dilemmas from the end of the previous daf about whether a vow of assessment (damim) requires a fresh court appraisal each time it is repeated — settled by the mishna's ruling about a vower who dies before any assessment was made. A new mishna then draws the mirror-image stringency: valuations (arakhin), fixed by the Torah, bind even a dead vower's heirs, while assessments (damim) do not, since 'there is no monetary value for the dead.' The mishna and Gemara then work through vows on limbs, on 'half' of oneself phrased two different ways, and on the valuation or assessment of someone else entirely. The daf closes with a fresh mishna distinguishing 'this bull is a burnt-offering' from 'it is incumbent upon me to bring this bull,' and a related dispute about consecrating something that does not yet exist.

🧮 Resolving Rava's Chain of Dilemmas

Does 'my assessment is incumbent upon me,' repeated or pre-existing, need a fresh appraisal?
❓ Rava's dilemmas: one assessment or two for a double vow? Does an outside appraisal (by ten, not for the Temple) count?
↓
📜 Proof offered: the mishna rules that if one vows 'my assessment is incumbent upon me' and dies before being appraised, heirs pay nothing, 'for there is no value for the dead'
↓
Suggested proof
If an outside appraisal counted, every person is worth at least 4 zuz — so heirs should always owe something
Gemara's rejection
An outside appraisal 'enters into' a real assessment; a bare 'damai alai' with no appraisal at all never entered an assessment — the cases are not equivalent
↓
✅ The dilemmas are not fully resolved by this mishna alone — but the Gemara draws out what the ruling does and doesn't prove
Underlying question: Must an assessment be performed with explicit intent for Temple consecration, or does any appraisal by a valid court suffice?

⚰️ Mishna: Valuations Are Stronger Than Assessments

What happens if the vower dies before paying?
VowVower dies before payingWhy
'My valuation (erekh) is incumbent upon me'Heirs must payThe sum is fixed by the Torah according to age and sex — nothing is 'lacking'
'My assessment (damim) is incumbent upon me'Heirs need not payThere is no monetary value for the dead, and the exact sum still needed a court appraisal
'The valuation of so-and-so is incumbent upon me,' and both dieHeirs must payOnce he stood in judgment, his property was liened; the appraisal of the other is a mere disclosure
'The assessment of so-and-so is incumbent upon me,' the vower diesHeirs must payHis own obligation was fixed against him in judgment while alive
Same vow, but the subject of the vow diesHeirs need not payNo monetary value can be fixed for a dead person
Key move: The Gemara reads every 'heirs must pay' clause as referring specifically to a case where the vower 'stood in judgment' before death — the court's ruling functions like a debt written in a document.

🦵 Vowing a Limb: 'Nothing' vs. 'The Whole Valuation'

Only items the soul depends on trigger a full-body valuation

Says Nothing

  • 'The valuation of my forearm'
  • 'The valuation of my leg'
  • Rav Giddel/Rav: still owes the assessment (damim) of the limb, per Rabbi Meir — 'a person does not speak for naught'

Owes Full Valuation

  • 'The valuation of my head'
  • 'The valuation of my liver'
  • Principle: דָּבָר שֶׁהַנְּשָׁמָה תְּלוּיָה בּוֹ — an item the soul depends on
  • Includes the leg 'from the knee and above,' since severing it there is fatal
Source: Leviticus 27:2, 'a valuation of living people (nefashot),' teaches that only a vow on something life-dependent triggers the full valuation of the entire self.

➗ Half My Valuation vs. The Valuation of Half of Me

Word order changes the bill completely — and the same split applies to assessments
1️⃣ 'Half of my valuation is incumbent upon me' → pays exactly half his valuation
↓
2️⃣ 'The valuation of half of me is incumbent upon me' → pays his entire valuation, since 'half of a person' is life-dependent
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Yosei bar Yehuda: even 'half of my valuation' is punished with lashes and must pay the full valuation — a rabbinic decree lest it be confused with 'valuation of half of me'
↓
4️⃣ The identical split applies to assessments (damim): 'half of my assessment' vs. 'the assessment of half of me,' derived by juxtaposing neder and erekh in the same verse
Vessels compared: Rabbi Meir and the Rabbis dispute a parallel case — vowing half the valuation of a vessel. Rava and Abaye explain the Rabbis follow both Rabbi Meir (no wasted words, so a whole vessel's valuation becomes its assessment) and Rabbi Shimon (only a typical act of donation takes effect — donating a whole vessel is typical, half a vessel is not).

🐂 'This Bull Is a Burnt-Offering' vs. 'It Is Incumbent Upon Me'

New mishna: who bears the loss if the animal dies or the house collapses?
Mishna
'This bull is a burnt-offering' or 'this house is an offering' — if it dies or collapses, he is exempt from replacing it.
Mishna
But 'it is incumbent upon me to bring this bull/house as an offering' — if it dies or collapses, he is obligated to pay its value.
R' Ḥiyya bar Rav
This distinction only holds when he adds 'the monetary value of.' If he just says 'this bull is incumbent upon me,' the word 'this' ties the vow to that specific animal — he isn't responsible if it dies.
Objection
A baraita seems to say even without 'monetary value,' saying 'incumbent upon me' creates liability for death or theft!
Resolution
Both clauses of that baraita in fact discuss 'monetary value' vows — one where the bull itself is consecrated for its value, one where the future sale proceeds are consecrated (following Rabbi Meir, who allows consecrating something not yet in existence).
Side halakha: A related Tosefta teaches that a landlord whose rented house is struck with leprosy may tell the tenant 'that which is yours is before you' while it stands — but once a priest has it dismantled, the landlord must supply a replacement house.
