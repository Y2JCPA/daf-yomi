📖 Overview: The One-Year Clock on a Walled City House

Arakhin 31a–31b
הַמּוֹכֵר בֵּית בְּבָתֵּי עָרֵי חוֹמָה — הֲרֵי זֶה גּוֹאֵל מִיָּד, וְגוֹאֵל כׇּל שְׁנֵים עָשָׂר חֹדֶשׁ
The scene: A new mishna and chapter address a special Torah category: a house within a city walled since the era of Joshua. Unlike an ancestral field, such a house can be redeemed at any time during the year following its sale — but if that year passes unredeemed, it becomes the buyer's forever, never returning even at the Jubilee. The Gemara works out precisely how that year is counted, debates whether repaying the buyer in full (without deducting for his year of residence) constitutes forbidden interest, and contrasts the strict one-year clock on an ordinary sale with the far more permissive rule for redeeming such a house from the Temple treasury. The daf closes with Hillel's practical fix for buyers who hid to run out the clock.

💰 Repaying in Full: Interest, or Not?

The mishna calls it 'like a form of interest, yet not interest' — the Gemara tests that claim
Objection
A baraita flatly states: this IS full-fledged interest — the Torah simply permitted it as an exception!
R' Yoḥanan
No contradiction: our mishna follows Rabbi Yehuda, that baraita follows the Rabbis — they dispute a related case of a conditional field-sale securing a loan.
The case
If a lender takes a conditional sale of a debtor's field (voided if the loan is repaid on time), the Rabbis permit it only if the SELLER keeps eating the produce meanwhile — if the LENDER eats it, that's 'interest that might apply' (a one-sided uncertainty), forbidden.
R' Yehuda
Even the lender may eat the produce — citing the real case of Baitos ben Zunin, ruled on by Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya.
Rava's alternate framing
Actually, all agree 'one-sided possible interest' is forbidden; they truly disagree about interest given on condition it will be refunded if the sale is voided.

⛪ A Consecrated House: Redeemable Forever

The Temple treasury doesn't get the buyer's one-year advantage
1️⃣ One who CONSECRATES his walled-city house may redeem it from the Temple treasury at ANY time — no twelve-month deadline at all
↓
2️⃣ Why? 'To the one who bought it... throughout his generations' (Lev. 25:30) — the Temple treasury has no 'generations,' so it can never claim permanent ownership
↓
3️⃣ But if ANOTHER person redeems it FROM the Temple treasury, the ordinary twelve-month clock starts running against that new owner
↓
4️⃣ Rav Safra: the phrase 'it shall not go out in the Jubilee' is needed for the case where the Jubilee falls mid-year of an ordinary sale — the house still becomes the buyer's permanently and does NOT revert early

🚪 Hillel's Takanah: No More Hiding

A practical fix for a practical abuse
הִתְקִין הִלֵּל שֶׁיְּהֵא חוֹלֵשׁ מְעוֹתָיו לַלִּשְׁכָּה, וִיהֵא שׁוֹבֵר אֶת הַדֶּלֶת וְנִכְנָס
The problem and the fix: Originally, a buyer who wanted the house permanently would simply hide on the final day of the twelve months, making it impossible for the seller to hand over the redemption money in time. Hillel instituted that the seller could instead deposit the money in the Temple treasury's chamber, then break down the door of the house and enter — with the buyer free to collect his money from the chamber whenever he liked.

🤝 A Halakhic Ripple: Conditional Divorce

Rava draws a surprising inference from Hillel's ordinance
1️⃣ Hillel had to invent a workaround so that 'giving against someone's will' would count as valid payment
↓
2️⃣ The Gemara infers: ordinarily, forcing money on someone who refuses it does NOT count as a valid act of giving
↓
3️⃣ Rava applies this to a conditional get: 'this is your bill of divorce on condition you give me 200 dinars' — if she pays willingly, she is divorced
↓
4️⃣ But if she is forced to pay against her will, the condition is not fulfilled and the divorce does NOT take effect
Source: מִדְּאִיצְטְרִיךְ לֵיהּ לְהִלֵּל לְתַקּוֹנֵי נְתִינָה בְּעַל כׇּרְחוֹ הָוְיָא נְתִינָה — 'From the fact that Hillel needed to institute that giving against one's will counts as giving...'
