📖 Overview: Doubtful Gifts and a Condemned Man's Vow

Arakhin 6a–6b
הַגּוֹסֵס וְהַיּוֹצֵא לֵיהָרֵג — לֹא נִידָּר וְלֹא נֶעֱרָךְ
The scene: The Gemara first works out when a gentile's donation to the Temple may be accepted — never at the start of construction, and only if unidentifiable as his at the end — and how to handle doubtful intent, as with a gentile's teruma or an inscribed beam, both of which require interment when his purpose is unclear. It then asks whether a coin pledged to charity, or a lamp donated to a synagogue, may still be redirected, distinguishing a Jewish donor from a gentile one. A new mishna turns to a person on his deathbed or condemned to execution: is he the object of a vow, or valuated? The tanna kamma says neither; Rabbi Ḥanina ben Akavya says he is still valuated, since the Torah's sum is fixed regardless of market value; and Rabbi Yosei holds he can still vow, valuate, and consecrate his own property, and remains liable for damage he causes — a liability the Gemara ties to whether his estate can be made to pay after his execution.

🏛️ Gentile Donations for the Temple — Timing and Type

Rabbi Asi in the name of Rabbi Yoḥanan sets out the stages
Stage of constructionRuleExample
BeginningNothing accepted, not even water and saltSo the builders do not come to rely on gentiles
End — a defined itemNot accepted, since it would be identifiable as hisThe cubit-wide spiked plates that keep ravens off the roof
End — an undefined itemAccepted, since no one could tell it came from himAny ordinary, unidentifiable material
Exception: Nehemiah accepted timber from the Persian king's forest — but government gifts are different, since 'a government does not retract what it says.'

🪙 'This Sela Is for Charity' — Can I Take It Back?

Tracking the halakha through several versions
1️⃣ Rav Naḥman/Rabba bar Avuh: one who pledges a coin to charity may still use it himself and later replace it
↓
2️⃣ Initial understanding: this applies only if he said 'it is incumbent upon me' (a future pledge), not 'THIS coin is for charity' (a specific designation)
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Yoḥanan (via Rav Ami) and Rava: no such distinction — he may change it in either wording, for himself or for someone else
↓
4️⃣ Final ruling (Rav Zevid of Neharde'a's version): permitted in every combination — but only until the coin reaches the hand of the charity collector; afterward it may no longer be changed

🕯️ Changing a Synagogue Donation — Jew vs. Gentile

The same lamp, two very different rules depending on the donor

A Jew's Menorah or Lamp

  • Forbidden to redirect it to a mundane (non-mitzva) use
  • Permitted to redirect it to a DIFFERENT mitzva

A Gentile's Menorah or Lamp

  • Forbidden to change it at all while his name as donor is still remembered
  • Permitted once his name has been forgotten
  • Reason: a gentile donor would protest and demand it back; a Jew would not object to a shift to another mitzva
The Sha'azrak story: An Arab merchant donated a lamp to Rav Yehuda's synagogue. It was switched while people might still remember him as the donor — and the Sages disputed among themselves who had acted too hastily.

⚰️ Is a Dying or Condemned Man Vowed About?

The mishna's three positions on a goses and one led to execution
Tanna Kamma
Neither a moribund person (גוֹסֵס) nor one being led to execution is the object of a vow, nor is he valuated.
R' Chanina
He IS valuated — his sum is fixed by the Torah according to age and sex, regardless of his actual market value!
R' Yosei
He can still vow to donate another's assessment, take vows of valuation, and consecrate his own property — and if he damages someone else's property, he is liable to pay.
Gemara
Everyone actually agrees he can vow, valuate, and consecrate. The real dispute with Rabbi Yosei is narrower: only whether he is liable for damage he causes to others.
Why 'not vowed about'? A goses has no market value (he cannot be sold); one led to execution likewise has none, and moreover the verse 'anything dedicated shall not be redeemed' (Leviticus 27:29) excludes him once his verdict is issued.

💰 Why Would a Condemned Man's Damage Payment Depend on His Heirs?

Tracing the true root of the dispute between the tanna kamma and Rabbi Yosei
❓ Why does Rabbi Yosei hold one led to execution is liable for damage he causes, while the tanna kamma exempts him?
↓
Rav Yosef
They dispute whether an ORAL loan can be collected from a debtor's heirs after his death — since he will be executed, only heir-collectible debts remain enforceable
Rava
Rather, everyone agrees an oral loan is not collectible from heirs; they dispute only whether a Torah-mandated debt (like damages) counts as a 'written' loan that IS collectible from heirs
↓
✅ Either way, the practical question is the same: can payment be recovered from the condemned man's estate after his execution?
