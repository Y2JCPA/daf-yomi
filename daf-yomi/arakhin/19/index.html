📖 Overview: Vows of Weight, Height, and a Limb's Worth

Arakhin 19a–19b
הָאוֹמֵר: ״מְשַׁקְּלִי עָלַי״ נוֹתֵן מִשְׁקָלוֹ, אִם כֶּסֶף כֶּסֶף, וְאִם זָהָב זָהָב
The scene: Having closed the chapter of Hesseg Yad (means-tested valuations), the daf opens a new mishna about people who vow to donate their own weight or height to the Temple treasury rather than a fixed Torah valuation. The Gemara works out what material fulfills a weight-vow, tells of a wealthy woman who paid her daughter's weight in gold, and debates how to measure a forearm's weight and a person's height when no simple scale applies. Four related dilemmas are left unresolved. The daf closes with a new mishna on vowing the assessment (damim) of one's forearm — a stringency of vows of assessment over Torah valuations — and the Gemara's attempt to pin down how such an assessment is even calculated.

The mishna's base rule, and how the Gemara expands what counts as payment

Pitch counts, even if some merchants there measure it by volumeRaḥavaEnough that some local merchants weigh it

The mother of Yirmatya: She vowed 'my daughter's weight is incumbent upon me' with no metal specified, yet paid in gold. The Gemara resolves this: the mishna is incomplete and really teaches that a distinguished, wealthy person must pay according to their honor even without specifying — and she was such a person.

💧 How Do You Weigh a Forearm?

Rabbi Yehuda's barrel of water against Rabbi Yosei's objection
R' Yehuda
Fill a barrel with water, plunge the arm in up to the elbow, note the water line. Then weigh donkey flesh, bones and sinews and add them until the barrel refills to that line — that weight is what he owes.
R' Yosei
But how can you match flesh to flesh, bone to bone, sinew to sinew by mere displacement? Volume and weight are not the same thing!
R' Yehuda
One estimates the proportions as best one can.
R' Yosei
If you're only estimating anyway, skip the barrel — just estimate the arm's weight directly!
R' Yehuda
We still do whatever is possible to be more precise — even an imperfect physical measure beats a pure guess.
Scriptural allusion: Though there's no formal proof that donkey flesh weighs like human flesh, R' Yehuda's method is supported by Ezekiel 23:20: 'whose flesh is as the flesh of donkeys.'

📏 My Height vs. My Full Height

Rav Yehuda's ruling, built on Rabbi Akiva's principle that no word is extra
1️⃣ 'My height is incumbent upon me' — Rav Yehuda: give a thick, unbending rod (שַׁרְבִיט שֶׁאֵינוֹ נִכְפָּף) equal to his height
↓
2️⃣ 'My full height is incumbent upon me' — a thin, bendable rod is also acceptable, so long as it equals his height
↓
3️⃣ A baraita objects: both phrasings require the thick, unbending rod — so why does Rav Yehuda distinguish?
↓
4️⃣ Resolution: Rav Yehuda follows Rabbi Akiva (Bava Batra 64a, on selling a house 'with its depth and height'), who reads extra words as adding something — so the extra word 'full' must add the option of a thin rod
Left unresolved: The Gemara raises but cannot answer (teiku) what one owes for vowing one's stature, width, sitting-height, thickness, or girth.

🖐️ How Far Does 'Hand' or 'Leg' Reach?

The Tosefta's water-barrel measure sparks a wider question about the word's scope
❓ Tosefta: for a forearm-weight vow, immerse the arm 'up to the elbow' (הָאַצִּיל). But for hand-washing in the Temple, 'hands' means only up to the wrist!
↓
💡 Resolution: Torah language (מִקְדָּשׁ) fixes 'hand' at the wrist; but for vows we follow ordinary human speech, where 'hand' reaches the elbow
↓
Tefillin
'Your hand' (Ex. 13:9) = the bicep, per the school of Menashe — yet another scope
Ḥalitza
'From upon his foot' includes up to the knee — Rav Pappa: the ankle bone is not a separate section
↓
✅ Each context has its own rule: vows follow colloquial usage; the Temple's wrist-line is a received halakha; tefillin and ḥalitza follow their own verses
Why it matters: The same Hebrew word (yad, regel) can mean different physical boundaries depending on whether the Torah is speaking about vows, ritual washing, tefillin, or ḥalitza.

🧑‍⚖️ New Mishna: 'The Assessment of My Forearm'

A stringency of vows of assessment (damim) over Torah valuations (arakhin) — and two ways to calculate it

Rava's Approach

  • Use the same method as assessing damages (nizakin)
  • Picture the person as a slave sold in the market: value before the injury minus value after
  • Abaye objects: that method fits someone who has lost a hand and depreciated — this vower's hand is intact and healthy

Abaye's Refinement

  • Compare the price of a slave who can work with two hands to one who works with only one
  • Not a slave missing a hand — rather one whose bill of sale reserves his first owner's claim on one hand
  • This isolates the pure value of 'the use of a forearm' without conflating it with an injury discount
Why the mishna is stringent: For Torah valuations (arakhin) there is no valuation of a lone limb at all — 'the valuation of my forearm' says nothing. But for a vow of assessment (damim), the court values it directly, which is why the mishna calls this 'more stringent with regard to vows than with regard to valuations.'
