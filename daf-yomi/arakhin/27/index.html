📖 Overview: Auctioning a Consecrated Field

Arakhin 27a–27b
הַמַּקְדִּישׁ אֶת שָׂדֵהוּ בְּשָׁעָה שֶׁאֵין הַיּוֹבֵל, אוֹמֵר לוֹ: פְּתַח אַתָּה רִאשׁוֹן
The scene: Having closed out the fixed-rate rules that apply to a field consecrated near the Jubilee, the daf opens a new chapter about consecrating a field at a time when no fixed 50-sela rate applies — its redemption price is simply whatever it's worth, determined by open bidding. The mishna requires the original owner to open that bidding, since he alone pays an extra fifth on top of his bid. The Gemara works through a real incident of a lowball bid, and then a detailed mishna choreographs what happens as bidders progressively drop out of a rising auction, down to the exact sela-by-sela arithmetic of the owner's mandatory fifth.

🏷️ New Chapter: The Owner Opens the Bidding

When there's no fixed Jubilee-rate, the field goes to auction
שֶׁהַבְּעָלִים נוֹתְנִין חוֹמֶשׁ, וְכׇל אָדָם אֵין נוֹתְנִין חוֹמֶשׁ
The rule: When a field is consecrated at a time the Jubilee rate doesn't apply, the treasurer tells the owner: 'You bid first.' Why single him out? The Gemara offers three reasons: since the field is dear to him, he'll likely bid it up; the mitzva of redemption belongs first to the owner by Torah law; and — the mishna's own stated reason — only the owner pays an extra one-fifth on top of his bid, so starting with him benefits the Temple treasury most.

🥚 The Man Who Bid 'An Issar'

A real incident, and Rabbi Yosei's correction
The owner
Consecrating his own low-quality field, told to bid first: 'It is mine — for an issar!' (a tiny coin).
R' Yosei
He didn't actually say 'issar' — he said 'an egg'! Consecrated property may be redeemed with money OR with money's equivalent value, like produce.
Treasurer
'The field has come into your possession' — the bid stands. He loses his issar (or egg) and gets the field back.
Gemara
This isn't really about whether an item's worth counts as money — everyone agrees it does. The real dispute is whether you may redeem with something whose one-fifth surcharge is worth less than a peruta: the first tanna requires at least an issar's worth; Rabbi Yosei allows even an egg.

➕ Calculating the Owner's Extra Fifth

Owner bid 20; another bidder tops him — how much does the owner ultimately pay?
1️⃣ Equal bids (both say 20): the owner wins automatically, since his bid effectively includes a mandatory extra fifth
↓
2️⃣ Other bidder says 21 → owner pays 26 (his 20 + a fifth of the eventual total, plus covering the 1-sela gap)
↓
3️⃣ Other bidder says 25 → owner pays 30 — the owner adds a fifth only to his own bid, never to the other party's raise
↓
4️⃣ Other bidder says 26 (exceeding what the owner's fifth alone can cover) → owner may still win by paying 31 sela and a dinar, or else the treasurer tells the outbidder: 'it's yours'

⚖️ Why the Fifth Matters Here but Not for Second Tithe

Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel also dispute a related fine point

Consecrated Field (Hekdesh)

  • The extra fifth is a gain for the Temple treasury
  • So an owner's bid (with its guaranteed fifth) beats an equal bid from anyone else
  • Beit Shammai/Beit Hillel dispute (per Rav Ḥisda): if a court of three already appraised the field at another bidder's price, does the owner's fifth apply to THAT raised sum too?

Second Tithe (Ma'aser Sheni)

  • The extra fifth there benefits the owner himself, not the Temple
  • So a mishna elsewhere favors whichever bid raises the principal more, regardless of who adds a fifth
  • Shows the general principle: whoever's fifth profits the Temple determines whose bid 'wins' in a tie

📋 Arakhin 27 — Summary Map

🏷️ Owner opens the bidding for his own field, since only he pays an extra fifth
↓
🥚 A too-low bid ('an issar' or even 'an egg') still binds the bidder, per the dispute over redeeming with a fraction-of-a-peruta fifth
↓
📉 Reneging bidders' property covers the shortfall down the chain — split if two renege together
➕ The owner's mandatory fifth applies only to his own bid, never to another's raise
↓
⚖️ The Temple always benefits from whoever's fifth is larger — unlike second-tithe redemption, where the fifth benefits the owner and doesn't decide the winning bid
↓
🎓 Key Principle: an open bidding auction can be regulated down to the last dinar to guarantee the Temple treasury never comes out behind
