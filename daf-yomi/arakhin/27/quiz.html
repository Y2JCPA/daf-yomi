QUESTION 1 OF 5
Why does the mishna require the ORIGINAL OWNER to open the bidding when a consecrated field (without a fixed Jubilee-rate) is redeemed by auction?
A. Because he is legally obligated to buy it back regardless of price
B. Because only the owner is required to add an extra one-fifth to his bid, which benefits the Temple treasury
C. Because other bidders are legally barred from bidding first
D. Because the owner always offers the lowest price
Correct: B — The mishna's stated reason is that the owner, unique among bidders, must add an extra fifth to his payment — starting the bidding with him is advantageous to the Temple treasury, alongside the practical reasons that the field is dear to him and that redemption is his mitzva by right.
QUESTION 2 OF 5
In the incident of the man who bid 'an issar' for his own field, what does the ensuing Gemara discussion reveal the dispute with Rabbi Yosei is really about?
A. Whether he actually owned the field
B. Whether one may redeem consecrated property with an item whose one-fifth surcharge is worth less than a peruta (a tiny coin)
C. Whether redemption bids must be made in the Temple itself
D. Whether the field was properly consecrated in the first place
Correct: B — The Gemara clarifies that everyone agrees money's-equivalent value counts as money; the actual dispute is whether an item like an egg, whose one-fifth surcharge doesn't even reach a peruta's value, can still be used to redeem — the first tanna requires at least an issar's worth, Rabbi Yosei permits even less.
QUESTION 3 OF 5
According to the mishna's falling-auction rules, if the bidder who offered fifty sela reneges and the forty-sela bidder still stands by his bid, what happens?
A. The field is sold at its assessed value with no penalty to anyone
B. The property of the fifty-sela bidder covers the ten-sela gap, and the field goes to the forty-sela bidder
C. The field is given for free to the forty-sela bidder
D. All prior bidders must renegotiate from scratch
Correct: B — The treasurer collects the ten-sela shortfall (fifty minus forty) from the reneging bidder's property, and the field is then redeemed by the next-highest bidder, who offered forty.
QUESTION 4 OF 5
According to the mishna's worked example, if the owner bids 20 sela and another person bids 25 sela, how much does the owner ultimately pay to win the field?
A. 25 sela, matching the other bidder exactly
B. 20 sela with no additional payment
C. 30 sela — the owner adds a fifth only to his own original bid of 20, not to the other bidder's addition
D. 26 sela, adding a fifth to the other person's bid
Correct: C — The mishna specifies that the owner adds one-fifth only to his own bid (20 + 1/4 of 20 = 25, rounded to reach the other's offer, then structured so the total comes to 30), never adding a fifth to the amount by which the other bidder exceeded him.
QUESTION 5 OF 5
Why does the extra one-fifth surcharge determine the winning bidder in a consecrated-field auction, but NOT in a comparable second-tithe (ma'aser sheni) redemption?
A. Because second-tithe redemptions never involve extra payments
B. Because in the field case the extra fifth benefits the Temple treasury, while in the second-tithe case the extra fifth belongs to the owner himself, so only the principal amount matters there
C. Because second-tithe items cannot legally be auctioned
D. Because the fifth only applies to land, never to produce
Correct: B — The Gemara explains that the field's extra fifth is a genuine gain for hekdesh (the Temple treasury), making the owner's bid preferable in a tie; but for second tithe, the fifth simply returns to the same owner, so the mishna there favors whichever bid raises the actual principal, regardless of who pays a fifth.
