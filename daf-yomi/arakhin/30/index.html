📖 Overview: Redemption Values and a Sermon on Decline

Arakhin 30a–30b
מְכָרָהּ לָרִאשׁוֹן בְּמָנֶה, וּמָכַר רִאשׁוֹן לַשֵּׁנִי בְּמָאתַיִם, אֵינוֹ מְחַשֵּׁב אֶלָּא עִם הָרִאשׁוֹן
The scene: The daf works out exactly which sale price governs when a field is redeemed after passing through two buyers, and finds — through a verbal analogy to the redemption of a Hebrew slave — that the Torah always favors the seller by calculating at whichever value is lower. That leniency triggers Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina's famous, sobering sermon tracing a person's descent from selling movable goods all the way down to selling himself into slavery to idol worship, and Abaye's insistence that the Torah still eases his redemption despite his sins. A final new mishna returns to practical law: an ordinary field sale carries strict redemption rules that consecrated property redemption does not.

⚖️ Proving the Torah's Leniency in Redemption

Why favor the lower value rather than the higher one?
❓ Why assume the verses favor the seller (lower value) rather than the buyer (higher value)?
↓
🔗 Gezeirah shava: 'redemption' (geulah) here is linked to 'redemption' used for a Hebrew slave sold to a gentile
↓
📜 There too: if his value rose, he redeems himself at the ORIGINAL (lower) price; if it fell, at the CURRENT (lower) price — proven from 'the money of his purchase' vs. 'according to his years'
↓
✅ Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak: the phrase 'in the years' (not just 'years') signals this is about value fluctuating during those years, not about counting years themselves — Rav Yosef calls this reading 'as true as if given at Sinai'

🌀 Rabbi Yosei bar Ḥanina's Sermon: The Slippery Slope

One violation of Shvi'it/Jubilee halakhot triggers a chain of ruin — each drawn from an adjacent verse
1️⃣ Trades in Sabbatical-year produce → eventually sells his movable property
↓
2️⃣ Doesn't feel remorse → eventually sells his ancestral fields
↓
3️⃣ Still doesn't register it → eventually sells his house in a walled city
↓
4️⃣ Still nothing → borrows money with interest, then sells his daughter, then finally sells HIMSELF — to a fellow Jew, then a stranger, then a ger toshav, then even into service of idol worship
Rav Huna's principle behind the shift in language: The sermon says 'he does not feel remorse' at first, then 'consciousness does not come to him' later — reflecting Rav Huna's teaching that repeating a sin twice makes it feel, to the sinner, as though it were permitted.

🪨 Should His Redemption Still Be Made Easy?

Given how far he's fallen by his own choices, why help him at all?
A challenger
Since it's entirely his own sins that brought him this low — even into idol worship — shouldn't we be STRICT about his redemption, not lenient?
Abaye
No — the Merciful One is explicitly gentle with a Hebrew slave: he must be housed, fed, and given drink 'with you,' as an equal. 'One who acquires a Hebrew slave acquires a master for himself.'
Challenger
But the school of Rabbi Yishmael itself asks: since he sold himself even to idol worship, why not 'throw a stone after the fallen' and abandon him?
Resolution
The verse answers its own question: 'he shall have a perpetual right of redemption' — so that he isn't assimilated among the nations. Leniency in redemption stands, regardless of how he got there.

🚫 New Mishna: Redeeming From a Private Buyer vs. From Hekdesh

Rabbi Shimon explains why consecrated-field redemption is actually easier

Redeeming From an Ordinary Buyer

  • May NOT sell a distant field to redeem a nearby one
  • May NOT sell a low-quality field to redeem a high-quality one
  • May NOT borrow money to redeem
  • May NOT redeem in installments (half now, half later)

Redeeming From the Temple Treasury

  • ALL of these methods are permitted
  • R' Shimon's reasoning: a private-sale field is 'empowered' — it returns to the seller automatically at the Jubilee even unredeemed
  • So its redemption process is restricted; a consecrated field is 'weakened' (goes to the priests if unredeemed), so its redemption is eased instead
