📖 Overview: The Limits and Mechanics of Ḥerem

Arakhin 28a–28b
מַחֲרִים אָדָם מִצֹּאנוֹ וּמִבְּקָרוֹ, וּמֵעֲבָדָיו וּמִשִּׁפְחוֹתָיו הַכְּנַעֲנִים, וּמִשְּׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּתוֹ, וְאִם הֶחְרִים אֶת כּוּלָּם אֵינָם מוּחְרָמִים
The scene: After finishing the auction mechanics of the previous chapter, the daf turns to a stronger, more absolute form of consecration: ḥerem, dedication for the priests. A new mishna sets its outer limit — one may dedicate part of his flocks, slaves, or ancestral field, but never everything, even for God's own sake. The Gemara traces this to four separate exclusions in a single verse, each independently necessary. It then asks who may dedicate at all (not property that isn't fully one's own — and priests and Levites are debated), who receives a dedication once made, and finally how one dedicates an item that is already consecrated for the altar, such as a vow-offering, a gift-offering, or a firstborn animal.

🔒 New Mishna: You May Not Dedicate EVERYTHING

Rabbi Eliezer's limit, and Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya's a fortiori extension
1️⃣ A person may dedicate some of his flock, cattle, Canaanite slaves, or ancestral field — but not all of any category
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Eliezer: if he dedicated ALL of a category, the dedication simply does not take effect
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya's kal vachomer: if a person may not give even the Most High everything, all the more so he should be sparing with what he gives to other people
↓
4️⃣ Practical difference: the Sages of Usha later enacted that charity should never exceed one-fifth of one's wealth — Rabbi Eliezer allows giving nearly all (keeping a token amount) to hekdesh; Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya caps it at a fifth even there
A real test case: One man wished to give away more than a fifth of his wealth to charity; his colleague — Rabbi Yeshevav (or, some say, Rabbi Akiva) — stopped him from doing so.

📜 Why the Torah Needed Four Separate Exclusions

Leviticus 27:28: 'of all that he has... of man... of animal... of his ancestral field'
❓ Why not just write 'of all that he has'? Why also spell out man, animal, and field separately?
↓
💡 Without each: one might think only the general rule applies, but a person could still dedicate an ENTIRE single category — all his slaves, or all his animals, or all his field
↓
Slaves & field
Both tied to livelihood — one might think both are equally protected without a separate verse for each
Movable goods
Without 'animal' as a third case, one might think livelihood-items alone are protected and ALL movables could still be dedicated
↓
✅ Bonus teaching from 'animal': one may dedicate only what he has permission to sell permanently — excluding a minor daughter (sellable only temporarily as a maidservant) or a purchased field (returns at the Jubilee)

🤝 Can Priests and Levites Dedicate Their Own Property?

New mishna: Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon disagree, and Rebbi reconciles them
R' Yehuda
Neither priests nor Levites can dedicate their property. Priests can't, because dedications already belong to them as a priestly gift — you can't give someone their own thing. Levites can't either, since the Torah juxtaposes movable goods to land, and Levite land is inalienable ('their perpetual possession').
R' Shimon
Priests can't dedicate — same reason as R' Yehuda. But Levites CAN dedicate, since dedications are not theirs at all — I don't read that juxtaposition to land the same way.
Rebbi's resolution
Rabbi Yehuda's view fits land best (Levite fields truly are inalienable). Rabbi Shimon's view fits movable property best (Levites may freely dedicate that) — but even Rabbi Shimon concedes Levite land cannot be dedicated.

🐑 Dedicating What's Already Consecrated

New mishna: dedications for priests need no redemption at all — and can even apply to sacrifices

General Dedications (Ḥerem)

  • Unlike Temple-maintenance consecration, ḥerem has no redemption — given directly to a priest, like teruma
  • R' Yehuda ben Beteira: unspecified dedication defaults to Temple maintenance (bedek habayit)
  • The Rabbis: unspecified dedication defaults to the priests directly

Dedicating Already-Sacred Animals

  • A vow-offering (e.g. 'a burnt-offering is upon me') → he owes its full value, since he must replace it
  • A gift-offering (e.g. 'this bull is a burnt-offering') → he owes only the 'benefit' — what someone would pay to sacrifice it, though not strictly permitted to
  • A firstborn animal, blemished or not → redeemed by assessing what an Israelite would pay a priest-relative to receive it

📋 Arakhin 28 — Summary Map

🔒 A person may dedicate part, never all, of his flocks, slaves, or field — a fortiori true for ordinary charity
↓
📜 Four separate scriptural exclusions guard against dedicating an entire category, each independently necessary
↓
🤝 Priests can't dedicate (it's already theirs); Levites' land can't, but Rabbi Shimon allows their movables
👳 Movable dedications go to any priest; dedicated land goes only to the currently serving watch
↓
🐑 Dedicating one's own sacrifices: full value for a vow-offering, only its 'benefit' for a gift-offering
↓
🎓 Key Principle: even the most absolute act of dedication to God still respects the boundary that a person may never surrender the entirety of his livelihood
