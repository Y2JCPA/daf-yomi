📖 Overview: Naming a Limb, and Naming a Nation

Arakhin 5a–5b
רַגְלָהּ שֶׁל זוֹ עוֹלָה
The scene: The Gemara first asks how much of an animal becomes consecrated when one names only its leg as a burnt offering, weighing Rabbi Meir's view that only the limb is sacred against Rabbi Yehuda, Rabbi Yosei, and Rabbi Shimon's view that the whole animal becomes holy — and raises a string of unresolved dilemmas (teiku) about consecrating a body part, a valuation, or a field specifically 'for the altar.' A new mishna then rules that a child less than a month old cannot be valuated at all, since the Torah fixes no sum for him, launching a debate over what such a vow really means. The daf closes with a fresh mishna: is a gentile valuated, does he vow, both, or neither? Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehuda take mirror-image positions, and the Gemara probes where a gentile's Temple donation actually goes.

🦵 'The Leg of This Animal Is a Burnt Offering'

How much of the animal actually becomes sacred?
R' Meir
Only the leg becomes sacred — 'of it that one gives to the Lord, it shall be sacred,' not all of it. Sell the animal for its burnt-offering value; the proceeds are non-sacred except for that limb's share.
Sages
No — the extra word 'shall be' includes the WHOLE animal. Naming even one limb sanctifies the entire creature.
R' Meir
I agree only with a vital exception: if the limb named is one the animal's LIFE depends on — its head — then I too say the whole animal becomes holy.
Abaye
But Rava, you yourself ruled that consecrating a male animal 'for its value' still gives it inherent sanctity! Doesn't that collapse your distinction?
Rava
Only when the WHOLE animal is named 'for its value.' Naming just one limb 'for its value' — as with the head — is an entirely different case.
Underlying question: Does naming one part of a body or one part of its value pull the rest along with it? The Gemara finds the answer depends on exactly what was named and for what purpose.

👶 Valuating a Child Under a Month — What Did He Really Mean?

The Torah fixes no erech for an infant less than one month old
❓ Mishna: a baby under a month is not valuated — no erech applies. What if someone vows his erech anyway?
↓
Rabbi Meir
He must pay the baby's market value (damim). 'A person does not make his words for naught' — knowing there's no erech, he must have meant assessment
The Rabbis
He owes nothing — a person's words CAN be for naught. His statement is taken literally, and erech has no application here
↓
💡 The same Rabbi Meir logic extends further: one who vows 'the erech of this utensil,' or who consecrates ANOTHER person's animal, likewise must pay its value — since those declarations, too, cannot mean what they literally say

⚖️ Three Near-Identical Cases, Each Needing Its Own Teaching

Why didn't Rav simply derive one ruling from another?

A Utensil ('erech keli alai')

  • No possibility of error — everyone knows utensils have no erech
  • So he clearly meant damim (market value)

Another's Animal

  • He cannot consecrate what isn't his, but an animal CAN in principle be consecrated
  • Rav: he still meant damim, intending to buy it first and then offer it

A Child Under a Month

  • Here one COULD mistakenly believe erech applies, since it does for a one-month-old child
  • Rabbi Meir needed a separate ruling to block that specific error

🌍 Can a Gentile Vow — or Be Valuated?

Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehuda's mirror-image dispute, and its aftermath
1️⃣ Mishna: Rabbi Meir — a gentile IS valuated (if a Jew vows his value) but does NOT vow; Rabbi Yehuda — the reverse: he vows, but is not valuated. Both agree he can vow another's assessment and be the object of an assessment vow
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Meir's proof: 'the children of Israel' excludes gentiles from vowing; 'a man' re-includes them for being valuated, since the verse 'includes more' there — like a deaf-mute or minor, who ARE valuated but cannot vow
↓
3️⃣ Rabbi Yehuda's proof runs the reverse comparison: the verse includes more in the vowing category — like a tumtum, who DOES vow but is not valuated
↓
4️⃣ Rava's verdict: Rabbi Meir's HALAKHA is sound (Ezra 4:3, 'you have nothing to do with us to build our Temple') but his PROOF is weak; Rabbi Yehuda's PROOF is sound, but his HALAKHA seems to contradict that same verse
Where does the money go? Rav Ḥisda/Avimi: a gentile's valuation is interred, unused. But a baraita imposes me'ilah liability on gentile Temple-upkeep consecrations — so Rava resolves that Rabbi Yehuda actually holds the money IS used for upkeep, and Ezra's rejection of the gentiles was about weakening the builders' hands, not a halakhic exclusion.
