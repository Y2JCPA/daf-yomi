📖 Overview: Second Jubilees and Inherited Fields

Arakhin 26a–26b
הַלּוֹקֵחַ שָׂדֶה מֵאָבִיו, וּמֵת אָבִיו, וְאַחַר כָּךְ הִקְדִּישָׁהּ — הֲרֵי הִיא כִּשְׂדֵה אֲחוּזָּה
The scene: The daf continues chasing down the true scriptural basis for Rabbi Eliezer's view that an unredeemed field never simply passes to the priests, testing and rejecting one reading before landing on another tied to the word 'anymore.' It then asks a pointed follow-up: if the owner missed his chance to redeem during the first Jubilee cycle but redeems during the second, is he now treated like 'another person'? A new mishna shifts to a different scenario — a son who buys his father's field and later inherits it outright — and asks whether the field's status as 'ancestral' or merely 'purchased' depends on the order of the father's death and the son's consecration. The Gemara traces the resulting dispute to a foundational question about what kind of ownership a lifetime usufruct actually confers.

🧑‍🌾 Finding Rabbi Eliezer's Real Reason

A rejected reading, and the case that finally locates his source
❓ Rabba: Rabbi Eliezer splits Leviticus 27:20 into two separate halakhot, so an unsold field never reaches the priests at all
↓
🗡️ Abaye objects: 'does a sharp knife cut through verses?' — you can't just chop a verse in half to make your point
↓
💡 Real derivation: 'anymore' (עוֹד) addresses a specific case — a field that already went to a priest, whom the priest then consecrated, and the ORIGINAL owner comes to redeem it back
↓
✅ Rava's final formulation: the priests only receive a field 'when it goes out' of the hands of another redeemer — never straight from an owner who held onto it himself

🔁 Redeeming in the Second Jubilee Cycle: Owner or 'Another'?

A dilemma with two conflicting versions of Rabbi Eliezer's own ruling
The dilemma
If the owner missed redeeming his field in the first Jubilee cycle, but redeems it himself in the second — is he now treated as 'another person,' so the field goes to the priests at the NEXT Jubilee?
Version 1
A baraita: 'Rabbi Eliezer says: if the owner redeemed it in the second Jubilee cycle, it DOES leave for the priests.' — Yes, he's treated like another.
Version 2
A different version: 'Rabbi Eliezer says: it does NOT leave for the priests.' — No, he keeps his own status as owner.
Ravina to Rav Ashi
But doesn't the mishna itself already say priests never enter until 'another' redeems it — implying the owner, anytime, keeps it?
Rav Ashi
Precisely the novel point: without this baraita I'd have assumed the second-cycle owner counts as 'another.' It teaches me he does NOT — he remains the owner in every cycle.

🚫 Purchased Fields vs. Priestly and Levitical Fields

Two very different relationships to the Jubilee cycle

A Consecrated Purchased Field

  • NEVER transfers to the priests at the Jubilee
  • Reason: a person cannot consecrate something that isn't fully his — his rights end at the Jubilee regardless
  • Instead it simply reverts out of consecration when the purchase-term naturally ends

Priests' and Levites' Own Fields

  • May be consecrated and redeemed at any time — before or after the Jubilee, without the ordinary timing restrictions
  • Their ancestral land relationship isn't governed by the same sale/return dynamic as an ordinary Israelite's

🍇 The Deeper Dispute: Kinyan Peirot or Kinyan Haguf?

Why each side needs Scripture for a different half of the case
1️⃣ Rabbi Meir needs a verse only for 'father died first, then consecrated' — implying that before that, owning the right to use the field (kinyan peirot) was NOT yet like owning the field itself (kinyan haguf)
↓
2️⃣ R' Yehuda and R' Shimon need a verse only for 'consecrated first, father died after' — implying that for THEM, kinyan peirot already equals kinyan haguf, so the son's ownership needed no upgrade at his father's death
↓
3️⃣ Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak refines this: elsewhere, R' Yehuda and R' Shimon actually treat kinyan peirot as fully equal to kinyan haguf too — they still need the verse, just to teach that the field's ancestral character specifically requires the father's death
