📖 Overview: Discipline, Reform, and the First Rite of the Day

Tamid 28a–28b
הִגִּיעַ עֵת
The scene: The daf opens by completing the account of the officer who patrolled the night watches — a sleeping watchman was struck and had his garment burned, a harsh but instructive precedent the Gemara uses to launch into teachings about the value of loving rebuke. The Gemara then resolves an apparent contradiction in the ash-removal mishna by tracing its history: originally a footrace up the ramp determined who removed the ashes, until an injury led the Sages to institute a lottery instead. A new mishna details the pre-dawn procession through the Temple courtyard — two groups checking every vessel was in place — before describing, in vivid sensory detail, how the winning priest performed the ash-removal rite entirely in darkness and silence, guided only by the glow of the altar's fire and the sound of a hidden mechanism.

🔥 Discipline for a Sleeping Watchman, and a Lesson in Rebuke

From harsh enforcement to a broader teaching about tochacha
1️⃣ The officer overseeing the watches greets each guard with 'peace be upon you' — if there's no reply, the guard is asleep
↓
2️⃣ He strikes the sleeping watchman with his staff and has license to burn his garment as discipline
↓
3️⃣ Others hearing the commotion would say: 'That's the sound of a Levite being flogged, his clothes burned, for sleeping on watch'
↓
4️⃣ Rabbi Yoḥanan's reflection: 'Praiseworthy were the early generations, who enforced judgment even for sleep beyond one's control — how much more so for deliberate wrongdoing'
↓
5️⃣ Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's broader teaching: the right path in life is to LOVE admonition, since rebuke brings pleasantness, goodness, and blessing into the world (Proverbs 24:25); Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani adds that one who rebukes another for Heaven's sake merits closeness to God Himself

🏃 Resolving a Contradiction: Race, Then Lottery

Why the mishna seems to describe two different systems
❓ The mishna first says whoever is READY (immersed) can remove the ashes — implying no lottery — then says the officer calls for a LOTTERY. Which is it?
↓
🔧 Abaye's resolution: the mishna describes TWO ERAS — 'ready first' describes the OLD system (before a reform); the lottery describes the NEW system (after the reform)
↓
🔄 Rava's alternative: BOTH clauses describe the post-reform era — 'rises early and immerses' means simply becoming ELIGIBLE to enter the lottery, not automatically winning the task
What was the old system? Originally (per a parallel mishna in Yoma), any priest wanting the honor would RACE up the ramp — whoever reached within four cubits of the top first, won. If two tied, they'd raise one or two fingers for a random count (never the thumb, to prevent cheating).

🏃 The Incident That Changed Everything

From competitive footrace to a calm lottery
StageWhat Happened
Original systemPriests raced up the ramp; first to reach within four cubits of the top won the ash-removal privilege
Tie-breakerTwo tied priests would extend one or two fingers for a random count (never a thumb, to prevent manipulation)
The incidentTwo priests running neck-and-neck up the ramp — one shoved the other, who fell and broke his leg
The reformThe court, seeing the danger this competition created, instituted that ash-removal would be determined ONLY by a calm lottery from then on

🚪 New Mishna: The Pre-Dawn Procession

Splitting into two groups to check every service vessel
שָׁלוֹם, הַכֹּל שָׁלוֹם
The scene: Near dawn, the appointed priest took a key hidden beneath a floor tablet, opened the wicket gate, and led the priestly family into the courtyard, torches lighting the way. They split into two groups — one walking the eastern portico, one the western — inspecting every service vessel along the route until both groups converged at the Chamber of the Griddle-Cake Preparer. There they declared to each other: 'Peace, all is well' — confirming every vessel was in place — before setting that day's griddle-cake preparer to begin his work. (The Gemara clarifies these porticoes were built of STONE, not wood, since Deuteronomy 16:21 forbids any tree or wooden structure beside the altar.)

🔥 Removing the Ashes — Alone, in the Dark

A rite performed by sound and firelight alone
1️⃣ No one accompanied the winning priest, and he carried no lamp — he walked only by the glow of the still-burning wood arrangement on the altar
↓
2️⃣ The other priests couldn't see or hear him — the altar itself blocked their view
↓
3️⃣ They knew he'd reached the right moment only when they heard the creak of ben Katin's pulley MECHANISM, raising the Basin that had been sunk in flowing water overnight (so its water wouldn't be disqualified) — 'The time has come!'
↓
4️⃣ He sanctified his hands and feet, took the silver coal pan, ascended the altar, cleared the outer coals aside, and scooped the fully-consumed inner coals into the pan
↓
5️⃣ He descended, turned north, walked along the ramp's east side, and heaped the coals on the floor — the same spot used for bird-offering remains and other Temple ashes
Then the brethren joined in: Once the other priests saw him descend, they rushed to sanctify their own hands and feet, grabbed shovels and forks, and ascended to clear unburned limbs and fats to the altar's sides, then heaped remaining ash onto the great central mound (תַּפּוּחַ) — sometimes holding as much as 300 kor, left untouched on Festivals as a mark of the altar's honor.
