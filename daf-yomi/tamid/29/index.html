📖 Overview: Building the Fire, and When the Sages Exaggerated

Tamid 29a–29b
אֵין עֲנִיּוּת בִּמְקוֹם עֲשִׁירוּת
The scene: The mishna continues describing the morning service: after clearing the ashes, priests built two wood arrangements on the altar — a large one for the daily offering, and a smaller one specifically to produce coals for the incense. Nearly any wood qualifies except olive and vine. This launches a broader Gemara discussion: Rava dismisses the mishna's claim of 300 kor of ashes and a golden drinking-cup for the lamb as exaggeration, prompting Rabbi Ami's famous principle that Torah, Prophets, and Sages all sometimes speak in hyperbole. Shmuel narrows this to exactly three rabbinic exaggerations — the ash heap, the Temple's golden vine, and its massive Curtain — while insisting the golden cup was entirely real, since 'there is no poverty in a place of wealth.' The daf closes with a dispute over exactly why olive and vine wood are excluded from the altar's fire.

📖 Rabbi Ami's Principle: Sacred Texts Sometimes Exaggerate

Prompted by Rava's skepticism about the ash heap and the golden cup
1️⃣ Rava: the mishna's claim of 300 kor of ashes on the altar, and giving the daily lamb water from an actual GOLD cup, are both exaggerations (גּוּזְמָא) — not literal facts
↓
2️⃣ Rabbi Ami generalizes: the TORAH itself uses exaggeration — e.g., 'cities great and fortified up to heaven' (Deut. 9:1) — no one thinks cities literally reached the sky
↓
3️⃣ The PROPHETS do too — 'the earth rent with the sound of them' at Solomon's coronation (I Kings 1:40) means the sound was very loud, not that the ground literally split
↓
4️⃣ And the SAGES themselves speak this way — as in the ash heap and golden cup examples just discussed
The lesson: Recognizing intentional hyperbole in sacred texts is itself a hermeneutical skill — not every vivid description is meant to be read with strict literalism.

🍇 Two of the Temple's Most Astonishing Treasures

The golden vine and the Curtain, in vivid (if slightly exaggerated) detail
וּשְׁלֹשׁ מֵאוֹת כֹּהֲנִים מַטְבִּילִין אוֹתָהּ
The details: A golden ornamental VINE stood at the Sanctuary's entrance, hung on posts; anyone could donate a gold leaf, grape, or cluster to add to it. Rabbi Elazar bar Rabbi Tzadok recounts that 300 priests were once needed to move it (an exaggeration for its true, still-impressive, weight). The CURTAIN separating the Sanctuary from the Holy of Holies was a handbreadth thick, woven from 72 strands of 24 threads each, in four colors (sky-blue, purple, scarlet, and linen) — 40 cubits long, 20 cubits wide, costing 820,000 gold dinars, with two new curtains made every single year. When it needed immersion, 300 priests were said to be required — again, an exaggeration, but a sign of its genuinely enormous size and weight.

🪓 Why Are Olive and Vine Wood Banned From the Altar?

Two reasons proposed, tested against consistency
Rav Pappa
Their wood has thick internal knots (קְטִרִי) — it burns poorly and produces excess smoke, unlike smooth, skewer-like branches that burn cleanly all the way through.
Rav Aḥa bar Ya'akov
It's about conserving the Land of Israel — cutting down olive trees and grapevines, praised species of Eretz Yisrael, for firewood would be destructive to the Land's settlement and economy.
Challenge to Rav Aḥa
But if it's about conservation, shouldn't PALM trees (also praised species) be excluded too? Yet the mishna treats palm wood as fit!
Resolution
The same challenge applies to FIG trees, which the mishna explicitly prefers — so both fig and palm wood in this context must refer to non-fruit-bearing varieties, which carry no such conservation concern.
