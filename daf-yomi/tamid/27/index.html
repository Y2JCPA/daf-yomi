📖 Overview: Counting Watches, Guarding Modesty

Tamid 27a–27b
עֵת לַעֲשׂוֹת לַה׳ הֵפֵרוּ תּוֹרָתֶךָ
The scene: The Gemara first explains why young priests are called 'rovim' in one mishna and 'pirḥei khehunna' in another — a distinction of eligibility for service, not age alone. It then tackles the Levites' twenty-one Temple watch posts, reconciling them against a biblical verse that appears to list twenty-four, and explains why the Temple Mount's watch could sit (inside the wall) while the Temple courtyard's watch could not (outside the wall, since sitting there is reserved for Davidic kings). A lengthy analysis follows on whether priests could sleep with their vestments beneath their heads, given the diverse-kinds (kilayim) prohibition, before the Gemara turns to whether priestly garments may be enjoyed as ordinary clothing — illustrated by Shimon HaTzaddik's dramatic meeting with Alexander the Great. The daf closes with a story about bathroom etiquette and practical fatherly advice passed from Rav and Rav Huna to their sons.

🔢 Reconciling the Levites' Twenty-One Watch Posts

A biblical verse (I Chronicles 26:17–18) seems to count twenty-four
StepReasoning
Raw count from the verseAdds up to 24 watches, not the mishna's 21
Abaye's first fix'For the Asuppim two' is read as always exactly two — removing a repeated count, bringing the total closer
Still a discrepancy: 22The Parbar watch (western side) had only ONE actual watchman; the second person mentioned merely kept him company since that post was isolated
Alternative approach (Ulla/anonymous)Simply accept 24 as accurate: 3 of the watches are the PRIESTS' three posts, and 21 are the Levites'
But doesn't the verse say all 24 are Levites?Per Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi: in 24 places Scripture calls priests 'Levites' too (e.g., Ezekiel 44:15's 'the priests the Levites') — so 3 of the 24 are really priestly posts described with Levite terminology

🪑 Why Location Determines Who May Sit

Temple Mount watch (inside the wall) vs. Temple courtyard watch (outside)
❓ Why do Levites keep watch INSIDE the Temple Mount's wall, but OUTSIDE the Temple courtyard's wall?
↓
🪑 On the Temple Mount, sitting is permitted for anyone — so posting watchmen inside lets them sit and rest when tired
↓
🚫 In the Temple courtyard, sitting is forbidden to everyone except kings of the House of David — so watchmen are stationed OUTSIDE the wall instead, where they're free to sit
↓
🚪 Related dispute: how many gates did the courtyard actually have — 5, 7, or 8? Abaye: two gates (Spark, Hearth) needed no Levite watch since priests already guarded them. Rava: it's a tannaitic dispute, evidenced by differing counts of Temple treasurers

🧵 Can Priests Sleep on Vestments Containing Wool and Linen?

The diverse-kinds (kilayim) problem, worked through step by step
Difficulty
If priests placed their vestments literally UNDER their heads while sleeping, doesn't the belt (containing wool and linen) violate the kilayim prohibition — even if the Torah permits wearing it during service?
Attempted defense
Maybe kilayim only forbids WEARING the mixture, not lying upon it? But a baraita says even ten mattresses stacked atop a kilayim garment are forbidden to sleep on — the rabbinic decree applies regardless of actual risk.
Resolution 1
The mishna's 'beneath their heads' really means NEXT TO their heads, not literally underneath — avoiding the kilayim issue entirely.
Resolution 2 (Rav Ashi)
Priestly vestments, especially the belt, are STIFF — and the diverse-kinds prohibition applies only to soft, warmth-giving garments people derive comfort from, not stiff material like felt.

👑 Shimon HaTzaddik and Alexander the Great

Resolving whether priestly garments may be worn for ordinary benefit
עֵת לַעֲשׂוֹת לַה׳ הֵפֵרוּ תּוֹרָתֶךָ
The proof text: A baraita states that priestly vestments may be worn anywhere WITHIN the Temple — even not during service — because 'priestly vestments were given for [ordinary] enjoyment,' but wearing them OUTSIDE the Temple, 'in the country,' is forbidden. Yet a well-known account has Shimon HaTzaddik greeting Alexander the Macedonian while dressed in the priestly vestments, outside the Temple, averting its destruction. Two resolutions: perhaps his garments merely RESEMBLED priestly vestments without being consecrated; or, if they truly were consecrated, the extraordinary circumstance justified overriding the normal halakha under the principle 'It is time to act for the Lord; they have nullified Your Torah' (Psalms 119:126).

🚽 Modesty, Health, and Etiquette — Practical Wisdom Passed to Sons

Framed by a memorable story about Rav Safra and Rabbi Abba
1️⃣ Story: Rav Safra was in the (doorless) bathroom; Rabbi Abba coughed to signal his presence. Rav Safra said 'enter' — but the mishna's privacy protocol suggests he shouldn't have! Rav Safra explains: he feared for Rabbi Abba's health if he had to wait
↓
2️⃣ Rav to his son Ḥiyya, and Rav Huna to his son Rabba: relieve yourself at dawn and dusk (streets are empty then) so you're never forced to hold it in during the day, which risks health — and always sit before uncovering, cover before standing
↓
3️⃣ When drinking wine: rinse the cup before AND after; with water, just pour some out before passing the cup to a student
↓
4️⃣ A tragic precedent: a student too refined to drink from an unrinsed cup died of thirst rather than drink after his teacher — hence the enduring rule to always pour some out first
↓
5️⃣ General etiquette: don't spit in your teacher's presence — except after gourds or porridge, whose saliva is dangerous (like molten metal) to hold in
